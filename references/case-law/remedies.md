# How to use this file

Remedies matter to patentassist in four places. Load this file at each one.
1. **FTO screen (Phase 2, FTO mode).** Risk framing: how big the exposure could be (lost profits vs royalty, royalty base, foreign sales, total profit for design patents), whether an injunction is realistic, and what the user's own conduct does to enhancement risk.
2. **Willfulness hygiene (every phase that touches a third-party patent).** Knowledge of a specific patent is the fact that later matters. How the report is written, stored, and forwarded matters.
3. **Marking and notice (portfolio module, post-grant, licensing).** Marking decides whether pre-suit damages exist at all.
4. **Portfolio value and claim drafting (Phase 3, portfolio).** Claims that read on the unit actually sold, practiced by one actor in the US, support damages. Claims that read only on a minor feature of a large product, or need foreign steps, are worth less.

Distinguish a supported conditional assessment from a binding damages or willfulness determination. Do not guarantee launch clearance or a specific award. Give exposure as ranked risks with the doctrine that drives each, and send damages numbers to counsel and a damages expert.

Statutes: § 283 (injunctions "in accordance with the principles of equity"), § 284 (damages "adequate to compensate," floor of a reasonable royalty, enhancement up to 3x), § 285 (fees in "exceptional cases"), § 286 (six-year lookback), § 287(a) (marking or actual notice), § 289 (design total profit, see `design-patents.md`), § 298 (no adverse inference from not getting advice of counsel), § 154(d) (provisional rights from publication).

## 1. Permanent injunctions

### eBay Inc. v. MercExchange, L.L.C., 547 U.S. 388 (2006) · CB p. 936
- **Facts:** Jury found eBay's site infringed MercExchange's business-method patent. MercExchange licensed its patents and did not practice them. District court denied an injunction, reasoning that a willingness to license and no commercial activity showed no irreparable harm. Federal Circuit reversed under its rule that injunctions issue absent exceptional circumstances.
- **Holding / rule:** The traditional four-factor equity test applies to patent cases: irreparable injury; legal remedies inadequate; balance of hardships; public interest not disserved. The decision rests in district-court discretion, reviewed for abuse of discretion. Both categorical rules were wrong: no automatic injunction for patentees, and no automatic denial for licensors or non-practicing owners.
- **Nuance:** The Court took no position on the outcome. Two concurrences pull in opposite directions: Roberts (3 Justices) says history shows injunctions usually issue because money rarely protects a right to exclude; Kennedy (4 Justices) says firms that use patents mainly for licensing fees, small components in large products, and vague business-method patents may warrant money only. In practice, injunctions are common between direct competitors and rare for non-practicing entities whose harm is a lost royalty. The fact that flips it: direct competition with lost market share, price erosion, or lost goodwill that money cannot measure.
- **Status:** Good law as of 2026-11-18. The Federal Circuit holds eBay "jettisoned" any presumption of irreparable harm (Robert Bosch LLC v. Pylon Mfg. Corp., 659 F.3d 1142 (Fed. Cir. 2011)) and requires a causal nexus between the infringement and the harm (Apple Inc. v. Samsung Elecs. Co., 735 F.3d 1352 (Fed. Cir. 2013); 809 F.3d 633 (Fed. Cir. 2015)). The RESTORE Patent Rights Act (introduced 2024, reintroduced 2025) would add a rebuttable presumption to § 283; no enactment found as of 2026-11-18 (status of 2025–26 committee action: not verified).
- **Skill rule:** FTO: never tell a user an injunction is automatic or impossible; rank injunction risk by who owns the patent (competitor that sells a covered product: higher; licensing entity: lower) and say why. Portfolio (user is patentee): if the user may want an injunction, tell them to keep evidence of direct competition, lost customers, price cuts, and goodwill harm tied to the patented feature.

### Note cases on injunctions (compact)
- Smith Int'l, Inc. v. Hughes Tool Co., 718 F.2d 1573 (Fed. Cir. 1983) · CB p. 936: pre-eBay view that a valid, infringed patent should be enforced by injunction. Abrogated in approach by eBay. Skill rule: never cite it for a presumption.
- Paice LLC v. Toyota Motor Corp., 504 F.3d 1293 (Fed. Cir. 2007) · CB p. 943: when an injunction is denied, the court may set an ongoing royalty for future infringement, and must explain how it set the rate; the panel distinguished this from a statutory compulsory license (Rader, J., concurring, disagreed). Skill rule: FTO: when an injunction is unlikely, frame the downside as past damages plus an ongoing royalty; Paice lets the court take new evidence on economic factors arising from the ongoing royalty, so do not assume the pre-verdict rate simply carries forward. (Any claim that post-verdict rates usually exceed the pre-verdict rate: status: not verified.)
- Abbott Labs. v. TorPharm, Inc., 503 F.3d 1372 (Fed. Cir. 2007) · CB p. 951: two-step contempt test for redesigns ("more than a colorable difference" → no contempt hearing). Status: replaced by TiVo Inc. v. EchoStar Corp., 646 F.3d 869 (Fed. Cir. 2011) (en banc): the two KSM/Abbott steps merge into one question, whether the redesigned product is "more than colorably different" from the adjudged product, judged by the modified elements; significant differences end the contempt inquiry (the patentee must bring a new suit); otherwise the patentee must also prove by clear and convincing evidence that the new product infringes. Skill rule: design-around advice after a judgment: change the features the infringement finding relied on, substantially, and document why; a cosmetic change risks contempt.

## 2. Preliminary injunctions

### Sanofi-Synthelabo v. Apotex, Inc., 470 F.3d 1368 (Fed. Cir. 2006) · CB p. 944
- **Facts:** Generic clopidogrel (Plavix) ANDA filer with a Paragraph IV certification launched at risk after the 30-month stay expired, during the infringement suit. Apotex conceded infringement of claim 3. Within weeks it shipped about six months of supply. District court granted a preliminary injunction (no recall) with a $400 million bond.
- **Holding / rule:** Affirmed. Four factors: likelihood of success, irreparable harm, balance of hardships, public interest. Where infringement is conceded, likelihood of success turns on validity: the movant wins this factor unless the accused infringer raises a "substantial question" of validity or enforceability that the patentee cannot show lacks substantial merit. Irreversible price erosion (formulary-tier pricing that cannot be restored), lost goodwill, layoffs, and halted research supported irreparable harm. Harm to a defendant that chose an at-risk launch was "almost entirely preventable" and weighed little. Public interest in drug-development incentives outweighed short-term price concerns. Bond amount is discretionary under Rule 65(c).
- **Nuance:** The district court also used a presumption of irreparable harm. The Federal Circuit avoided deciding whether eBay forbids it (footnote 9) because independent harm findings sufficed. A PI ruling is not a merits ruling; the trial court is not bound. The losing argument: harm had already happened, so an injunction could not undo it. The fact that flips it: a validity defense with substantial merit (a "substantial question") defeats the first factor even if it may lose at trial. (Sanofi later won on validity: Sanofi-Synthelabo v. Apotex, Inc., 550 F.3d 1075 (Fed. Cir. 2008).)
- **Status:** Good law on the framework as of 2026-11-18, but the presumption of irreparable harm is gone after eBay (Robert Bosch, 2011, for permanent injunctions; Apple v. Samsung, 678 F.3d 1314 (Fed. Cir. 2012), requiring a causal nexus for PIs). The "substantial question" standard is restated in Titan Tire Corp. v. Case New Holland, Inc., 566 F.3d 1372 (Fed. Cir. 2009). Winter v. NRDC, 555 U.S. 7 (2008), requires irreparable harm to be likely, not merely possible.
- **Skill rule:** FTO: when the user plans to launch despite a known patent or pending suit, flag the preliminary-injunction risk separately from damages, note that self-inflicted launch costs count little in the balance, and list the user's strongest validity attacks, because a substantial validity question is the main PI defense. Invalidity mode: rank attacks by whether each raises a "substantial question" at the PI stage, not only by trial strength.

### Note cases (compact)
- Contempt and redesign: see TiVo above. Foreign interim measures (Anton Piller orders, French saisie-contrefaçon) are evidence-gathering tools, not damages remedies · CB p. 952. Skill rule: rule 9 applies; refer foreign enforcement questions to local counsel.

## 3. Lost profits

### Panduit Corp. v. Stahlin Bros. Fibre Works, Inc., 575 F.2d 1152 (6th Cir. 1978) · CB p. 952
- **Facts:** Patent on wiring duct, held valid and infringed; infringer continued through an injunction and contempt. A master awarded a 2.5% royalty based on the infringer's average 4% profit. Patentee sought lost profits on diverted sales, or a 35% royalty, plus price-cut damages.
- **Holding / rule:** To get lost profits on the infringer's sales, the patentee proves (1) demand for the patented product, (2) absence of acceptable non-infringing substitutes, (3) manufacturing and marketing capacity to meet the demand, and (4) the amount of profit it would have made. Panduit failed factor 4 for lack of fixed-cost evidence, so lost profits were denied. The royalty was reversed as too low: an infringer cannot pay only what a willing licensee would have paid, or infringement becomes a free compulsory license. Other infringers selling infringing products are not "substitutes." A product without the patented advantages is not an acceptable substitute for buyers who want them. On remand, weigh no substitutes, the patentee's no-license policy, the business the patentee would lose by licensing, and that the patent drove the product's entire value.
- **Nuance:** Factor 4 failed on accounting, not on law: a patentee that cannot prove incremental profit loses lost profits but keeps the royalty floor. The opinion's punitive tone ("heads-I-win, tails-you-lose") is not the modern frame; later law treats damages as compensation and reads "acceptable substitute" economically (Grain Processing, below). Evidence used for nonobviousness (long-felt need, copying, commercial success) also shows no acceptable substitutes.
- **Status:** Good law as a useful but non-exclusive test as of 2026-11-18 (Rite-Hite, below). Satisfying Panduit can itself satisfy apportionment for lost profits (Mentor Graphics Corp. v. EVE-USA, Inc., 851 F.3d 1275 (Fed. Cir. 2017)). The "Panduit kicker" for litigation costs is barred (Mahurkar, § 4 notes).
- **Skill rule:** Portfolio (user practices its patent): tell the user lost profits depend on records they should keep now: unit sales, incremental cost and profit, capacity, and evidence of why buyers choose the patented feature. FTO: if the patent owner sells a covered product and the market has few non-infringing alternatives, rank lost-profits exposure above royalty exposure.

### Rite-Hite Corp. v. Kelley Co., 56 F.3d 1538 (Fed. Cir. 1995) (en banc) · CB p. 959
- **Facts:** Patent covered a manual truck restraint (MDL-55). Infringer's automatic restraint mainly took sales of the patentee's automatic ADL-100, which the patent in suit did not cover (other patents did). Patentee also claimed profits on dock levelers often sold with restraints.
- **Holding / rule:** (1) Lost profits on a patentee's product that directly competes with the infringing product are recoverable even if that product is not covered by the patent in suit, if "but for" causation is proved and the loss was reasonably, objectively foreseeable. The patentee's own patented substitute is not an "acceptable non-infringing substitute" because buyers could get it only from the patentee. (2) Entire market value rule and convoyed sales: unpatented items count only if they function together with the patented item as a single assembly or functional unit; items sold together only for convenience or business advantage do not. Dock levelers excluded.
- **Nuance:** Foreseeability, not mere causation, limits recovery (no damages for remote harms like stock-price drops). Dissent (Nies, 4 judges): patent damages should be limited to harm to the market for goods embodying the patent; profits on other products expand the patent grant. Partial dissent (Newman, 2 judges): the functional-unit test for convoyed sales is inconsistent with the foreseeability test used for competing products. Unpatented competing products: had the ADL-100 been unpatented and available from others, the patentee would have had to prove buyers would not have bought it elsewhere.
- **Status:** Good law as of 2026-11-18. Companion rule: a patentee need not practice its own patent to get lost profits if it sells a product that competes with the infringing one (King Instruments, below). Lost profits belong to the patentee entity that lost the sales; a related company's lost profits are not automatically recoverable (Poly-America, L.P. v. GSE Lining Tech., Inc., 383 F.3d 1303 (Fed. Cir. 2004): a sister corporation's lost sales were not the patentee's lost profits, even though the two collaborated under a common parent).
- **Skill rule:** FTO: when the user's product competes head-to-head with the patent owner's product line, flag that lost-profits exposure may cover the owner's competing products beyond the asserted patent's own embodiment. Portfolio: holding several patents on rival product lines raises damages value (no "acceptable substitute" from third parties); note this when ranking portfolio assets.

### Note cases on lost profits (compact)
- Kaufman Co. v. Lantech, Inc., 926 F.2d 1136 (Fed. Cir. 1991); Lam, Inc. v. Johns-Manville Corp., 718 F.2d 1056 (Fed. Cir. 1983) · CB p. 958: in a two-supplier market (patentee and infringer), lost sales can be inferred. Market-share apportionment for multi-supplier markets: State Indus., Inc. v. Mor-Flo Indus., Inc., 883 F.2d 1573 (Fed. Cir. 1989). Skill rule: FTO: a market with only the user and the patentee carries higher lost-profits risk.
- King Instruments Corp. v. Perego, 65 F.3d 941 (Fed. Cir. 1995) · CB p. 970: patentee that did not practice the patented feature still got lost profits on its competing machine; no "reverse infringement" proof required. See also Hebert v. Lisle Corp., 99 F.3d 1109 (Fed. Cir. 1996).
- Grain Processing Corp. v. American Maize-Products Co., 185 F.3d 1341 (Fed. Cir. 1999) · CB p. 986: the "but for" world includes non-infringing alternatives the infringer could and would have used, even if not on the market during infringement, if available (materials, know-how, cost) at the time. Defeats lost profits where a cheap design-around existed. Skill rule: FTO: documenting a ready, cheap design-around early is the main lever against lost profits; record when it became available.
- Crystal Semiconductor Corp. v. TriTech Microelectronics Int'l, Inc., 246 F.3d 1336 (Fed. Cir. 2001) · CB p. 985: price-erosion damages require evidence of how a higher price would have reduced quantity sold (elasticity); patentee cannot claim both full lost sales and a higher price without that showing. Yale Lock Mfg. Co. v. Sargent, 117 U.S. 536 (1886): price reductions forced by infringing competition are compensable.
- Aro Mfg. Co. v. Convertible Top Replacement Co., 377 U.S. 476 (1964); General Motors Corp. v. Devex Corp., 461 U.S. 648 (1983) · CB pp. 954, 962: damages are the difference between the patentee's position with and without infringement; § 284 aims at full compensation, and prejudgment interest should ordinarily be awarded (Devex).

## 4. Reasonable royalty, entire market value, and apportionment

### Georgia-Pacific Corp. v. United States Plywood Corp., 318 F. Supp. 1116 (S.D.N.Y. 1970), modified, 446 F.2d 295 (2d Cir. 1971) · CB p. 994
- **Facts:** District court compiled the evidence courts had used to set a reasonable royalty.
- **Holding / rule:** Fifteen factors frame a hypothetical arm's-length negotiation between a willing licensor and licensee at the time infringement began. Key ones: the patentee's own licenses for the patent (1), rates the licensee paid for comparable patents (2), license scope and exclusivity (3), the patentee's policy of not licensing (4), competitive relationship (5), convoyed sales (6), remaining term (7), profitability and commercial success (8), advantages over old modes (9–10), extent of use (11), customary profit share in the industry (12), the share of profit credited to the invention versus unpatented features and the infringer's own contributions (13), experts (14), and the negotiated result (15).
- **Nuance:** The list is a framework, not a formula; not every factor must be addressed. The negotiation assumes validity and infringement and is set at the date infringement began, but later facts can inform it (the "book of wisdom," Sinclair Refining Co. v. Jenkins Petroleum Process Co., 289 U.S. 689 (1933); Fromson v. Western Litho Plate & Supply Co., 853 F.2d 1568 (Fed. Cir. 1988)). Factor 13 is where apportionment lives.
- **Status:** Good law and still the standard frame as of 2026-11-18, policed by Daubert and Rule 702 (Uniloc; EcoFactor en banc, 2025).
- **Skill rule:** Portfolio valuation and licensing: collect the evidence these factors need (prior licenses with their technical and economic comparability, the feature's contribution to profit, non-infringing alternatives and their cost) and label any number as an input for a damages expert, not a value.

### Uniloc USA, Inc. v. Microsoft Corp., 632 F.3d 1292 (Fed. Cir. 2011) · CB p. 970
- **Facts:** Patent on a software registration (anti-copying) system; accused feature was Microsoft's Product Activation for Office and Windows. Expert took $10 per key from an internal document, applied the 25% rule of thumb ($2.50), multiplied by 226 million licenses ($565 million), then "checked" it against $19 billion of total product revenue (2.9%). Jury awarded $388 million.
- **Holding / rule:** (1) The 25% rule of thumb is "fundamentally flawed" and inadmissible under Daubert and FRE 702, because it does not tie the royalty to the facts of the case; starting from it and adjusting with Georgia-Pacific factors does not cure it. (2) Entire market value rule: damages may be based on the entire product's value only when the patented feature is the basis for customer demand. Using total revenue even as a "check" tainted the verdict; a low enough rate does not make an improper base proper. New trial on damages affirmed.
- **Nuance:** Georgia-Pacific factors 1, 2, and 12 remain valid if the evidence is tied to this patent, these parties, and this technology. Uniloc reads Lucent's "low rate on a full base" passage narrowly, in its context of no demand evidence. Once the jury hears the big revenue number, instructions rarely undo it (the "$19 billion cat").
- **Status:** Good law as of 2026-11-18. Extended by LaserDynamics, Inc. v. Quanta Computer, Inc., 694 F.3d 51 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (EMVR only if the patented feature alone drives demand for the whole product; being valuable or necessary is not enough); VirnetX, Inc. v. Cisco Sys., Inc., 767 F.3d 1308 (Fed. Cir. 2014) (the smallest salable patent-practicing unit is not automatically safe; further apportion if it contains unpatented features; rejected an untethered Nash 50/50 split); Power Integrations, Inc. v. Fairchild Semiconductor Int'l, Inc., 904 F.3d 965 (Fed. Cir. 2018) (other valuable features defeat EMVR); Exmark Mfg. Co. v. Briggs & Stratton Power Prods. Grp., LLC, 879 F.3d 1332 (Fed. Cir. 2018) (the base can be the whole product if the rate itself is apportioned, where the claim covers the whole product). Applied again in Provisur Techs. v. Weber (Fed. Cir. 2024) (new damages trial: EMVR used with no evidence the feature drove demand).
- **Skill rule:** Drafting claims: for a feature inside a larger product, add claims at the level of the unit actually sold (the device or system that contains the feature), not only the sub-component, so the royalty base is the claimed article; state the feature's benefit in the spec so demand can be tied to it. FTO: estimate exposure on the accused feature's value, not total product revenue, unless the feature drives demand for the whole product, and say which.

### Note cases on royalty evidence and apportionment (compact)
- Garretson v. Clark, 111 U.S. 120 (1884) · CB p. 982: the patentee must apportion between patented and unpatented features with reliable, tangible evidence, or show the entire value is attributable to the patented feature. Root of EMVR.
- Lucent Techs., Inc. v. Gateway, Inc., 580 F.3d 1301 (Fed. Cir. 2009); ResQNet.com, Inc. v. Lansa, Inc., 594 F.3d 860 (Fed. Cir. 2010); Wordtech Sys., Inc. v. Integrated Networks Solutions, Inc., 609 F.3d 1308 (Fed. Cir. 2010) · CB p. 980: licenses used to set a rate must be comparable to the hypothetical license (technology, parties, structure); unrelated licenses cannot inflate the rate; running-royalty licenses do not support a lump sum without explanation, and lump sums need a basis (expected units, how calculated).
- Mahurkar v. C.R. Bard, Inc., 79 F.3d 1572 (Fed. Cir. 1996) · CB pp. 993, 996: a royalty may draw on comparable royalties even if the patentee never licensed, but no "Panduit kicker" added to cover litigation expense; extra money comes only through § 284 enhancement or § 285 fees. Maxwell v. J. Baker, Inc., 86 F.3d 1098 (Fed. Cir. 1996) · CB p. 997: jury may award damages above the royalty if proved as actual damages.
- Nickson Indus., Inc. v. Rol Mfg. Co., 847 F.2d 795 (Fed. Cir. 1988) · CB p. 993: a reasonable royalty may exceed an established rate where widespread infringement depressed it.
- EcoFactor, Inc. v. Google LLC, 137 F.4th 1333 (Fed. Cir. 2025) (en banc) (8-2): expert testimony that converted lump-sum settlement licenses into a per-unit rate, resting on the patentee's own "whereas" recitals of a rate the licensees had not agreed to, was not based on sufficient facts or data under Rule 702(b); new damages trial. The majority stressed the judge's gatekeeping role and said this was not a case where the evidence could reasonably support competing conclusions; Judges Reyna and Stark dissented in part and warned against reading the decision as letting judges resolve fact disputes under Rule 702. (Post-casebook; see below.)
- Rex Medical, L.P. v. Intuitive Surgical, Inc., No. 2024-1072 (Fed. Cir. Oct. 2, 2025) (precedential): the expert was properly excluded because he failed to apportion a $10 million lump-sum settlement license among the licensed patents; with no other evidence from which the jury could apportion without speculation, the reduction of a $10 million verdict to $1 (treated on appeal as JMOL of no damages) was affirmed, as was the denial of a new damages trial.

## 5. Extraterritorial damages

### WesternGeco LLC v. ION Geophysical Corp., 585 U.S. 407 (2018) · CB p. 987
- **Facts:** ION made components of a patented marine-survey system in the US and shipped them abroad, where customers assembled the system and won survey contracts the patentee would otherwise have won. Jury awarded $12.5 million royalty plus $93.4 million lost profits on 10 foreign contracts. Federal Circuit reversed the lost profits as extraterritorial.
- **Holding / rule:** Reversed (7-2). Skipping step one of the extraterritoriality framework, the Court held at step two that the focus of § 284 is "the infringement," and the infringement under § 271(f)(2) is the domestic act of supplying components from the US. Lost foreign profits caused by that domestic act are a permissible domestic application of § 284.
- **Nuance:** Gorsuch (with Breyer) dissented: US patents cannot reach foreign uses, and awarding foreign lost profits treats the foreign assembly as infringing. The Court did not decide § 271(a) or whether the presumption applies to remedial provisions (step one). Multiple-recovery concerns across countries were left for later.
- **Status:** Good law as of 2026-11-18. Extended to § 271(a) by Brumfield v. IBG LLC, 97 F.4th 854 (Fed. Cir. 2024): WesternGeco displaces Power Integrations, Inc. v. Fairchild Semiconductor Int'l, Inc., 711 F.3d 1348 (Fed. Cir. 2013) as the framework; damages for foreign conduct are possible if the domestic infringing act proximately caused them, but the patentee there lost for lack of that causal link. Limited in application by Trustees of Columbia Univ. v. Gen Digital Inc., No. 2024-1243 (Fed. Cir. Mar. 11, 2026) (precedential): software copies sold to foreign customers are made abroad when installed there (Microsoft v. AT&T logic), so a royalty on foreign sales could not rest on a "made in or distributed from the United States" theory; a Brumfield master-copy causation theory not put to the jury was not reached.
- **Skill rule:** FTO: when the user makes components or master copies in the US for products assembled, installed, or sold abroad, list § 271(f) and § 271(a)-plus-causation exposure for foreign sales, country by country, and mark foreign lost profits as possible, not excluded. Drafting claims: include claims whose infringement is complete by acts in the US (component or kit claims, single-actor method claims, claims on the server side the user's competitor would run domestically).

### Note cases (compact)
- Microsoft Corp. v. AT&T Corp., 550 U.S. 437 (2007) · CB p. 987: software in the abstract is not a "component" under § 271(f); copies made abroad from a master shipped from the US are not "supplied" from the US.
- Life Technologies Corp. v. Promega Corp., 580 U.S. 140 (2017) · CB p. 987: a single staple component is not "a substantial portion" under § 271(f)(1). Digested in `infringement.md`.
- Carnegie Mellon Univ. v. Marvell Tech. Grp., Ltd., 807 F.3d 1283 (Fed. Cir. 2015): chips made and delivered abroad generate no US royalty unless a sale (or other § 271(a) act) occurred in the US. Brumfield (2024) describes CMU as applying Power Integrations-based extraterritoriality standards "now superseded by WesternGeco," while citing CMU's causation reasoning as a point of comparison.

## 6. Willfulness and enhanced damages

### Halo Electronics, Inc. v. Pulse Electronics, Inc., 579 U.S. 93 (2016) · CB p. 999
- **Facts:** Consolidated with Stryker v. Zimmer. Halo sent Pulse letters offering licenses in 2002; a Pulse engineer briefly concluded the patents were invalid and Pulse kept selling. In Stryker, the jury found Zimmer all but told its designers to copy Stryker's products; the district court trebled. In both, the Federal Circuit denied enhancement under In re Seagate Tech., LLC, 497 F.3d 1360 (Fed. Cir. 2007) (en banc) because the defendants raised reasonable defenses at trial.
- **Holding / rule:** Seagate's two-part test (objective recklessness by clear and convincing evidence, then subjective knowledge) is inconsistent with § 284. District courts have discretion to enhance, but enhancement is reserved for "egregious cases of culpable behavior" — willful, wanton, malicious, bad-faith, deliberate, consciously wrongful, flagrant, or pirate-like. Culpability is measured by the infringer's knowledge at the time of the conduct; a defense invented later by counsel does not immunize. Proof is by preponderance. Review is for abuse of discretion.
- **Nuance:** Breyer's concurrence (3 Justices): mere knowledge of the patent is not enough; § 298 bars using failure to get advice of counsel to prove willfulness; enhancement cannot compensate for litigation costs. Even with a willfulness verdict, enhancement is discretionary. The fact that flips it: deliberate copying of a known patented product with no good-faith basis to think the patent invalid or not infringed.
- **Status:** Good law as of 2026-11-18. Willfulness (a jury question) requires deliberate or intentional infringement; "egregiousness" bears on the court's enhancement decision, not the jury's willfulness finding (Eko Brands, LLC v. Adrian Rivera Maynez Enters., Inc., 946 F.3d 1367 (Fed. Cir. 2020); SRI Int'l, Inc. v. Cisco Sys., Inc., 14 F.4th 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2021), which disavowed the "wanton, malicious, and bad-faith" wording of its 2019 opinion in the same case, 930 F.3d 1295, as a willfulness standard; Bayer Healthcare LLC v. Baxalta Inc., 989 F.3d 964 (Fed. Cir. 2021) (knowledge of the patent and of infringement is necessary but not sufficient)). The Read factors still guide the amount (Read Corp. v. Portec, Inc., 970 F.2d 816 (Fed. Cir. 1992)). Provisur Techs., Inc. v. Weber, Inc., 119 F.4th 948 (Fed. Cir. 2024) (cert. denied Feb. 24, 2025): willfulness verdict reversed; a patentee cannot get around § 298 by pointing to the infringer's failure to get advice from a third party instead of counsel, and the rest of the evidence (a patent-tracking matrix showing the defendant knew of the patents) did not show it knew of its infringement or had specific intent to infringe. Trustees of Columbia Univ. v. Gen Digital Inc., No. 2024-1243 (Fed. Cir. Mar. 11, 2026) (precedential; judgment vacated on § 101, issues addressed for remand): no error in denying JMOL on willfulness, because the jury could infer from licensing talks that the defendant knew of the pending application, and could find it did not rely on its later defenses when it acted; the 2.6x enhancement cannot stand because it rested partly on a negative inference from a contempt order reversed in companion No. 2024-1244, and on remand the closeness-of-case factor must weigh the defendant's noninfringement and other defenses (repetitive arguments are generally not litigation misconduct).
- **Skill rule:** FTO and every phase: write third-party patent analysis as a confidential screen addressed to counsel, never as "we infringe" or "we copied"; tell the user that knowing about a specific patent and proceeding without a good-faith, contemporaneous basis (design-around, invalidity analysis, or noninfringement reasoning) is the fact pattern that supports enhancement, and that a later litigation defense does not cure it. Never tell the user that skipping an opinion of counsel creates willfulness (§ 298), but do say a timely, competent opinion is evidence of good faith.

### Note cases on willfulness (compact)
- Read Corp. v. Portec, Inc., 970 F.2d 816 (Fed. Cir. 1992) · CB p. 998: enhancement factors: deliberate copying; whether the infringer, knowing of the patent, investigated and formed a good-faith belief of invalidity or noninfringement; litigation conduct; size and finances; closeness of the case; duration; remedial action; motive to harm; concealment. Still used post-Halo for the amount.
- Knorr-Bremse Systeme fuer Nutzfahrzeuge GmbH v. Dana Corp., 383 F.3d 1337 (Fed. Cir. 2004) (en banc) · CB p. 999: no adverse inference from invoking privilege over an opinion or from not getting one. Now codified for failure to obtain or present advice in § 298 (AIA, which also covers inducement intent).
- In re Seagate Tech., LLC, 497 F.3d 1360 (Fed. Cir. 2007) (en banc) · CB p. 999: abolished the affirmative duty of due care and set the objective-recklessness test. Status: test abrogated by Halo. Its holding that waiver from relying on opinion counsel generally does not extend to trial counsel was not addressed by Halo.
- Beatrice Foods Co. v. New England Printing & Lithographing Co., 930 F.2d 1572 (Fed. Cir. 1991) · CB p. 997: enhancement and fees tied mainly to willfulness and litigation bad faith.
- Willfulness does not equal § 287 notice: Arctic Cat II (2020), § 8 below.

## 7. Attorney fees (§ 285)

### Octane Fitness, LLC v. ICON Health & Fitness, Inc., 572 U.S. 545 (2014) · CB p. 1008
- **Facts:** ICON (a large exercise-equipment maker that never sold the patented elliptical) sued Octane; Octane won summary judgment of noninfringement. Fees denied under Brooks Furniture Mfg., Inc. v. Dutailier Int'l, Inc., 393 F.3d 1378 (Fed. Cir. 2005): fees only for sanctionable misconduct, or a suit both objectively baseless and brought in subjective bad faith, proved by clear and convincing evidence.
- **Holding / rule:** Brooks Furniture rejected. An "exceptional" case is "simply one that stands out from others" in the substantive strength of a party's position (law and facts) or the unreasonable manner of litigation, decided case by case on the totality of the circumstances. Either exceptional weakness or bad faith can suffice; conduct need not be independently sanctionable. Proof by preponderance. Companion case Highmark Inc. v. Allcare Health Mgmt. Sys., Inc., 572 U.S. 559 (2014) · CB p. 1015: all aspects reviewed for abuse of discretion.
- **Nuance:** Losing is not enough; fees are not a penalty for failing to win. Fee shifting runs both ways (prevailing patentees too, e.g., for willful infringement or litigation misconduct). The Fogerty-style considerations (frivolousness, motivation, objective unreasonableness, need for compensation and deterrence) guide discretion.
- **Status:** Good law as of 2026-11-18. Applied to weak post-Alice assertions and inadequate pre-suit investigation in later Federal Circuit cases (specific cites: status: not verified). A fee award that relied in part on a sanction later reversed must be reconsidered without it (Columbia v. Gen Digital, 2026).
- **Skill rule:** Portfolio and assertion planning (user is patentee): before any demand letter, run eligibility, claim-construction, and noninfringement self-checks, because a weak or uninvestigated assertion risks paying the defendant's fees. FTO (user is accused): note that an exceptionally weak assertion against the user can support a fee award, which changes settlement math.

## 8. Marking and notice (§ 287)

### Amsted Industries Inc. v. Buckeye Steel Castings Co., 24 F.3d 178 (Fed. Cir. 1994) · CB p. 1017
- **Facts:** Patent claimed a railcar underframe combination including a center plate. Amsted sold only the unmarked center plate to car builders with installation drawings, so its customers made the patented combination. In 1986 Amsted sent an industry-wide letter announcing its patent ownership and warning against supplying infringing parts; in 1989 it sent the accused infringer a letter identifying its specific casting as infringing and demanding it stop.
- **Holding / rule:** (1) Customers who build the patented article under an implied license make it "for or under" the patentee, so § 287(a) applies; unmarked customer products bar pre-notice damages. Amsted could have marked its plate "for use under U.S. [patent no.]" or required purchasers to mark "licensed under," without violating § 292. (2) Actual notice requires "the affirmative communication of a specific charge of infringement by a specific accused product or device." A general letter about ownership and enforcement is not notice; the infringer's own knowledge is irrelevant; the test looks at the patentee's act. Damages run from the 1989 letter.
- **Nuance:** Following Dunlap v. Schofield, 152 U.S. 244 (1894), notice must be of the infringement, not just the patent. Filing suit is notice.
- **Status:** Good law as of 2026-11-18. The AIA (effective Sept. 16, 2011) added virtual marking: "patent" or "pat." plus a free, public web address that associates the article with the patent number. Reaffirmed in Lubby Holdings LLC v. Chung, 11 F.4th 1355 (Fed. Cir. 2021): knowing that a patent issued is not notice; no specific charge before suit meant damages only from the complaint. Burden: the accused infringer first identifies unmarked products it says practice the patent (a low bar), then the patentee must prove compliance, e.g., that those products do not practice the claims (Arctic Cat Inc. v. Bombardier Recreational Prods. Inc., 876 F.3d 1350 (Fed. Cir. 2017)). Once a patentee or licensee sells unmarked patented products, stopping those sales does not restart damages; only marking or actual notice does, and a willfulness finding is not notice (Arctic Cat Inc. v. Bombardier Recreational Prods. Inc., 950 F.3d 860 (Fed. Cir. 2020)).
- **Skill rule:** Portfolio (after grant): tell the user to mark every product that practices any claim (physical number or a virtual-marking page listing patents per product), to require licensees by contract to mark, to audit compliance, and to update the page when patents issue or expire; flag that unmarked sales by anyone "for or under" them cut off pre-notice damages. Licensing and notice letters: a letter meant to start damages must name the patent, the specific accused product, and charge infringement; a general "please respect our patents" letter starts nothing.

### Note cases on marking (compact)
- Product and method claims: American Med. Sys., Inc. v. Medical Eng'g Corp., 6 F.3d 1523 (Fed. Cir. 1993) · CB p. 1022: where a patent has apparatus and method claims and there is a tangible item to mark, the patentee must mark to get constructive notice. Hanson v. Alpine Valley Ski Area, Inc., 718 F.2d 1075 (Fed. Cir. 1983) · CB p. 1023: § 287 does not apply where only method claims are asserted. Crystal Semiconductor (2001) · CB p. 1023: damages on unmarked process claims sustained. Skill rule: drafting claims: when the client sells a product but may not mark reliably (many SKUs, licensees, OEM parts), include method claims, which preserve pre-notice damages if asserted alone; say that asserting the apparatus claims brings marking back in.
- False marking, 35 U.S.C. § 292: since the AIA, only the United States or a competitor with a "competitive injury" may sue (qui tam ended); intent to deceive still required; marking with an expired patent that once covered the product is not a violation (§ 292(c)). Sukumar v. Nautilus, Inc., 785 F.3d 1396 (Fed. Cir. 2015) · CB p. 1021: competitive injury needs at least an attempt to enter the market (intent plus action). Skill rule: portfolio: mark only products a listed patent covers; review the marking page when products change.
- Marking estoppel: Frolow v. Wilson Sporting Goods Co., 710 F.3d 1303 (Fed. Cir. 2013) · CB p. 1022: no special equitable estoppel; marking is an extrajudicial admission the marker may rebut. Skill rule: analysis: the patentee's own marking of a product is evidence (not proof) that the product practices the claims, useful in invalidity and in reading claim scope.
- "One or more of the following patents" lists: United States v. General Electric Co., 82 F. Supp. 753 (D.N.J. 1949) · CB p. 1021: such a notice was not an antitrust restraint even if only one listed patent covered the product. Old district-court authority; § 292 intent rules now govern.
- Design patents: § 287 applies to § 289 total-profit recovery (Nike, Inc. v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc., 138 F.3d 1437 (Fed. Cir. 1998)); see `design-patents.md`.
- Provisional rights, § 154(d): a reasonable royalty from publication to grant, only if the issued claims are substantially identical to the published claims and the infringer had actual notice of the published application (Rosebud LMS Inc. v. Adobe Sys. Inc., 812 F.3d 1070 (Fed. Cir. 2016): actual knowledge suffices without an affirmative act by the applicant, but constructive knowledge does not). Skill rule: prosecution: when the client may want pre-grant royalties, publish early with claims close to what will issue and send the competitor the published application; "patent pending" marking alone does not trigger provisional rights.
- Foreign marking regimes differ (some require marking, some none) · CB p. 1023; refer to local counsel (rule 9).

## 9. Time limits on damages (§ 286) and laches

- **§ 286:** no recovery for infringement more than six years before the complaint or counterclaim. It is a damages lookback, not a statute of limitations on the suit; each infringing act starts its own clock.
- **SCA Hygiene Prods. Aktiebolag v. First Quality Baby Prods., LLC, 580 U.S. 328 (2017):** laches cannot bar legal damages within the § 286 window (following Petrella for copyright). Equitable estoppel (misleading conduct, reliance, prejudice) remains available, and delay can still affect equitable relief such as injunctions. Digested in `defenses.md`.
- **Skill rule:** Portfolio and FTO: compute exposure windows from the six-year lookback, the marking or notice date (§ 287), and grant date (or publication date for § 154(d)), and show which is binding; tell an accused user that the patentee's years of silence do not bar damages within six years, while express assurances by the patentee may support estoppel.

## Edge-case playbook

1. **"They knew about the patent, so it's willful."** Trap: knowledge alone treated as willfulness. Correct: willfulness needs deliberate or intentional infringement, and enhancement needs egregious conduct judged by what the infringer knew when it acted; knowledge plus a good-faith, contemporaneous basis (design-around, invalidity analysis) cuts against enhancement. (Halo; Breyer concurrence; Eko Brands; SRI (2021); Bayer.)
2. **"We never got an opinion of counsel, so we're exposed to treble damages."** Trap: assuming absence of an opinion proves willfulness. Correct: § 298 bars using it to prove willfulness or inducement intent, and a patentee cannot substitute "failure to consult a third party" (Provisur, 2024). An opinion is still useful affirmative evidence of good faith.
3. **"Our litigators will find a strong defense, so willfulness is off the table."** Trap: Seagate thinking. Correct: Halo removed the objective-recklessness shield; a defense the infringer did not know or rely on when acting does not protect it.
4. **The patentee sold unmarked products, then sent a letter saying "we own patent X; respect our rights."** Trap: treating the letter as notice. Correct: actual notice requires a specific charge against a specific product; damages run from that charge or the complaint (Amsted; Lubby). The infringer's knowledge is irrelevant.
5. **Licensee sold unmarked products for a year, then stopped; patentee says damages restart after that.** Trap: on/off marking. Correct: once unmarked sales occur, only marking or actual notice starts damages (Arctic Cat II, 2020). And a willfulness verdict is not notice.
6. **Patent has apparatus and method claims; the patentee never marked.** Trap: assuming no pre-suit damages at all. Correct: if only method claims are asserted, § 287 does not apply (Hanson; Crystal Semiconductor); asserting apparatus claims where a markable product exists brings marking in (American Medical Systems).
7. **Patentee sells only a component; customers build the claimed combination.** Trap: "we never made the patented article, so marking doesn't apply." Correct: implied licensees make it "for or under" the patentee; mark the component "for use under" or require customers to mark (Amsted).
8. **Small feature, huge product: the expert shows the jury total product revenue "just as a sanity check."** Trap: thinking a check or a low rate cures it. Correct: EMVR requires proof the feature drives demand for the whole product; using total revenue even as a check taints the verdict (Uniloc; LaserDynamics; Provisur).
9. **Royalty built on the 25% rule, or on a settlement license that recites a per-unit rate the licensee never agreed to.** Trap: treating industry rules of thumb or self-serving recitals as data. Correct: 25% rule inadmissible (Uniloc); per-unit rates drawn from lump-sum licenses need factual support (EcoFactor en banc, 2025); unapportioned license evidence can leave only nominal damages (Rex Medical, 2025).
10. **Non-practicing patentee asks for lost profits.** Trap: assuming lost profits for any patentee. Correct: no sales, no lost profits; but a patentee that sells a competing product need not practice the patent to recover lost profits on it (King Instruments; Rite-Hite). Injunction is not categorically barred for non-practicing owners either (eBay), though harder to get.
11. **Infringer argues it would have sold a design-around nobody sold during the infringement.** Trap: "not on the market means not a substitute." Correct: an alternative available to the infringer at the time can defeat lost profits even if never sold (Grain Processing). Panduit's strict "all the advantages" view of substitutes has been softened by economic proof.
12. **Patentee's own other patented product is the only alternative.** Trap: counting it as an acceptable non-infringing substitute. Correct: it is not available from third parties, so it does not defeat lost profits (Rite-Hite).
13. **Accessories and add-ons sold with the patented device.** Trap: adding all convoyed sales to the base. Correct: only items that function together with the patented item as a functional unit count; items bought together for convenience do not (Rite-Hite dock levelers).
14. **Components made in the US, assembled and used abroad.** Trap: "foreign use is outside US patent law, so no foreign damages." Correct: § 271(f) liability supports foreign lost profits caused by the domestic supply (WesternGeco); § 271(a) acts can support foreign damages if they proximately caused them (Brumfield). But copies of software installed abroad are made abroad (Columbia v. Gen Digital, 2026).
15. **Patentee waited five years after learning of the infringement.** Trap: "laches bars damages." Correct: laches cannot bar damages within § 286's six years (SCA Hygiene); look instead for equitable estoppel facts (assurances, reliance).
16. **Patentee wants a "kicker" added to the royalty for its legal costs.** Trap: inflating the royalty. Correct: no Panduit kicker; costs come only through § 284 enhancement or § 285 fees (Mahurkar; Halo says enhancement is not compensation for litigation costs).
17. **Enjoined infringer makes a small redesign.** Trap: assuming any change avoids contempt, or that any similarity is contempt. Correct: contempt asks whether the new product is more than colorably different in the features the infringement finding relied on, and whether it still infringes, by clear and convincing evidence (TiVo en banc).
18. **Generic or competitor launches at risk while a validity fight is pending.** Trap: weighting the launcher's sunk costs. Correct: self-inflicted harm counts little; the main defense is a substantial question of validity (Sanofi; Titan Tire). Irreversible price erosion is classic irreparable harm.
19. **Defendant won summary judgment, so it expects fees.** Trap: fees follow victory. Correct: only exceptional cases (exceptionally weak position or unreasonable litigation), preponderance, abuse-of-discretion review (Octane; Highmark).
20. **Pre-grant copying of a published application.** Trap: assuming no money for the pre-grant period, or assuming automatic recovery. Correct: § 154(d) royalty requires substantially identical issued claims and actual notice of the published application; marking "patent pending" does not supply it.

## Post-casebook developments (through 2026-11-18)

- **Lubby Holdings LLC v. Chung, 11 F.4th 1355 (Fed. Cir. 2021) (No. 2019-2286, decided Sept. 1, 2021; precedential; Newman, J., dissenting in part; reporter cite not verified).** Actual notice under § 287 requires a specific charge by the patentee; the infringer's knowledge of the patent's issuance does not count; accused infringer's initial burden to identify unmarked products, then patentee's burden. Skill rule: portfolio: send a specific charge letter (or sue) to start damages when products are unmarked.
- **Arctic Cat Inc. v. Bombardier Recreational Prods. Inc., 950 F.3d 860 (Fed. Cir. 2020).** Notice obligation cannot be switched on and off by stopping unmarked sales; willfulness is not notice. Skill rule: licensing: put marking duties and audit rights in every license.
- **Brumfield v. IBG LLC, 97 F.4th 854 (Fed. Cir. 2024).** WesternGeco applies to § 271(a) and reasonable royalties; foreign conduct can be compensable only if proximately caused by domestic infringement; Power Integrations (2013) displaced as the framework. Skill rule: FTO: list foreign sales tied to US acts as possible exposure.
- **Provisur Techs., Inc. v. Weber, Inc., 119 F.4th 948 (Fed. Cir. 2024) (No. 2023-1438, decided Oct. 2, 2024), cert. denied, No. 24-723 (Feb. 24, 2025).** Willfulness reversed (no proof of deliberate infringement once § 298-barred "no third-party advice" evidence is removed; knowing of the patents was not enough); new damages trial for EMVR used without proof the feature drove demand. Skill rule: never imply a duty to consult anyone; frame good-faith evidence as helpful, not required.
- **EcoFactor, Inc. v. Google LLC, 137 F.4th 1333 (Fed. Cir. 2025) (en banc, 8-2), decided May 21, 2025.** Rule 702 gatekeeping applies with full force to damages experts; per-unit rates inferred from lump-sum licenses need sufficient facts or data; new damages trial. Skill rule: portfolio valuation: treat license recitals as weak evidence unless the licensee's own conduct or terms confirm the rate.
- **Rex Medical, L.P. v. Intuitive Surgical, Inc., No. 2024-1072 (Fed. Cir. Oct. 2, 2025) (precedential).** Unapportioned lump-sum license evidence excluded; the $1 judgment (treated as JMOL of no damages) and denial of a new damages trial affirmed. Skill rule: valuation: a patent licensed only in bundles has little standalone royalty evidence; say so.
- **Trustees of Columbia Univ. v. Gen Digital Inc., No. 2024-1243 (Fed. Cir. Mar. 11, 2026) (precedential).** Judgment on a $185 million willfulness verdict vacated because the claims were held directed to an abstract idea at Alice step one (remanded for step two). For remand: the roughly $94 million royalty on foreign sales must be eliminated if the claims survive (copies installed abroad are made abroad); denial of JMOL on willfulness upheld; the 2.6x enhancement and the fee award must be reconsidered because they relied on a negative inference from a contempt order reversed in companion No. 2024-1244. Holdings checked against the slip opinion; reporter cite not verified. Skill rule: FTO for software: map where copies are made and installed, not where the code was written.
- **RESTORE Patent Rights Act (2024; reintroduced 2025).** Would create a rebuttable presumption of a permanent injunction after a final infringement judgment. Not enacted as far as verified on 2026-11-18. Skill rule: keep applying eBay; recheck this bill before advising on injunction odds.

## Checklist for the skill

- Before any FTO or invalidity output, set the willfulness posture: confidential, addressed to counsel in practitioner mode, no "we infringe/copied" language, and a note that a contemporaneous good-faith basis matters (Halo; § 298).
- In FTO, rank exposure per patent: (a) owner type and injunction realism (eBay); (b) lost profits vs royalty (does the owner sell a competing product? few alternatives? two-supplier market?); (c) royalty base (feature vs whole product; EMVR); (d) foreign sales tied to US acts (WesternGeco, Brumfield); (e) the window (§ 286, § 287 notice date, grant or § 154(d)).
- Check the patent owner's marking: does its product (or licensee's) bear the number or a virtual-marking page? If not, and the asserted claims are apparatus claims, pre-notice damages may be unavailable; if only method claims, marking is irrelevant.
- Never quote a damages figure as a conclusion; label numbers as inputs and send them to counsel and a damages expert.
- In claim drafting, include at least one claim at the level of the unit sold, one single-actor US-practiced claim, and (where marking will be hard) method claims; describe the feature's commercial benefit in the spec.
- In portfolio work, give a marking plan (physical or virtual, licensee clauses, audits, update on issue and expiry), a notice-letter template that names patent, product, and charge, and records to keep for lost profits (sales, incremental costs, capacity, reasons buyers choose the feature).
- For a user asserting patents, run a fee-risk self-check (eligibility, construction, noninfringement, pre-suit investigation) before any letter (Octane).
- For at-risk launches, flag preliminary-injunction risk and list the substantial-question validity attacks (Sanofi; Titan Tire).
- After a judgment or injunction, any redesign must change the features the finding relied on; document the change (TiVo).
