## 1. Continuing applications, priority chains, new matter

### Transco Products Inc. v. Performance Contracting, Inc., 38 F.3d 551 (Fed. Cir. 1994) · CB p. 495
- **Facts:** The casebook excerpt is only the court's taxonomy. In the full case, the patentee's continuation did not update the best mode it had disclosed in the parent, and the district court held the patent invalid for that.
- **Holding / rule:** A continuation, divisional, or CIP must be filed while an earlier application is pending, share at least one inventor, and carry over at least part of its disclosure. A continuation and a divisional add nothing to the disclosure; a CIP adds matter. For subject matter carried over from the parent, the continuation stands in the parent's shoes: compliance with 112(a), including best mode, is judged as of the parent's filing date, so there is no duty to "update" best mode in a continuation. The labels are administrative; what counts is what each claim is supported by.
- **Nuance:** The no-update rule covers only subject matter disclosed in the parent. New matter in a CIP is judged as of the CIP date. "Continuation" vs. "divisional" vs. "CIP" names do not change the effective date of any claim; support does.
- **Status:** Taxonomy and copendency rules good law (35 U.S.C. 120, 121; MPEP 211). Best mode is still a 112(a) duty in the application, but since the AIA (sec. 15) failure to disclose it is no longer a ground to invalidate, cancel, or hold unenforceable any claim in proceedings begun on or after Sept. 16, 2011 (35 U.S.C. 282(b)(3)(A)). The litigation bite of Transco's best-mode holding is therefore mostly gone; its priority logic is not.
- **Skill rule:** Phase 3 / portfolio: for every continuing application, record per claim which application in the chain first supports it; never assume a "continuation" label gives the parent's date. Still disclose the inventor's best mode in every new filing (the duty survives even though the litigation penalty does not).

**Note material: kinds of continuing filings and the RCE (CB pp. 496–497).** A continuation can be filed even after the parent is allowed (before it issues), to pursue broader or competitor-tracking claims. A CIP has two or more effective dates, claim by claim. An RCE (35 U.S.C. 132(b)) is not a new application; it reopens prosecution in the same one. No statute caps the number of continuations; the controversy (submarines, claims drafted to read on a competitor's product) is policy, but it feeds prosecution laches (section 8 below) and, since Jan. 19, 2025, fee surcharges for continuing applications filed more than 6 and 9 years after the earliest benefit date (see `../fees.md`, 1.17(w)).

Compact priority-chain cases:
- *Lockwood v. American Airlines, Inc.*, 107 F.3d 1565 (Fed. Cir. 1997): each application in the chain must itself describe the claimed invention; a missing link breaks the chain. **Skill rule:** Phase 3: before claiming benefit through a chain, check written-description support in every intermediate application, not just the first and last.
- *Augustine Medical, Inc. v. Gaymar Indus., Inc.*, 181 F.3d 1291 (Fed. Cir. 1999): CIP claims that need the added matter get only the CIP's date; the patentee's own earlier activity can then bar them (there, displaying a prototype more than a year before the CIP was an on-sale bar under pre-AIA 102(b)). **Skill rule:** Phase 3: when filing a CIP, flag every claim that relies on new matter and run a prior-art cutoff at the CIP date for it, including the parent's own publication.
- *PowerOasis, Inc. v. T-Mobile USA, Inc.*, 522 F.3d 1299 (Fed. Cir. 2008): once a challenger shows intervening art, the patentee must come forward with evidence that the earlier application supports the claims; the Office's silence on priority during examination is not a finding. **Skill rule:** Invalidity / FTO: never accept a patent's printed priority claim at face value; test support for each asserted claim.
- *Medtronic CoreValve, LLC v. Edwards Lifesciences Corp.*, 741 F.3d 1359 (Fed. Cir. 2014): the 120 "specific reference" must identify each intermediate application and its relationship; a reference that skips a generation fails. **Skill rule:** Phase 3 ADS: list every application in the chain with its relationship (continuation of / division of / CIP of), back to the original.
- *Droplets, Inc. v. E*Trade Bank*, 887 F.3d 1309 (Fed. Cir. 2018): incorporation-by-reference language in the spec does not satisfy the 120 specific-reference requirement. **Skill rule:** Phase 3: put benefit claims in the ADS (37 CFR 1.78), never only in spec boilerplate.
- *New Railhead Mfg., L.L.C. v. Vermeer Mfg. Co.*, 298 F.3d 1290 (Fed. Cir. 2002): a nonprovisional gets the provisional's date only for claims the provisional describes (the Gosteli rule applied to 119(e)); the drill-bit patent fell to the patentee's own commercial sales more than a year before the nonprovisional (on-sale bar). **Skill rule:** Provisional drafting: describe every element and every fallback the later claims will need; a thin provisional buys no date.

## 2. Inventorship and co-ownership

### Ethicon, Inc. v. United States Surgical Corp., 135 F.3d 1456 (Fed. Cir. 1998) · CB p. 504
- **Facts:** Dr. Yoon, a surgeon, named himself sole inventor of a safety-trocar patent (55 claims) after an 18-month unpaid collaboration with Choi, an electronics technician. The accused infringer found Choi, took a retroactive license from him, and moved under 35 U.S.C. 256 to add him as co-inventor. The trial court found Choi co-invented claims 33 and 47 and that Yoon had altered and backdated documents.
- **Holding / rule:** Conception is "the touchstone of inventorship." A joint inventor must contribute to the conception of at least one claim; contribution to one claim is enough (35 U.S.C. 116). The alleged co-inventor must prove it by clear and convincing evidence, and his own testimony needs corroboration, judged under a "rule of reason" (sketches plus circumstances sufficed). Where a claim element is in means-plus-function form, whoever contributed any one of the disclosed alternative structures for that means is a joint inventor of the claim, unless the sole inventor shows the contribution was only a reduction to practice of his own broader concept. A co-inventor of one claim presumptively owns an undivided interest in the entire patent, can license it without the other owners' consent, and must join an infringement suit; Choi's license and refusal to join required dismissal.
- **Nuance:** Not inventors: someone who only follows instructions, explains well-known principles or the state of the art, or reduces another's complete idea to practice. The appeals court rejected one of Choi's two alleged structures for claim 47 (his sketch worked differently from the patented detent) but one was enough. Judge Newman dissented: after the 1984 amendment let minor contributors be named, ownership of all 55 claims should not follow from contributing to two. The fact that flips the outcome is a written assignment or a duty to assign (employment or collaboration agreement) signed before the work.
- **Status:** Good law as of 2026-11-18. Followed in *Blue Gentian, LLC v. Tristar Products, Inc.*, 70 F.4th 1351 (Fed. Cir. 2023) (affirmed adding a joint inventor whose contributed features were the very ones used to distinguish the prior art; claim construction need not come first absent a scope dispute material to inventorship). The AIA (sec. 20) removed the "without deceptive intention" requirement from 35 U.S.C. 256 and the related correction provisions. AI cannot be an inventor; for AI-assisted work see `../ai-and-inventorship.md` (Nov. 2025 guidance).
- **Skill rule:** Phase 3 intake: ask who conceived each claimed element (not who built, tested, managed, or paid), map named inventors claim by claim, and record corroborating documents. Ask whether every contributor (including outside collaborators and contractors) has assigned or is obligated to assign; if not, put an ownership warning in the banner, because one unassigned co-inventor of one claim can license the whole patent to an infringer.

Compact note cases:
- *Mueller Brass Co. v. Reading Indus.*, 352 F. Supp. 1357, 1372 (E.D. Pa. 1972), aff'd, 487 F.2d 1395 (3d Cir. 1973) (CB p. 519): a joint inventor must be able to say that without his contribution the final conception would have been "less" in some useful way; following instructions is not enough.
- *Pannu v. Iolab Corp.*, 155 F.3d 1344 (Fed. Cir. 1998): three-part test: a significant contribution to conception (or reduction to practice), not insignificant in quality measured against the full invention, and more than explaining known concepts. **Skill rule:** Phase 3: run the Pannu test for each candidate inventor and each claim; log the result in state.json.
- *Dana-Farber Cancer Inst. v. Ono Pharm. Co.*, 964 F.3d 1365 (Fed. Cir. 2020): collaborators can be joint inventors even if they did not conceive at the same moment, and publishing one's contribution before the others finish does not disqualify it. **Skill rule:** Phase 3: ask about every academic or industry collaborator who shared data or ideas, even ones who later published separately.
- *STC.UNM v. Intel Corp.*, 754 F.3d 940 (Fed. Cir. 2014): a co-owner who refuses to join cannot be forced in under Rule 19, so the suit fails. **Skill rule:** Portfolio / FTO: when a patent has multiple owners, note that one owner's refusal to sue (or its license) can neutralize the patent.
- Inventive-entity strategy (CB p. 518 note): under AIA 102(a)(2), an earlier application is prior art only if it "names another inventor"; dropping a co-inventor's contributions from later claims (37 CFR 1.48) can change the inventive entity, but AIA 102(b)(2)(C) (common ownership or obligation to assign by the effective filing date) usually solves the problem more cleanly, and double patenting then still applies. **Skill rule:** Phase 3: when related applications have overlapping but not identical inventors, check common ownership as of each effective filing date before worrying about 102(a)(2).

## 3. Inequitable conduct and unclean hands

### Therasense, Inc. v. Becton, Dickinson & Co., 649 F.3d 1276 (Fed. Cir. 2011) (en banc) · CB p. 520
- **Facts:** To overcome repeated rejections over its own earlier patent (whose spec said a protective membrane was "optionally, but preferably" used on live blood), Abbott's attorney and R&D director told the USPTO, with a declaration, that skilled artisans would read that phrase as mere patent phraseology and that a membrane was required. Years earlier, Abbott had told the EPO that the same phrase was "unequivocally clear" that the membrane was optional. Abbott did not give the EPO briefs to the USPTO. The district court held the patent unenforceable under the Rule 56 materiality standard and a "should have known" intent standard.
- **Holding / rule:** The accused infringer must prove, by clear and convincing evidence, both (1) specific intent to deceive the USPTO and (2) but-for materiality. Intent: the applicant knew of the reference, knew it was material, and made a deliberate decision to withhold it; negligence or gross negligence is not enough; intent must be "the single most reasonable inference able to be drawn from the evidence"; no sliding scale; intent cannot be inferred from materiality alone; the patentee need not explain itself until intent is shown. Materiality: the USPTO would not have allowed a claim had it known of the withheld information, judged by the Office's standards (preponderance, broadest reasonable construction). Exception: affirmative egregious misconduct, such as filing an unmistakably false affidavit, is material without a but-for showing. Rule 56's broader definition is not the test. Vacated and remanded.
- **Nuance:** Because but-for is judged under the Office's lower standards, a reference can be material even if it fails to invalidate in court; if it does invalidate in court, it is necessarily material. Mere nondisclosure, and leaving references out of a declaration, are not "egregious." Dissent (Bryson, J., joined by three judges) would have kept Rule 56 materiality; Judge O'Malley wanted flexible equitable discretion. The remedy is still the "atomic bomb": the whole patent, and possibly related patents, become unenforceable, and reissue or reexamination cannot cure it. On remand the district court again found inequitable conduct under the new test (*Therasense, Inc. v. Becton, Dickinson & Co.*, 864 F. Supp. 2d 856 (N.D. Cal. 2012)), so the facts of this case remain a live warning.
- **Status:** Good law as of 2026-11-18. Applied strictly in *Global Tubing LLC v. Tenaris Coiled Tubes LLC*, Nos. 2023-1882, -1883 (Fed. Cir. Feb. 26, 2026) (precedential): summary judgment of inequitable conduct vacated; intent must be tied to a specific individual, inferences go to the nonmovant at summary judgment, and an inventor who handed the withheld documents to prosecution counsel supports a reasonable inference against intent; the court also affirmed denial of the patentee's motion for summary judgment of no inequitable conduct, so the issue goes to trial. Affirmances still happen where the record is strong (for example *Regeneron Pharms., Inc. v. Merus N.V.*, 864 F.3d 1343 (Fed. Cir. 2017), intent inferred as a sanction for litigation misconduct; *Belcher Pharms., LLC v. Hospira, Inc.*, 11 F.4th 1345 (Fed. Cir. 2021), contradictory statements to the FDA and the USPTO about the same pH range). The USPTO's own Rule 56 (37 CFR 1.56) still defines the duty of disclosure in examination; Therasense defines only the litigation defense.
- **Skill rule:** Prosecution / IDS: disclose to the USPTO every statement made to a foreign office or in litigation that is inconsistent with a US argument or declaration, along with the art itself; before any declaration or argument characterizing a reference goes out, search the client's foreign counterparts and related cases for contrary statements. Never draft or file a declaration whose factual statements the declarant has not verified; that is per se material.

Compact note cases (CB pp. 525–543):
- *Keystone Driller Co. v. General Excavator Co.*, 290 U.S. 240 (1933); *Hazel-Atlas Glass Co. v. Hartford-Empire Co.*, 322 U.S. 238 (1944); *Precision Instrument Mfg. Co. v. Automotive Maint. Mach. Co.*, 324 U.S. 806 (1945): unclean hands for bribed or fabricated evidence and concealed perjury; the remedy was dismissal of the suit, not unenforceability of the patent. Still available for egregious misconduct (Therasense says so).
- *Corona Cord Tire Co. v. Dovan Chem. Corp.*, 276 U.S. 358 (1928): false affidavits that were not needed for the grant did not destroy the presumption of validity; the root of but-for materiality.
- *Kingsdown Med. Consultants, Ltd. v. Hollister Inc.*, 863 F.2d 867 (Fed. Cir. 1988) (en banc in part): gross negligence does not prove intent; inequitable conduct as to one claim taints the whole patent.
- *Star Scientific, Inc. v. R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Co.*, 537 F.3d 1357 (Fed. Cir. 2008): single-most-reasonable-inference rule; no duty to explain until intent is shown.
- *Exergen Corp. v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc.*, 575 F.3d 1312 (Fed. Cir. 2009) (cited in the dissent): the defense must be pleaded with Rule 9(b) particularity (who, what, when, where, how). **Skill rule:** Analysis: when summarizing a file wrapper for litigation, list candidate inequitable-conduct facts in that who/what/when/where/how form, labeled as allegations only.
- *Rohm & Haas Co. v. Crystal Chem. Co.*, 722 F.2d 1556 (Fed. Cir. 1983) (CB p. 542): to purge a misrepresentation during prosecution, expressly tell the examiner what was wrong and where, give the true facts, and re-establish patentability on the corrected record; quietly submitting accurate facts is not enough. **Skill rule:** Office-action tool: if a prior argument or declaration is found to be wrong, draft an express correction identifying the error, never a silent substitution.
- 1988 USPTO policy (CB p. 542): the Office stopped investigating inequitable conduct in examination; the issue is litigated in court (and in OED discipline).
- Overseas (CB p. 543): most foreign systems have no inequitable-conduct defense; nullity actions and fee-shifting do the work. Never import the doctrine into foreign advice (SKILL.md US-utility scope).

Unclean hands after Therasense:
- *Gilead Sciences, Inc. v. Merck & Co.*, 888 F.3d 1231 (Fed. Cir. 2018): unclean hands barred enforcement where the patentee's agent misused a rival's confidential information during prosecution and gave false testimony; misconduct need not be before the USPTO or meet Therasense's materiality test.
- *Luv N' Care, Ltd. v. Laurain*, 98 F.4th 1081 (Fed. Cir. 2024) (verified): affirmed an unclean-hands bar based on litigation misconduct (withholding related applications relevant to claim construction, blocking discovery of the inventor's searches, evasive testimony); the misconduct must have an "immediate and necessary relation" to the relief sought; the court also vacated a no-inequitable-conduct finding for fuller analysis. **Skill rule:** Portfolio / litigation support: treat discovery conduct about prosecution files and inventor searches as enforcement risk, not just a litigation tactic.

AIA supplemental examination (CB pp. 559–560): 35 U.S.C. 257 lets the patentee have the Office consider, reconsider, or correct information; a patent then cannot be held unenforceable on the basis of that information, but the shield does not reach allegations already pleaded with particularity (or in a Paragraph IV notice) before the request, and the process must finish before suit to help. Material fraud can be referred to the Attorney General. **Skill rule:** Portfolio: when a client finds an undisclosed material reference or a wrong statement in an issued patent it plans to assert, raise supplemental examination before any enforcement letter goes out.

## 4. Double patenting

### In re Vogel, 422 F.2d 438 (C.C.P.A. 1970) · CB p. 544
- **Facts:** The applicants' earlier patent claimed a hot-processing method for pork. The application claimed the same kind of method for "meat" (claims 7, 10) and for beef (claim 11), adding a packaging-film permeability range taught by a reference (Ellies). No terminal disclaimer.
- **Holding / rule:** Two questions. (1) Same invention? "Same" means identical scope; the objective test is whether one claim could be literally infringed without infringing the other ("36 inches" and "3 feet" are the same; "halogen" and "chlorine" are not). Same-invention double patenting rests on 35 U.S.C. 101 and no terminal disclaimer can cure it. (2) If not the same, is any application claim merely an obvious variation of an invention claimed in the patent? The patent's disclosure is not prior art for this test, but it may be read to construe claim terms and to see the embodiments that the patent claims cover. The "meat" claims read on pork and added only Ellies' film, so they were obvious variants needing a terminal disclaimer. The beef claim was not: nothing showed pork's time-and-temperature data applied to beef.
- **Nuance:** Only the part of the patent's spec that supports the patent's claims may be consulted; the broad "meat sausage" background could not be used. A genus claim that covers an earlier-claimed species is the classic obviousness-type case. Note material: the default is a one-way test (is the application claim obvious over the patent claim?); a two-way test applies only when the Office alone caused the later-filed improvement to issue first (*In re Braat*, 937 F.2d 589 (Fed. Cir. 1991)), not when the applicant chose the filing and prosecution order (*In re Goodman*, 11 F.3d 1046 (Fed. Cir. 1993)). A terminal disclaimer must include the common-ownership enforceability term (37 CFR 1.321(c)(3); *In re Van Ornum*, 686 F.2d 937 (C.C.P.A. 1982)), so the patents cannot be split among owners. Keeping a continuation pending to file later claims (the "Vogel trailer") is legitimate (*Bott v. Four Star Corp.*, 675 F. Supp. 1069 (E.D. Mich. 1987), aff'd mem., 856 F.2d 202 (Fed. Cir. 1988)); see section 8 for the laches limit.
- **Status:** Good law as of 2026-11-18. The modern test is framed in two steps: construe the claims and find the differences, then ask whether the differences make the claims patentably distinct (*Eli Lilly & Co. v. Barr Labs., Inc.*, 251 F.3d 955 (Fed. Cir. 2001); MPEP 804). One-way test reaffirmed in *In re Fallaux*, 564 F.3d 1313 (Fed. Cir. 2009).
- **Skill rule:** Office-action tool: on a double-patenting rejection, first classify it (statutory "same invention" vs. nonstatutory); for statutory, the only fixes are amending scope or canceling, never a terminal disclaimer. For nonstatutory, compare claims to claims (the reference patent's spec is not prior art) and argue distinctness before offering a terminal disclaimer, because a disclaimer gives up term and ties ownership.

### Gilead Sciences, Inc. v. Natco Pharma Ltd., 753 F.3d 1208 (Fed. Cir. 2014) · CB p. 552
- **Facts:** Gilead's two antiviral patents had the same inventors and nearly identical specs but separate priority chains. The '375 patent had the earlier priority date (expires Feb. 2015) but issued later; the '483 patent was filed later, issued first, and expires Dec. 2016. Gilead filed a terminal disclaimer only in the '375 case, tying it to the '483 patent's (later) expiration, which did nothing. Natco challenged the '483 patent for obviousness-type double patenting over the '375 patent.
- **Holding / rule:** After the URAA, expiration dates, not issue dates, control: a later-issued but earlier-expiring patent can be an obviousness-type double patenting reference against an earlier-issued, later-expiring patent. A terminal disclaimer aligning the later expiration with the earlier one would have cured it.
- **Nuance:** The holding assumed the claims were obvious variants. Chief Judge Rader dissented: Gilead gave up about ten months of priority by starting a new chain, so its later term was paid for, and the common-ownership clause already prevented split-owner harassment. The rule is aimed at gamesmanship in ordering issuance; a later case limits it where the reference's earlier expiry came from the URAA transition (*Novartis Pharms. Corp. v. Breckenridge Pharm. Inc.*, 909 F.3d 1355 (Fed. Cir. 2018), pre-URAA patent).
- **Status:** Good law as of 2026-11-18, extended and limited as follows. *AbbVie Inc. v. Kennedy Inst. of Rheumatology*, 764 F.3d 1366 (Fed. Cir. 2014) (ODP applies between post-URAA patents in different families). *Novartis AG v. Ezra Ventures LLC*, 909 F.3d 1367 (Fed. Cir. 2018) (term from a 35 U.S.C. 156 extension is not cut off by ODP). *In re Cellect, LLC*, 81 F.4th 1216 (Fed. Cir. 2023), cert. denied Oct. 7, 2024 (No. 23-1231) (ODP is judged on the expiration date after patent term adjustment; PTA-extended claims without a terminal disclaimer fell, and good-faith prosecution was no answer). *Allergan USA, Inc. v. MSN Labs. Private Ltd.*, 111 F.4th 1358 (Fed. Cir. 2024) (a first-filed, first-issued, later-expiring claim cannot be invalidated by a later-filed, later-issued, earlier-expiring reference claim sharing its priority date; the first patent in the family "sets the maximum period of exclusivity"). Applied in *Acadia Pharms. Inc. v. Aurobindo Pharma Ltd.*, No. 2024-1401 (Fed. Cir. June 9, 2025) (nonprecedential). The USPTO Appeals Review Panel read Allergan narrowly (it applies only where the claims and the reference are in the same family with the same patent term filing date and the claims are first-filed, first-issued, and later-expiring; a pending application is not "first-issued," so this will rarely arise in original examination) and held that, under binding Federal Circuit precedent, the risk of harassment by separate owners supports ODP even absent term extension, while inviting the court to clarify (*Ex parte Baurin*, Appeal 2024-002920 (PTAB ARP Aug. 6, 2026) (precedential)); *In re Ablynx N.V.*, No. 2026-1333 (Fed. Cir.), on the harassment rationale, was pending as of Oct. 2026.
- **Skill rule:** Portfolio / Phase 3: for every family, compute each patent's expiration including PTA, and flag any patent that would outlive a commonly owned, patentably indistinct patent; recommend a terminal disclaimer (before the reference expires) or claiming common priority. Never start a separate priority chain for obvious variants to gain term without explaining the Gilead risk.

Compact double-patenting cases:
- *In re Hubbell*, 709 F.3d 1140 (Fed. Cir. 2013) (cited CB p. 492): ODP can apply with common inventors even without common ownership, and then a terminal disclaimer cannot satisfy the common-ownership clause. **Skill rule:** Office-action tool: if the reference patent is owned by someone else but shares an inventor, the realistic paths are arguing patentable distinctness, obtaining common ownership, or a joint research agreement disclaimer under 37 CFR 1.321(d); say so.
- *Boehringer Ingelheim Int'l GmbH v. Barr Labs., Inc.*, 592 F.3d 1340 (Fed. Cir. 2010): a terminal disclaimer filed after the reference patent expired is too late to cure ODP; also, the 35 U.S.C. 121 safe harbor requires "consonance" (the later claims must stay within the restricted group). **Skill rule:** Portfolio: docket the expiration of every ODP reference patent as a hard deadline for filing a terminal disclaimer.
- *Amgen Inc. v. F. Hoffmann-La Roche Ltd.*, 580 F.3d 1340 (Fed. Cir. 2009); *Pfizer, Inc. v. Teva Pharms. USA, Inc.*, 518 F.3d 1353 (Fed. Cir. 2008); *G.D. Searle LLC v. Lupin Pharms., Inc.*, 790 F.3d 1349 (Fed. Cir. 2015); *In re Janssen Biotech, Inc.*, 880 F.3d 1315 (Fed. Cir. 2018): the 121 safe harbor protects only divisionals filed because of a restriction requirement; continuations and CIPs get no protection, and relabeling after the fact by reissue (Searle) or reexamination (Janssen) does not help. **Skill rule:** Phase 3 / office-action tool: after a restriction, file the non-elected inventions as divisionals (designated as such, no new matter) to keep the safe harbor.
- *Sun Pharm. Indus., Ltd. v. Eli Lilly & Co.*, 611 F.3d 1381 (Fed. Cir. 2010): a later claim to a use disclosed in the earlier patent's spec for the earlier-claimed compound is not patentably distinct.
- Terminal-disclaimer rule proposal (verified): the USPTO's May 10, 2024 NPRM (89 FR 40439) to make tied patents unenforceable if any one fell was withdrawn Dec. 4, 2024 (89 FR 96152). Current TD practice is unchanged (37 CFR 1.321).
- Provisional rejections (CB p. 564): two pending applications get a provisional double-patenting rejection that becomes actual when one issues (MPEP 804).

## 5. Priority: Paris Convention and 35 U.S.C. 119

### In re Gosteli, 872 F.2d 1008 (Fed. Cir. 1989) · CB p. 576
- **Facts:** The US application claimed a Markush genus and two 21-species subgenera of antibiotic intermediates. The Luxembourg priority application disclosed only a narrower subgenus, including the two species that a US patent (Menard) disclosed with an effective date between the Luxembourg and US filing dates.
- **Holding / rule:** "Invention" in 119 means what is claimed. A US claim gets the foreign priority date only if the foreign application supports that claim as 112(a) requires, the same test used for 120 benefit. The Luxembourg application did not describe the claimed genus, so the genus claims took the US date and were anticipated by Menard's species.
- **Nuance:** The government conceded that claims limited to the species disclosed in the priority document would have gotten the earlier date, which would have defeated Menard. The fix is claim drafting: include claims that track the priority disclosure exactly (and, after issue, reissue can add them). The same logic applies to a range claimed later when the priority document gave a single value.
- **Status:** Good law as of 2026-11-18; priority is decided claim by claim (MPEP 211.05, 213/214 practice). The pre-AIA 102(e) frame is now AIA 102(a)(2) and 102(d). Under AIA 102(d), a US patent or published application is prior art as of its foreign priority date if that application describes the subject matter, abolishing for AIA cases the *In re Hilmer*, 359 F.2d 859 (C.C.P.A. 1966) rule that foreign priority was a shield but not a sword (Hilmer still governs pre-AIA references). AIA sec. 3(g)(6) also struck the old 119(a) clause that measured the one-year bar from the actual US filing date, so for AIA applications the 102(b)(1) grace period runs back from the effective filing date, which includes a supported foreign priority date (verified, 35 U.S.C. 119(a) current text).
- **Skill rule:** Phase 3: when a nonprovisional or Paris filing adds anything to its priority document, write at least one claim per independent claim that is fully supported by the priority document alone, and label which claims rely on added matter. Invalidity: test priority entitlement claim by claim before choosing the prior-art cutoff.

Paris Convention notes (CB pp. 571–575), compact:
- 12-month priority period for utility (6 for designs), restorable in the US for 2 more months if the delay was unintentional (35 U.S.C. 119(a); 37 CFR 1.55(c); see `../deadlines.md`). Foreign countries' restoration rules differ; give local-counsel advice (SKILL.md US-utility scope).
- Article 4(C)(4): a later application in the same country can restart the year only if the earlier one was withdrawn, abandoned, or refused before publication, with no rights outstanding and no priority claimed from it.
- Article 4(H): priority cannot be refused because an element was not claimed in the first application, if the application as a whole specifically discloses it.
- A US provisional can be the first Paris application; a US provisional can also claim foreign priority (35 U.S.C. 111(b)(7) cross-reference in the casebook question; verify current text before relying).
- Priority is examined only when it matters (an intervening reference or a contest), MPEP 211/213. **Skill rule:** Invalidity / FTO: an examiner's silence on priority means nothing; test it yourself (PowerOasis, above).

## 6. Patent term, PTA, and provisional rights

Term basics (CB pp. 501–503). Applications filed on or after June 8, 1995: 20 years from the earliest US nonprovisional filing date claimed under 120, 121, 365(c) (or 386(c)), never from a provisional or foreign priority date (35 U.S.C. 154(a)(2)–(3)). Pre-June 8, 1995 filings got the longer of 17 years from grant or 20 years from filing (*Merck & Co. v. Kessler*, 80 F.3d 1543 (Fed. Cir. 1996), which also holds the USPTO lacks substantive rulemaking power). Maintenance fees at 3.5, 7.5, and 11.5 years (see `../fees.md`).

- **Casebook caution:** the casebook's description of 154(b) adjustments as "up to five years" for interference, secrecy order, or successful appeal reflects the pre-2000 regime. For applications filed on or after May 29, 2000, PTA under the 1999 Patent Term Guarantee Act has A delay (Office missed 14-month, 4-month, and similar deadlines), B delay (pendency over 3 years, excluding RCE time and other carve-outs), and C delay (interference/derivation, secrecy order, successful appeal), less overlap and applicant delay, with no overall cap. PTA cannot extend a patent past a terminal-disclaimer date (154(b)(2)(B)). Treat the casebook's "five years" as outdated.
- *Wyeth v. Kappos*, 591 F.3d 1364 (Fed. Cir. 2010): A and B delays overlap only on the same calendar days.
- *Novartis AG v. Lee*, 740 F.3d 593 (Fed. Cir. 2014) (cited CB p. 503): time consumed by an RCE does not count as B delay, but time from allowance to issuance after the RCE does.
- *Supernus Pharms., Inc. v. Iancu*, 913 F.3d 1351 (Fed. Cir. 2019): the Office may reduce PTA for applicant delay only for the period in which the applicant failed to make reasonable efforts, not for time when nothing could have been done.
- Interplay with ODP: PTA counts in the ODP comparison (*Cellect*, section 4), while a 156 extension does not (*Ezra*). **Skill rule:** Portfolio: when reporting expiration, show base term, PTA, any terminal disclaimer, any 156 extension (see `pharma-and-sep.md`), and the ODP exposure created by PTA, separately.
- Provisional rights (CB pp. 500–501): 35 U.S.C. 154(d) gives a reasonable royalty from publication to grant only if the infringer had actual notice of the published application and the issued claims are substantially identical to the published ones. *Rosebud LMS Inc. v. Adobe Sys. Inc.*, 812 F.3d 1070 (Fed. Cir. 2016): actual notice of the published application is required; knowing of a related patent is not enough. **Skill rule:** Prosecution: when a client wants pre-grant damages, send the published application (with claims expected to issue unchanged) to the target, and avoid amending those claims in substance.
- Non-publication request (CB p. 499): available only if the applicant certifies no foreign filing that publishes at 18 months (35 U.S.C. 122(b)(2)(B)); a later foreign filing must be reported within 45 days or the application is regarded as abandoned (see `../deadlines.md`).

## 7. Correcting or re-examining an issued patent

Certificates of correction (CB p. 564): 35 U.S.C. 254 (Office mistakes, free) and 255 (applicant's clerical or typographical mistakes made in good faith, for a fee), only for errors that do not change scope in a way requiring reexamination.

Reissue (CB pp. 564–567). 35 U.S.C. 251–252: a patent "wholly or partly inoperative or invalid" through error, by a defective spec or drawing or claiming more or less than the patentee had the right to claim, can be reissued for the rest of the original term; no new matter; a broadening reissue must be applied for within two years of grant (251(d)); the original patent is surrendered; a reissue is examined from scratch and third parties can protest.
- *Hester Indus., Inc. v. Stein, Inc.*, 142 F.3d 1472 (Fed. Cir. 1998) (cited CB p. 566): recapture rule: reissue cannot regain scope surrendered to get the original claims allowed, and surrender can come from argument as well as amendment. *In re McDonald*, 43 F.4th 1340 (Fed. Cir. 2022) applied the rule to reissue claims that broadened a surrendered limitation.
- *Tillotson, Ltd. v. Walbro Corp.*, 831 F.2d 1033 (Fed. Cir. 1987): a reissue claim is broadening if it covers anything the original claims did not, even if it is narrower in other respects. **Skill rule:** Portfolio: treat any reissue claim that drops or generalizes any limitation as broadening, so it must be filed within two years of grant, whatever else is added.
- *In re Tanaka*, 640 F.3d 1246 (Fed. Cir. 2011): adding narrower dependent claims as a hedge, while keeping the original claims, is a correctable error under 251.
- *Antares Pharma, Inc. v. Medac Pharma Inc.*, 771 F.3d 1354 (Fed. Cir. 2014): reissue claims must be directed to the invention the original patent disclosed as the invention, not merely something mentioned in it.
- Intervening rights (252): absolute rights for specific things made or used before the reissue; equitable rights for substantial preparations; neither applies to a reissue claim that is "substantially identical" to an original claim. The same applies to amended or new claims after reexamination (307(b)) and IPR (318(c)). *Marine Polymer Techs., Inc. v. HemCon, Inc.*, 672 F.3d 1350 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (en banc): no intervening rights for claims that come out of reexamination unamended, even if the patentee argued a narrow construction. **Skill rule:** Office-action tool (reissue/reexam): prefer carrying original claims forward unchanged and adding new claims, and warn that any amended claim opens intervening rights for pre-amendment activity.
- Inequitable conduct cannot be cured by reissue (Therasense, citing *Aventis*), and inventorship and foreign-priority claims can be corrected by reissue (casebook exercise themes; verify specifics in MPEP 1402 before advising).

Ex parte reexamination (CB pp. 567–569). 35 U.S.C. 301–307: anyone (including the patentee or an anonymous requester) can request it on patents and printed publications only, if they raise a "substantial new question of patentability"; no broadening; no new matter; no continuation; cannot be withdrawn once ordered; claims that survive regain the presumption of validity; intervening rights attach to amended claims. Inter partes reexamination was replaced by IPR (see `ptab.md`).
- *In re Swanson*, 540 F.3d 1368 (Fed. Cir. 2008): a court's rejection of an invalidity attack on the same art does not bar reexamination, because the standards differ.
- *Fresenius USA, Inc. v. Baxter Int'l, Inc.*, 721 F.3d 1330 (Fed. Cir. 2013): cancellation in reexamination before a court judgment is final wipes out a pending damages award. **Skill rule:** Portfolio / FTO: a pending reexam or IPR on an asserted patent can erase a non-final judgment; report it as a major risk factor.
- *In re Vivint, Inc.*, 14 F.4th 1342 (Fed. Cir. 2021): the Office acted arbitrarily by ordering reexamination on substantially the same art and arguments it had refused to hear in serial IPR petitions.

Supplemental examination: see section 3.

## 8. Prosecution laches

Note material (CB p. 540): *Symbol Techs., Inc. v. Lemelson Med., Educ. & Research Found.*, 277 F.3d 1361 (Fed. Cir. 2002) recognized the defense, and the later appeal (422 F.3d 1378 (Fed. Cir. 2005)) held Lemelson's decades-long continuation chains unenforceable for unreasonable, unexplained delay.
- *Hyatt v. Hirshfeld*, 998 F.3d 1347 (Fed. Cir. 2021): the USPTO can raise prosecution laches in a 35 U.S.C. 145 suit, even if it never issued laches rejections or warnings during examination; the applicant's strategy of delay over hundreds of pre-GATT applications could support it. On remand, judgment for the USPTO on laches was affirmed (*Hyatt v. Stewart*, No. 2018-2390 (Fed. Cir. Aug. 29, 2025) (precedential)).
- *Personalized Media Commc'ns, LLC v. Apple Inc.*, 57 F.4th 1346 (Fed. Cir. 2023) (verified, precedential, Stark, J., dissenting): affirmed unenforceability for a pre-GATT patent where the applicant's delay was unreasonable and inexcusable under the totality of circumstances and caused prejudice.
- *Google LLC v. Sonos, Inc.*, No. 2024-1097 (Fed. Cir. Aug. 28, 2025) (nonprecedential, verified): reversed a finding that post-GATT continuation claims first presented in 2019, 13 years after the 2006 provisional, were unenforceable; laches needs proof of prejudice (intervening investment in the claimed technology during the delay), and the specification had become public in 2013, when the 2007 nonprovisional (filed with a nonpublication request) issued as a patent, before the accused infringer's asserted investment. The court did not hold that long delay can never support laches.
- **Status:** The defense survives but is rare, especially for post-GATT (20-years-from-filing) continuations whose specs were published. **Skill rule:** Phase 3 / portfolio: continuation strategy is legitimate, but log a business reason for each continuation and file claims to known competitor products promptly once the product is known; flag laches risk only for long unexplained gaps combined with intervening third-party investment.

## Edge-case playbook

1. **"It's a continuation, so it gets the 2019 date."** Trap: label equals date. Correct: copendency is required (filed before the parent issued or was abandoned), the chain must name every intermediate application, and each claim gets the earliest date whose application (and every link) describes it. A CIP claim needing new matter gets the CIP date, and the parent's own publication may be prior art against it. Transco; Lockwood; Medtronic CoreValve; Augustine Medical.
2. **Continuation filed the day after the parent issued.** Trap: "same family." Correct: no copendency, no 120 benefit; the parent patent (and its publication) can be prior art. File on or before the issue date. 35 U.S.C. 120.
3. **Provisional describes one embodiment; the nonprovisional claims a genus.** Trap: provisional date for everything. Correct: the genus gets the nonprovisional date; any disclosure or sale in between can kill it. New Railhead; Gosteli.
4. **Inventor published 11 months before a foreign priority filing; US filing 12 months later.** Trap: "the US grace period has run, measured from the US filing." Correct: for AIA applications, 102(b)(1) runs back from the effective filing date, which includes a supported foreign priority date (the old 119(a) clause was struck). But most foreign offices have no grace period, so foreign rights may already be lost. 35 U.S.C. 100(i), 102(b)(1), 119(a).
5. **A technician built and tested the prototype; a manager set the goal; an engineer conceived the claimed structure.** Trap: name everyone on the invention-disclosure form. Correct: only those who conceived at least one claimed element are inventors; but a technician who devised one alternative structure for a means-plus-function element is a joint inventor of that claim. Ethicon; Pannu.
6. **An outside collaborator contributed to one dependent claim and never signed an assignment.** Trap: "minor contributor, minor rights." Correct: he co-owns the whole patent, can license the defendant (even retroactively), and his refusal to join defeats the suit. Ethicon; STC.UNM.
7. **The client told the EPO a reference teaches X and now wants to argue to the USPTO that it teaches not-X.** Trap: different offices, different files. Correct: those foreign statements are exactly the Therasense facts; disclose them and reconcile, or do not make the argument. Therasense.
8. **A withheld reference would not invalidate the claim at trial.** Trap: "then it's immaterial." Correct: but-for materiality is judged under the Office's preponderance standard and broadest reasonable construction, so it can still be material. Intent remains a separate, demanding element. Therasense.
9. **A declaration overstates test results, but the claims would have issued anyway.** Trap: "no but-for, no problem." Correct: an unmistakably false affidavit is affirmative egregious misconduct and is material per se. Therasense; Rohm & Haas.
10. **No misconduct before the USPTO, but the patentee hid prosecution documents in discovery.** Trap: "inequitable conduct needs PTO misconduct, so we're safe." Correct: unclean hands can still bar enforcement. Luv N' Care; Gilead v. Merck.
11. **Two claims differ only in wording ("3 feet" vs. "36 inches").** Trap: file a terminal disclaimer. Correct: same-invention (statutory) double patenting cannot be cured by a disclaimer; cancel or change scope. Vogel.
12. **Separate family, later priority, issued first.** Trap: "the first-issued patent can't be the one that falls." Correct: post-URAA, an earlier-expiring commonly owned patent can be an ODP reference against a later-expiring one, regardless of issue order. Gilead.
13. **A continuation got 400 days of PTA; the parent got none.** Trap: "PTA is statutory term, ODP can't touch it." Correct: ODP is judged after PTA; without a terminal disclaimer the PTA-extended claims can fall over the parent (Cellect), unless the challenged patent is the first-filed, first-issued patent in the family with the same priority date (Allergan). The USPTO reads Allergan narrowly (*Ex parte Baurin*, ARP Aug. 6, 2026); check the latest before advising. A 156 extension is different (Ezra).
14. **Terminal disclaimer offered after the reference patent has expired.** Trap: "file it now." Correct: too late; the patent is invalid for ODP. Boehringer.
15. **Non-elected claims refiled as a continuation after a restriction.** Trap: "121 protects anything flowing from a restriction." Correct: the safe harbor covers only divisionals that stay consonant with the restriction. Amgen; Pfizer; Searle; Janssen.
16. **ODP rejection over a patent owned by a former employer that shares an inventor.** Trap: file a terminal disclaimer. Correct: a disclaimer requires common ownership (or a joint research agreement), which is not available; argue patentable distinctness. In re Hubbell; 37 CFR 1.321(c)–(d).
17. **Reissue filed 20 months after grant that narrows one element and generalizes another.** Trap: "net narrower, so no deadline issue." Correct: broader in any respect is broadening; it must be filed within 2 years (it was), and the generalized element must not recapture surrendered scope. Tillotson; Hester.
18. **Reissue claims copied unchanged from the original, plus new dependent claims.** Trap: "the defendant has intervening rights for everything." Correct: no intervening rights on claims substantially identical to original claims; the new claims can carry them. 35 U.S.C. 252; Marine Polymer.
19. **Competitor wants reexamination based on the patentee's own pre-filing sales and a never-published abandoned application.** Trap: "any prior art works." Correct: ex parte reexamination is limited to patents and printed publications; sales are out, and an unpublished abandoned application is neither a printed publication nor 102(a)(2) art. 35 U.S.C. 301–302.
20. **Pre-grant damages for a competitor that knew of the issued parent patent.** Trap: "they had notice." Correct: 154(d) needs actual notice of the published application and substantially identical claims. Rosebud.
21. **"Keep a continuation pending to extend the term."** Trap: continuations add years. Correct: the term runs 20 years from the earliest nonprovisional benefit date; continuations add flexibility, not term, and long unexplained delays carry a (narrow) laches risk. 35 U.S.C. 154(a)(2); Google v. Sonos; Hyatt.
22. **The casebook says PTA is capped at five years.** Trap: repeat it. Correct: that was the pre-May 29, 2000 regime; current PTA has no overall cap but is reduced for overlap, applicant delay, and terminal disclaimers. 35 U.S.C. 154(b).

## Post-casebook developments (through Nov. 2026)

- *In re Cellect, LLC*, 81 F.4th 1216 (Fed. Cir. 2023); cert. denied Oct. 7, 2024 (No. 23-1231). ODP measured on post-PTA expiration. **Skill rule:** Portfolio: flag every PTA-bearing patent with commonly owned, patentably indistinct relatives and no terminal disclaimer.
- *Allergan USA, Inc. v. MSN Labs. Private Ltd.*, 111 F.4th 1358 (Fed. Cir. 2024). First-filed, first-issued, later-expiring claims are not invalid over later-filed, later-issued, earlier-expiring relatives with the same priority date. **Skill rule:** Portfolio: identify the family's first-filed, first-issued patent and treat it as the term anchor.
- *Acadia Pharms. Inc. v. Aurobindo Pharma Ltd.*, No. 2024-1401 (Fed. Cir. June 9, 2025) (nonprecedential): applied Allergan; affirmed no ODP invalidity.
- *Ex parte Baurin*, Appeal 2024-002920 (USPTO Appeals Review Panel, Aug. 6, 2026) (precedential; decision read): the Office reads Allergan narrowly in examination (same family, same patent term filing date, claims first-filed, first-issued, and later-expiring) and treats harassment risk by separate owners as an independent ODP basis under current Federal Circuit precedent, while inviting the court to say otherwise. *In re Ablynx N.V.*, No. 2026-1333 (Fed. Cir.) pending on that question. Contrast *Ex parte Corteva Agriscience, LLC* (PTAB June 9, 2026), a Board panel decision that, per Baker Botts (Oct. 2026), reversed an ODP rejection by reading Allergan as turning on the relative relationship of the challenged and reference patents rather than their position in the family (an IPWatchdog report of June 24, 2026 on a Corteva reexamination describes a similar result with different PTA facts); status: not verified (decision not read). **Skill rule:** Office-action tool: on ODP rejections relying on Allergan, check whether Ablynx has been decided before arguing.
- Terminal-disclaimer NPRM (89 FR 40439, May 10, 2024) withdrawn Dec. 4, 2024 (89 FR 96152). No change to 37 CFR 1.321.
- *Global Tubing LLC v. Tenaris Coiled Tubes LLC*, Nos. 2023-1882, -1883 (Fed. Cir. Feb. 26, 2026) (precedential): vacated summary judgment of inequitable conduct; specific intent of an identified person, with inferences for the nonmovant. **Skill rule:** Analysis: never describe withheld art as "inequitable conduct" in a report; describe facts bearing on materiality and intent separately.
- *Luv N' Care, Ltd. v. Laurain*, 98 F.4th 1081 (Fed. Cir. 2024): unclean hands from litigation misconduct affirmed.
- *Blue Gentian, LLC v. Tristar Prods., Inc.*, 70 F.4th 1351 (Fed. Cir. 2023): joint inventor added where his contributions were the features used to distinguish the prior art.
- *Personalized Media Commc'ns, LLC v. Apple Inc.*, 57 F.4th 1346 (Fed. Cir. 2023) (laches affirmed, pre-GATT) and *Google LLC v. Sonos, Inc.*, No. 2024-1097 (Fed. Cir. Aug. 28, 2025) (nonprecedential; laches reversed, post-GATT continuation, no prejudice shown).
- USPTO Revised Inventorship Guidance for AI-Assisted Inventions, 90 FR 54636 (Nov. 28, 2025), rescinding the Feb. 2024 guidance: see `../ai-and-inventorship.md`.
- FY2025 fee rule (effective Jan. 19, 2025): continuing-application surcharges at more than 6 and 9 years from the earliest benefit date, and IDS size fees: see `../fees.md`.

## Checklist for the skill

- Map every claim to the earliest application (and every intermediate link) that supports it; record the result per claim in state.json.
- Confirm copendency and the full specific-reference chain in the ADS before any continuing filing.
- For any new matter in a CIP or Paris/nonprovisional filing, keep at least one claim set supported by the priority document alone.
- Run the inventorship interview claim by claim (conception, Pannu); list contributors who are not inventors and why.
- Check assignments or duties to assign for every inventor and collaborator; banner any gap.
- Before an IDS or declaration, gather foreign-counterpart and litigation statements about the same references; disclose inconsistencies.
- Never let a declaration go out with unverified factual statements; flag declarations as per se material if wrong.
- On double patenting: classify statutory vs. nonstatutory; compare claims to claims; argue distinctness first; offer a terminal disclaimer only with the term and ownership consequences stated.
- Compute expiration with PTA and terminal disclaimers for every family member; flag Gilead/Cellect exposure and the Allergan anchor.
- Docket each ODP reference patent's expiration as the last day to file a terminal disclaimer.
- After a restriction, file non-elected groups as divisionals to keep the 121 safe harbor.
- For reissue: confirm the error, the two-year limit for any broadening, recapture, and intervening-rights exposure; keep original claims unchanged where possible.
- Before enforcement, consider supplemental examination for any known disclosure problem.
- Log a business reason for each continuation; file competitor-tracking claims promptly.
- Never apply inequitable conduct, ODP, or US priority rules to foreign offices; route to local counsel.
