# How to use this file

patentassist covers US utility practice (SKILL.md US-utility scope). For design filing, prosecution, or a design infringement opinion, give the deadline facts in `references/deadlines.md` and recommend design counsel. This file exists so the skill does not miss design patents where they matter to utility work:
1. **FTO screens** on products whose appearance is distinctive or was copied. Design patents are a separate risk class with a harsh remedy (the infringer's total profit on the article, § 289).
2. **Portfolio advice.** Point out that appearance can be protected separately (15-year term from grant for applications filed on or after May 13, 2015, no maintenance fees, 6-month Paris priority, no provisional benefit), and route the filing to counsel.
3. **Utility drafting.** Drawings and statements in a utility application can later support, or undercut, a design application (priority under § 120; functionality evidence).
4. **Not importing utility rules.** Design claims are the drawings; there is no element-by-element chart, no doctrine of equivalents in the utility sense, and a different infringement test.

Design patents need a distinct analysis beyond this utility workflow. Give a scoped risk assessment, side-by-side views and closest prior art; identify when specialist review is needed. Do not guarantee validity or clearance.

Statutes: § 171 (new, original, ornamental design for an article of manufacture; utility provisions apply unless otherwise provided), § 172 (6-month priority; § 119(e) provisional benefit does not apply to designs), § 173 (term), § 289 (total profit, not less than $250; no double recovery with § 284).

## 1. Validity: obviousness

### LKQ Corp. v. GM Global Technology Operations LLC, 102 F.4th 1280 (Fed. Cir. 2024) (en banc) · supplemental reading (not in casebook)
- **Facts:** IPR against GM's design patent for a Chevrolet Equinox front fender. The Board found no anticipation by the Lian design patent and no obviousness because Lian was not a Rosen reference (not "basically the same" overall visual impression), ending the inquiry. A panel affirmed, saying only the en banc court or the Supreme Court could revisit Rosen-Durling.
- **Holding / rule:** Rosen-Durling overruled as improperly rigid after KSR and Whitman Saddle. No threshold that the primary reference be "basically the same" as the claimed design, and no requirement that secondary references be "so related" that one suggests applying its features to the other. Obviousness of designs follows the Graham factors: (1) scope and content of prior art, limited by an analogous-art requirement (same field of endeavor as the article of manufacture is analogous; other art may be, decided case by case); a primary reference must still be identified, likely the most visually similar design; (2) differences in overall visual appearance; (3) level of skill of an ordinary designer of articles of that type; (4) whether that designer would have modified the primary reference to create the "same overall visual appearance as the claimed design," with "some record-supported reason (without hindsight)"; and secondary considerations (commercial success, industry praise, copying recognized; long-felt need and failure of others left open).
- **Nuance:** The motivation need not come from the references themselves (KSR). The more different the primary and secondary designs look, the more work the challenger must do. The second ("reasonably pertinent to the problem") prong of utility analogous-art law does not translate cleanly to designs; left undefined. Lourie concurred in the judgment: modify Rosen-Durling's "must"/"only" language instead of overruling, and treat analogous art as a gateway, not part of the obviousness test. The court also reaffirmed that a tribunal need not, and should not, demand a detailed verbal description of the claimed design; the Board erred in rejecting LKQ's grounds for not verbalizing the design. No anticipation finding (ordinary observer test) reinstated.
- **Status:** Good law as of 2026-11-18. USPTO memo of May 22, 2024 told examiners and the PTAB to apply a flexible, Graham-based approach and to consult supervisors on analogous-art questions. Supersedes the obviousness framework applied in High Point (2013), Durling (1996), and In re Rosen (CCPA 1982).
- **Skill rule:** FTO and invalidity routing: when a design patent is a risk, collect prior art for the same article and plausibly analogous articles, plus evidence of what designers in that field commonly do (catalogs, trade practice), because after LKQ a combination need not start from a near-identical reference. Portfolio: tell clients that design patents issued under Rosen-Durling may be easier to attack now.

### High Point Design LLC v. Buyers Direct, Inc., 730 F.3d 1301 (Fed. Cir. 2013) · CB p. 1030
- **Facts:** Design patent on a fleece-lined slipper (SNOOZIES). District court held it obvious on summary judgment over Woolrich slippers plus two dot-sole design patents, judging from an "ordinary observer" view and disregarding the patentee's designer expert, and also invalid as "primarily functional" because the slipper's main features (covers the foot, fuzzy lining) serve functions.
- **Holding / rule:** Reversed on both grounds. (1) Obviousness of a design is judged from the viewpoint of the ordinary designer of articles of the type involved, not the ordinary observer (In re Nalbandian, 661 F.2d 1214 (CCPA 1981)). Expert testimony is relevant to the factual inquiries. Secondary considerations must be considered when presented. (2) Functionality invalidates a design only if the claimed design as a whole is "dictated by" the article's utilitarian purpose (primarily functional, not primarily ornamental). That an article and its features serve functions is a prerequisite of design patentability, not a defeat. Factors (PHG Techs., LLC v. St. John Cos., 469 F.3d 1361 (Fed. Cir. 2006)): whether the design is the best design; whether alternatives would hurt utility; concomitant utility patents; advertising touting particular features as useful; elements or overall appearance clearly not dictated by function.
- **Nuance:** Under the then-governing Durling test, the court also required the district court to verbalize the claimed design at the right level of detail and to show its side-by-side reasoning for the primary reference. That verbalization requirement is in tension with Egyptian Goddess and was rejected as a ground of decision in LKQ. The "dictated by" standard is stringent (Rosco, Inc. v. Mirror Lite Co., 304 F.3d 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2002)); availability of alternative designs that work as well weighs heavily against invalidity.
- **Status:** Obviousness framework superseded by LKQ (2024); designer-not-observer viewpoint reaffirmed in LKQ. Functionality holding good law as of 2026-11-18 (alternative designs as an important factor: Ethicon Endo-Surgery, Inc. v. Covidien, Inc., 796 F.3d 1312 (Fed. Cir. 2015)). Aesthetic "must-match" demand for replacement parts does not make a design functional (Automotive Body Parts Ass'n v. Ford Global Techs., LLC, 930 F.3d 1314 (Fed. Cir. 2019)).
- **Skill rule:** Utility drafting and portfolio: when the client may also seek design protection for a product's shape, warn that a utility patent claiming that shape, and marketing that says the shape works better, are PHG evidence of functionality; describe the shape's function in the utility case precisely and avoid calling the overall look necessary for function. FTO: never dismiss a design patent as "invalid because the product is functional"; the question is whether the particular appearance is dictated by function.

### Note cases on design validity (compact)
- Smith v. Whitman Saddle Co., 148 U.S. 674 (1893) · CB pp. 1050–52: joining the front of one known saddle to the back of another, as saddlers customarily did, was not inventive; and the one new feature (a sharp pommel drop) was absent from the accused saddle, so no infringement. LKQ relies on it for flexible obviousness; Egyptian Goddess for the prior-art-informed observer.
- Durling v. Spectrum Furniture Co., 101 F.3d 100 (Fed. Cir. 1996); In re Rosen, 673 F.2d 388 (CCPA 1982); In re Borden, 90 F.3d 1570 (Fed. Cir. 1996); In re Glavas, 230 F.2d 447 (CCPA 1956) · CB pp. 1034–38: the "basically the same" primary reference and "so related" secondary reference requirements. Overruled by LKQ. Glavas's point that a float designer would not look to soap or razor-sharpener designs survives as analogous-art reasoning.
- Hupp v. Siroflex of Am., Inc., 122 F.3d 1456 (Fed. Cir. 1997) · CB p. 1038: Graham factors govern design obviousness; prior art scope is designs of the same or sufficiently similar articles. Endorsed in LKQ.
- Titan Tire Corp. v. Case New Holland, Inc., 566 F.3d 1372 (Fed. Cir. 2009); Apple, Inc. v. Samsung Elecs. Co., 678 F.3d 1314 (Fed. Cir. 2012) · CB p. 1035: ordinary-designer viewpoint; too-abstract verbal descriptions error (Apple). Campbell Soup Co. v. Gamon Plus, Inc., 10 F.4th 1268 (Fed. Cir. 2021) and MRC Innovations, Inc. v. Hunter Mfg., LLP, 747 F.3d 1326 (Fed. Cir. 2014): secondary considerations apply to designs; nexus required. LKQ left these undisturbed.
- Functionality line: L.A. Gear, Inc. v. Thom McAn Shoe Co., 988 F.2d 1117 (Fed. Cir. 1993); Avia Grp. Int'l, Inc. v. L.A. Gear Cal., Inc., 853 F.2d 1557 (Fed. Cir. 1988); Berry Sterling Corp. v. Pescor Plastics, Inc., 122 F.3d 1452 (Fed. Cir. 1997); Best Lock Corp. v. Ilco Unican Corp., 94 F.3d 1563 (Fed. Cir. 1996) (key blade design invalid where its shape was dictated by fitting the lock; the parties agreed no other blade shape would fit the keyway) · CB pp. 1039–40.
- Anticipation uses the ordinary observer test (International Seaway Trading Corp. v. Walgreens Corp., 589 F.3d 1233 (Fed. Cir. 2009)), and the reference must be for the article named in the claim (In re SurgiSil, below).

## 2. Claim scope and claim construction

### Egyptian Goddess, Inc. v. Swisa, Inc., 543 F.3d 665 (Fed. Cir. 2008) (en banc), Part III · CB p. 1044
- **Holding / rule (construction):** Trial courts must construe design claims, but the preferable course is usually not to give a detailed verbal description; the drawings are the best description. The court may instead guide the fact-finder on drafting conventions (broken lines show unclaimed matter, 37 C.F.R. § 1.152), effects of prosecution history, and which features are ornamental versus purely functional (OddzOn Prods., Inc. v. Just Toys, Inc., 122 F.3d 1396 (Fed. Cir. 1997): where a design has functional and non-functional elements, construe the claim to identify the non-functional aspects).
- **Nuance:** A detailed verbal construction is not reversible error absent prejudice, but it risks making the jury focus on listed features instead of the whole design. Pointing out specific feature differences (three padded sides vs four) to a jury is allowed.
- **Status:** Good law as of 2026-11-18, reaffirmed in LKQ (2024) and Range of Motion (2026).
- **Skill rule:** Analysis: never build a utility-style element chart for a design claim; describe scope by the figures (solid lines claimed, broken lines not), the article named in the claim, and any prosecution statements, then compare whole appearances.

### Note cases on design claim scope (compact)
- Contessa Food Prods., Inc. v. Conagra, Inc., 282 F.3d 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002) · CB p. 1068: infringement compares all ornamental features shown in all views, including those not visible at the point of sale (e.g., a tray's underside), but visible in normal use. Elmer v. ICC Fabricating, Inc., 67 F.3d 1571 (Fed. Cir. 1995): features shown in solid lines limit the claim.
- Factoring out function: Richardson v. Stanley Works, Inc., 597 F.3d 1288 (Fed. Cir. 2010) (multi-tool; functional aspects factored out before comparing); Ethicon (2015) (claim covers the ornamental aspects of functional elements; do not erase them); Sport Dimension, Inc. v. Coleman Co., 820 F.3d 1316 (Fed. Cir. 2016) (error to eliminate whole structural elements from scope; factor out only their functional aspects); Lanard Toys Ltd. v. Dolgencorp LLC, 958 F.3d 1337 (Fed. Cir. 2020) (pencil-shaped chalk holder; proper to discount functional features and compare overall ornamental designs; no revival of point-of-novelty).
- Article of manufacture limits scope: Curver Luxembourg, SARL v. Home Expressions Inc., 938 F.3d 1334 (Fed. Cir. 2019) (claim to a pattern "for a chair" not infringed by baskets bearing the pattern); In re SurgiSil (2021), below.
- Prosecution history: Goodyear Tire & Rubber Co. v. Hercules Tire & Rubber Co., 162 F.3d 1113 (Fed. Cir. 1998) · CB p. 1060; Pacific Coast Marine Windshields Ltd. v. Malibu Boats, LLC, 739 F.3d 694 (Fed. Cir. 2014) (prosecution history estoppel applies to designs; cancelling figures with two holes and no holes surrendered those designs, but the accused three-hole design was outside the surrender, so summary judgment of noninfringement was reversed); Top Brand LLC v. Cozy Comfort Co., 143 F.4th 1349 (Fed. Cir. 2025) (prosecution history disclaimer applies; arguments distinguishing art on pocket size and location narrowed the claim; infringement verdict reversed).
- Priority between utility and design: a design application may claim § 120 benefit of an earlier utility application whose drawings describe the design (Racing Strollers, Inc. v. TRI Indus., Inc., 878 F.2d 1418 (Fed. Cir. 1989) (en banc)); written description by drawings controls: a continuation that removes a whole ornamental element shown in the parent keeps the parent's date (In re Daniels, 144 F.3d 1452 (Fed. Cir. 1998): container claimed without the parent's floral ornament), but one that adds a new unclaimed boundary line the parent did not show does not (In re Owens, 710 F.3d 1362 (Fed. Cir. 2013): broken line splitting a bottle's front panel).
- **Skill rule (drafting utility spec):** when a product's appearance may matter commercially, include clean, complete views (all six orthographic views plus perspective) of the commercial embodiment in the utility application so design counsel can later claim § 120 benefit; note in the gate summary that design protection is a separate filing for counsel, with the 6-month Paris and no-provisional rules.

## 3. Infringement

### Egyptian Goddess, Inc. v. Swisa, Inc., 543 F.3d 665 (Fed. Cir. 2008) (en banc) · CB p. 1044
- **Facts:** Design patent on a nail buffer: hollow tube, square cross-section, raised buffer pads on three of four sides, fourth side bare. Accused buffer: same hollow square tube with pads on all four sides. Prior art: Nailco (hollow triangular tube, pads on all three sides) and Falley (solid square block, pads on all sides). Lower courts applied the "point of novelty" test (and the panel a "non-trivial advance" test) and found no infringement.
- **Holding / rule:** The point-of-novelty test (Litton Sys., Inc. v. Whirlpool Corp., 728 F.2d 1423 (Fed. Cir. 1984)) and the non-trivial-advance test are abandoned. The Gorham ordinary observer test is the sole test: infringement if, to an ordinary observer familiar with the prior art, giving the attention a purchaser usually gives, the accused design is substantially the same as the claimed design, so as to induce purchase of one supposing it to be the other. If the designs are "plainly dissimilar," the patentee loses without more. If not, compare both against the prior art: in a crowded field, small differences matter more. The patentee bears the burden of proof by preponderance; the accused infringer bears the burden of producing comparison prior art it relies on. Applied: no reasonable fact-finder could find infringement because the accused four-sided buffer looks closer to Nailco than to the patented three-pad design.
- **Nuance:** The test is about the whole design, not individual features; the novel features still matter inside the comparison. A conclusory expert declaration that ignored the closest prior art could not create a fact issue. The prior-art comparison is not a validity test. Loser's argument: the patentee's expert said one extra pad does not change the overall effect, but she never explained why the accused design was closer to the patent than to Nailco. Fact that flips it: in a sparse field, the same accused design could well be substantially the same as the claim.
- **Status:** Good law as of 2026-11-18. The two-step practice ("plainly dissimilar," then three-way comparison) is applied routinely (Ethicon, 2015; Range of Motion, 2026). On Aug. 11, 2026 the Federal Circuit denied rehearing en banc in Range of Motion; Judges Stoll and Stark dissented without opinion, and Chief Judge Moore (joined by Judge Reyna) dissented, arguing the "sufficiently distinct"/"plainly dissimilar" gloss departs from Gorham's "substantially the same" standard and that the prior art should always be considered. Watch for further challenges.
- **Skill rule:** FTO: when a design patent is found, show the claimed figures, the product, and the two or three closest prior-art designs for the same article side by side, and note how crowded the field is; never chart features or conclude. Do not tell a client that changing one feature avoids infringement unless that feature distinguishes the design from close prior art.

### Arminak & Associates, Inc. v. Saint-Gobain Calmar, Inc., 501 F.3d 1314 (Fed. Cir. 2007) · CB p. 1064
- **Facts:** Calmar's two design patents claimed trigger-sprayer shrouds (not the nozzle, trigger, or cap). Calmar sold its shrouds to companies that assemble bottled household products, never to retail consumers. Accused AA Trigger shroud. District court gave a detailed figure-by-figure construction and granted summary judgment of noninfringement.
- **Holding / rule:** Affirmed. The ordinary observer is the purchaser of the article bearing the design; where the design is on a component bought by industrial or contract purchasers for assembly into retail goods, the ordinary observer is that commercial buyer, not the retail consumer of the finished product. Calmar's own expert conceded corporate buyers would easily tell the shrouds apart. Detailed claim construction is allowed if it captures the design as a whole and stays within the claimed non-functional ornamental features. The comparison covers all views in the patent, not just those a retail buyer would see.
- **Nuance:** "Ordinary" does not mean lay consumer; it means the person who buys (or is sufficiently interested in) the item and can make a reasonably discerning judgment, short of an expert (Gorham excluded trade experts comparing side by side). Earlier cases: truck drivers and fleet operators for truck-tire treads (Goodyear); trade-show visitors for retaining-wall blocks, judged block by block (KeyStone Retaining Wall Sys., Inc. v. Westrock, Inc., 997 F.2d 1444 (Fed. Cir. 1993)). Critics (Saidman) argued sophisticated observers make infringement nearly unprovable. The terminal disclaimer over Calmar's other design patent was noted in construction.
- **Status:** Ordinary-observer identity holding is good law as of 2026-11-18. Its application of a separate point-of-novelty test was abrogated by Egyptian Goddess (2008), which cited Arminak for the "colorable imitation" formulation.
- **Skill rule:** FTO: identify who actually buys the article that carries the design (end consumer, OEM buyer, surgeon, distributor) and record it with the design-patent hit, because it changes how much difference is needed. Portfolio: for component designs sold business-to-business, warn that enforcement is judged by expert-ish buyers, so broader or multiple design filings may be needed.

### Note cases on infringement (compact)
- Gorham Co. v. White, 81 U.S. (14 Wall.) 511 (1871) · CB p. 1047: sameness of effect upon the eye of an ordinary purchaser, not an expert; minor line differences do not avoid infringement.
- Litton Sys. (1984); Sears, Roebuck & Co. v. Talge, 140 F.2d 395 (8th Cir. 1944); Applied Arts Corp. v. Grand Rapids Metalcraft Corp., 67 F.2d 428 (6th Cir. 1933); Bevin Bros. (C.C.D. Conn. 1902); Zidell v. Dexter, 262 F. 145 (9th Cir. 1920) · CB pp. 1052–55: the historical basis for the prior-art-informed observer; in a crowded art, scope is narrow. Point-of-novelty as a separate test abrogated.
- Crocs, Inc. v. ITC, 598 F.3d 1294 (Fed. Cir. 2010): verbal claim construction should not displace the drawings; small differences matter more when the claim is close to the prior art.
- Logos and labels: Columbia Sportswear N. Am., Inc. v. Seirus Innovative Accessories, Inc., 942 F.3d 1119 (Fed. Cir. 2019) (summary judgment of infringement reversed; a logo cannot be ignored merely because it is a logo, though adding a label does not by itself avoid infringement); 80 F.4th 1363 (Fed. Cir. 2023) (comparison prior art must be designs applied to the article of manufacture named in the claim, here heat-reflective material; non-infringement judgment vacated and remanded; no error in the logo-related jury instructions, and a logo may make designs dissimilar even though source confusion is irrelevant).
- Component vs end product observers: Spotless Enters. (E.D.N.Y. 2003) (garment-hanger buyers) and Puritan-Bennett (S.D. Ind. 2004) (medical distributors) · CB p. 1067: district courts.

## 4. Remedy: total profit (§ 289)

### Samsung Electronics Co. v. Apple Inc., 580 U.S. 53 (2016) · CB p. 1071
- **Facts:** Samsung phones infringed Apple design patents on a black rounded-rectangle front face, a face with a raised rim, and a 16-icon grid. Jury awarded $399 million, Samsung's entire profit on the phones. Federal Circuit affirmed, reasoning the phone's components were not sold separately to consumers.
- **Holding / rule:** Reversed (unanimous). Under § 289, the "article of manufacture" to which the design is applied can be the end product sold to consumers or a component of it, whether or not the component is sold separately. A § 289 award has two steps: identify the article of manufacture; compute the infringer's total profit on that article.
- **Nuance:** The Court declined to set a test for identifying the article. The Solicitor General proposed four factors: the scope of the design as claimed, the design's prominence in the whole product, whether the design is conceptually distinct from the product, and the physical relationship between the design and the rest of the product (separable, sold or made separately). Once the article is identified, profit on it is not apportioned further: § 289 says "total profit," and Samsung recounts that Congress enacted the remedy in 1887 in response to the Dobson cases, which had required proof of profit "due to" the design. Do not cite Nordock, Inc. v. Systems Inc., 803 F.3d 1344 (Fed. Cir. 2015), for this: the Supreme Court vacated that judgment in light of Samsung (Systems, Inc. v. Nordock, Inc., No. 15-978 (U.S. Dec. 12, 2016)), and on remand the Federal Circuit ordered a new damages trial (nonprecedential, Mar. 17, 2017).
- **Status:** Good law as of 2026-11-18. On remand the district court adopted the SG's four-factor test and burden allocation (Apple Inc. v. Samsung Elecs. Co., No. 11-cv-01846 (N.D. Cal. 2017)) (status: not verified; order not retrieved). No Federal Circuit test located as of 2026-11-18; Columbia v. Seirus (2019 and 2023) twice declined to reach the article-of-manufacture and burden questions. Marking under § 287 applies to § 289 recovery (Nike, Inc. v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc., 138 F.3d 1437 (Fed. Cir. 1998)). The patentee may elect § 289 profits or § 284 damages but not recover twice.
- **Skill rule:** FTO: when a design patent hit concerns a product's appearance, rank its exposure separately and higher than a comparable utility hit, because recovery can be all profit on the article (possibly the whole product) without apportionment within it; note that the article may be a component, which is the main defense lever. Portfolio: for designs, advise claiming the specific component as well as the whole product (separate filings via counsel) so the claimed article matches what competitors copy.

## 5. System notes (compact)

- **Term.** 15 years from grant for design applications filed on or after May 13, 2015 (when the Hague Agreement provisions of the Patent Law Treaties Implementation Act took effect); 14 years from grant for earlier filings. The casebook's intro links the 15-year term to Dec. 18, 2013 (the PLTIA's other effective date), which is not the date that controls the term. No maintenance fees. Skill rule: portfolio: compute design expiry from grant date and filing date, never from filing date alone.
- **Hague Agreement** (Geneva Act): international design applications through the USPTO; the casebook gives Dec. 18, 2013 as the US effective date, but the Geneva Act took effect for the US on May 13, 2015 (USPTO; MPEP 1505). Route to counsel.
- **Same product, two patents.** A product's function can be claimed in a utility patent and its ornamental appearance in a design patent; overlapping claims can raise double-patenting questions (Carman Indus., Inc. v. Wahl, 724 F.2d 932 (Fed. Cir. 1983)) (status: not verified as a design-and-utility case; the casebook cites it, at 940, for the "same invention" double-patenting test). A concomitant utility patent is also a PHG functionality factor (see High Point).
- **GUIs and icons.** Design patents issue on computer-generated interfaces shown on a display screen (the casebook reproduces one); the claim must tie the design to an article such as a display screen; an image merely shown on a screen is not enough, but a computer icon or GUI shown on a display panel qualifies if properly claimed (MPEP 1504.01(a) [R-01.2024]). Route to counsel.
- **Vessel hulls and preemption.** Bonito Boats, Inc. v. Thunder Craft Boats, Inc., 489 U.S. 141 (1989): a state ban on plug-molding unpatented hulls was preempted. Congress then enacted the Vessel Hull Design Protection Act (17 U.S.C. ch. 13; registration, notice, 10-year term, innocent-infringer exception) · CB p. 1042. Fashion-design bills were not enacted.
- **Replacement parts.** No repair exception for design patents: the PARTS Act (introduced in several Congresses) has not been enacted as far as known (status not verified for 2025–26); the ITC excluded third-party F-150 replacement parts (Inv. No. 337-TA-557, 2007) · CB p. 1070; making replacement parts is not shielded by exhaustion or "repair" (Automotive Body Parts Ass'n v. Ford, 930 F.3d 1314 (Fed. Cir. 2019)). LKQ (2024) arose from the same industry fight.
- **Foreign systems** mostly use sui generis design registration (e.g., EU registered designs; Canada's industrial design office); TRIPS Arts. 25–26 set minimums · CB p. 1043. Rule 9: refer.

## Edge-case playbook

1. **"Our product looks similar but we changed several features."** Trap: counting differences like utility elements. Correct: the question is overall visual impression to the ordinary observer familiar with the prior art; in a sparse field, many small changes may not avoid infringement, while in a crowded field one change near the prior art can (Egyptian Goddess; Whitman Saddle).
2. **"The accused product doesn't copy what was novel, so no infringement."** Trap: applying the dead point-of-novelty test. Correct: there is no separate point-of-novelty requirement; novel features matter only within the whole-design comparison (Egyptian Goddess).
3. **"Our logo is on it, so no one is deceived."** Trap: trademark thinking. Correct: deception concerns the design, not the source; a logo is not ignored automatically but does not by itself avoid infringement (Columbia 2019, 2023; Gorham).
4. **Component sold to manufacturers.** Trap: judging through retail consumers' eyes. Correct: the ordinary observer is the buyer of the article bearing the design, often a discerning commercial buyer (Arminak; KeyStone; Goodyear).
5. **Comparison art from another product category.** Trap: using any similar-looking object to narrow the claim or anticipate it. Correct: comparison prior art for infringement and anticipating art must be for the article named in the claim (Columbia 2023; SurgiSil); but for obviousness, analogous art beyond the exact article may count (LKQ).
6. **Obviousness from the "ordinary observer" view.** Trap: mixing tests. Correct: anticipation and infringement use the ordinary observer; obviousness uses the ordinary designer (High Point; LKQ).
7. **No near-identical primary reference, so design is not obvious.** Trap: pre-2024 Rosen rule. Correct: after LKQ there is no "basically the same" threshold; a primary reference (likely the most similar) plus record-supported reasons to modify can suffice.
8. **"It's a functional product, so the design patent is invalid."** Trap: confusing a functional article with a functional design. Correct: invalidity requires the overall appearance be dictated by function; alternative designs that work as well weigh against invalidity (High Point; Ethicon; Rosco). Aesthetic must-match demand is not functionality (Automotive Body Parts).
9. **Functional features in the claim.** Trap (two opposite ones): ignoring function entirely, or deleting whole elements. Correct: construction factors out functional aspects and keeps the ornamental aspects of functional elements; then compare overall ornamental appearance (Richardson; Ethicon; Sport Dimension; Range of Motion, 2026).
10. **Design patent damages "apportioned to the design's value."** Trap: utility apportionment thinking. Correct: § 289 gives total profit on the article of manufacture; the fight is over what the article is (whole product or component), not apportionment within it (Samsung v. Apple).
11. **Unmarked product, design patent asserted.** Trap: thinking § 289 escapes marking. Correct: § 287 applies to § 289 recovery (Nike v. Wal-Mart).
12. **Claim says "chair," accused product is a basket with the same pattern.** Trap: designs as abstract patterns. Correct: the claim is limited to the named article (Curver; SurgiSil).
13. **Views the shopper never sees.** Trap: comparing only the display face. Correct: all claimed views count, including bottoms and interiors visible in normal use (Contessa; Arminak).
14. **Applicant distinguished prior art during design prosecution.** Trap: assuming design prosecution history doesn't matter. Correct: estoppel and disclaimer apply to designs (Pacific Coast Marine; Top Brand, 2025).
15. **Client wants design protection based on a provisional.** Trap: utility habits. Correct: designs cannot claim provisional benefit (§ 172); Paris priority for designs is 6 months; a design may claim § 120 benefit of an earlier utility nonprovisional whose drawings show it (Racing Strollers).
16. **Utility spec and ads say the product's shape is what makes it work.** Trap: harmless marketing. Correct: concomitant utility patents and function-touting ads are PHG factors that support functionality findings and narrower claim scope (High Point; Range of Motion relied on a utility patent and marketing).
17. **Design patent term computed like utility.** Trap: 20 years from filing, or 15 years for every design. Correct: 15 years from grant if filed on or after May 13, 2015; 14 years from grant if filed earlier; no maintenance fees.

## Post-casebook developments (through 2026-11-18)

- **Curver Luxembourg, SARL v. Home Expressions Inc., 938 F.3d 1334 (Fed. Cir. 2019).** Article named in the claim limits scope. Skill rule: portfolio: an abstract surface pattern needs claims on each article of commercial interest (counsel).
- **Automotive Body Parts Ass'n v. Ford Global Techs., LLC, 930 F.3d 1314 (Fed. Cir. 2019).** No aesthetic-functionality invalidity for must-match parts; exhaustion and repair do not permit making replacement parts. Skill rule: FTO in aftermarket parts: flag OEM design patents as high risk.
- **Columbia Sportswear v. Seirus, 942 F.3d 1119 (Fed. Cir. 2019); 80 F.4th 1363 (Fed. Cir. 2023).** Logos considered in the overall comparison; comparison prior art limited to the claimed article. Skill rule: FTO: pull comparison art for the same article only.
- **Lanard Toys Ltd. v. Dolgencorp LLC, 958 F.3d 1337 (Fed. Cir. 2020).** Factoring out functional elements and comparing overall ornamental design is proper; no point-of-novelty revival.
- **In re SurgiSil, L.L.P., 14 F.4th 1380 (Fed. Cir. 2021).** A design claim is limited to the article identified; an art-tool reference did not anticipate a lip-implant design. Skill rule: when screening design prior art, filter by article first.
- **Campbell Soup Co. v. Gamon Plus, Inc., 10 F.4th 1268 (Fed. Cir. 2021).** Secondary considerations for designs require nexus to the claimed design. Cited in LKQ.
- **LKQ Corp. v. GM Global Tech. Operations LLC, 102 F.4th 1280 (Fed. Cir. 2024) (en banc);** USPTO guidance May 22, 2024. Rosen-Durling overruled; Graham factors with analogous art. Skill rule: see entry above.
- **Top Brand LLC v. Cozy Comfort Co., 143 F.4th 1349 (Fed. Cir. July 17, 2025).** Prosecution history disclaimer applies to design patents; infringement verdict reversed. Reporter cite confirmed in the court's own Aug. 11, 2026 Range of Motion order.
- **Range of Motion Prods., LLC v. Armaid Co., No. 2023-2427 (Fed. Cir. Feb. 2, 2026) (precedential), reh'g en banc denied (Aug. 11, 2026).** Cunningham (with Hughes): courts construe design claims to discount aspects dictated by function using the PHG factors; dotted-line disclaimers and examiner silence do not settle functionality; the remaining ornamental designs were plainly dissimilar; side-by-side "overlay" comparisons that skip claim construction rejected. Moore dissented (the "plainly dissimilar" framing led to a feature-by-feature hunt for differences; a reasonable jury could find the designs substantially the same; prior art should always be considered) and, with Reyna, dissented from denial of rehearing en banc, adding that sorting functional from ornamental aspects is a jury question, not claim construction; Stoll and Stark dissented from the denial without opinion. Skill rule: FTO: a design patent's practical scope can be much narrower than its drawings once functional aspects are discounted; still report it and refer, because the standard is contested.

## Checklist for the skill

- Scope first: design filing, prosecution, and infringement opinions go to design counsel (rule 9); give deadlines (6-month Paris, no provisional benefit, term from grant) and stop.
- In FTO screens for consumer or industrial products with a distinctive look, run a design-patent pass (search D-numbers for the product category and the competitor's assignee) and report hits separately as `design patent – refer`.
- For each design hit, record: the article named in the claim, solid vs broken lines, the ordinary observer (who buys this article), the closest same-article prior art, how crowded the field is, and any prosecution statements; show figures side by side. No element chart, no conclusion.
- Rank design exposure with § 289 in mind (total profit on the article; the article may be a component); check whether the owner marks (§ 287 applies).
- In utility drafting, when appearance matters commercially: include complete orthographic and perspective views of the embodiment, avoid saying the overall look is required for function, and flag a parallel design filing for counsel before any public disclosure.
- In portfolio work, compute design expiry correctly (15 or 14 years from grant by filing date) and note no maintenance fees.
- Never apply utility tools (DOE, all-elements, apportionment) to design patents, and never use the abandoned point-of-novelty or Rosen-Durling tests.
