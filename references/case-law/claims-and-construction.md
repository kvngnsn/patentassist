# Claims, claim formats, and claim construction

How to use this file. Drafting (Phase 3 Step 2) uses the Skill rule lines as hard drafting rules. Claim charts (`modules/analyze.md`) and office-action work (`modules/prosecution.md`) use the construction entries to decide what each claim term means before any element is marked met or not met. The edge-case playbook at the end is the part to reread before signing off on a claim set or a chart.

Statute in one paragraph. 35 U.S.C. 112(b): claims must "particularly point out and distinctly claim" the invention. 112(c)–(e): dependent and multiple dependent claims; a dependent claim incorporates every limitation of its parent. 112(f): a combination element may be stated as a means or step for a function, and it then covers only the corresponding structure, material, or acts in the specification and their equivalents. Pre-AIA cites say "112, second paragraph" (now 112(b)) and "112, sixth paragraph" (now 112(f)); the rules are the same.

## 1. Peripheral claiming and form rules

### Ex parte Fressola, 27 U.S.P.Q.2d 1608 (B.P.A.I. 1993) · CB p. 651
- **Facts:** Claim 42 read, in full, "A system for the display of stereographic three-dimensional images of celestial objects as disclosed in the specification and drawings herein." The spec ran 147 pages with program listings and data tables.
- **Holding / rule:** An "omnibus" claim that defines the invention only by reference to the spec or drawings fails 112(b). The claim must itself define the invention; reference to the spec is allowed only where words cannot do the job.
- **Nuance:** Three independent grounds: (1) the claim does no defining work of its own; (2) it is impossible to tell how much of the disclosure is pulled in or what equivalents follow; (3) even if incorporation were allowed, the spec itself was not a boundary (prior art, alternatives, source code, and an "illustrative, not limiting" boilerplate clause). The applicant's "it's really a narrow picture claim" argument failed because nothing confined it to the exact embodiment. The narrow exception survives where a figure or table is the only practical way to state a limitation (for example, an X-ray diffraction pattern or a sequence listing); see MPEP 2173.05(s).
- **Status:** Good law as of 2026-11-18 (codified in practice at MPEP 2173.05(s); omnibus claims still allowed only in design patents).
- **Skill rule:** Drafting: never write "as shown in FIG. X," "substantially as described," or "as disclosed herein" in a utility claim; state every limitation in words, and reference a table or figure only when no verbal definition is possible (then also give the numbers in the claim). `check_claims.py` should BLOCK those phrases.

### Fressola v. Manbeck, 36 U.S.P.Q.2d 1211 (D.D.C. 1995) · CB p. 657
- One-sentence rule upheld against an APA challenge: the USPTO may require each claim to be a single sentence because form does not limit substance. **Skill rule:** Drafting: each claim is one sentence ending in one period, elements separated by semicolons and line indentation (37 CFR 1.75(i)); no periods inside a claim except in abbreviations.

Peripheral vs. central claiming (note material, CB pp. 652–653). Modern US claims mark the outer boundary ("peripheral"); courts do not expand literal scope to a "typical embodiment plus equivalents" ("central"). The doctrine of equivalents expands the right to exclude, not the claim (Wilson Sporting Goods v. David Geoffrey & Assocs., 904 F.2d 677, 684 (Fed. Cir. 1990)). **Skill rule:** Charts: chart literal scope first from the claim words; DOE is a separate column, never a way to "read the claim broadly."

Reverse-pyramid claim sets (note material, CB p. 658). Every claim can be asserted separately, and later-found art may kill the broad claims, so the set should step down from the broadest allowable claim to the commercial embodiment. **Skill rule:** Drafting: for each independent claim, include at least one dependent that reads exactly on the product the client will ship and at least two intermediate fallbacks that each avoid a different closest reference.

## 2. Claim anatomy: preamble, transition, body

### Catalina Marketing Int'l, Inc. v. Coolsavings.com, Inc., 289 F.3d 801 (Fed. Cir. 2002) · CB p. 659
- **Facts:** Claim 1's preamble recited a coupon system with terminals "located at predesignated sites such as consumer stores"; the body was a complete set of means elements for the terminal. Claim 25 repeated the location phrase in the body. The accused system was web-based coupons printed at home.
- **Holding / rule:** A preamble limits the claim when it recites essential structure or steps, or is "necessary to give life, meaning, and vitality" to the claim. It does not limit when the body defines a structurally complete invention and the preamble only states purpose or intended use. The location phrase did not limit claim 1, but the same phrase in claim 25's body did, and the accused system did not literally meet it.
- **Nuance:** Guideposts that make a preamble limiting: Jepson form; the body relies on the preamble for antecedent basis; the preamble is needed to understand body terms; the spec stresses the preamble feature as important; clear reliance on the preamble during prosecution to distinguish art. Applicant statements that the invention involved in-store terminals were not "clear reliance" because the examiner had called location insignificant and the applicant amended the body instead. Test: delete the phrase; if the claimed structure or steps do not change, the phrase is intended use. Intended use in an apparatus claim almost never limits, because an apparatus claim covers every use of the structure (Roberts v. Ryer, 91 U.S. 150 (1875)). Intended use can still support a separate new-use method claim (35 U.S.C. 100(b)). Fact that flips it: putting the phrase in the body (claim 25) or arguing it to the examiner.
- **Status:** Good law as of 2026-11-18; the guideposts are routinely applied (e.g., Pacing Techs., LLC v. Garmin Int'l, Inc., 778 F.3d 1021 (Fed. Cir. 2015) (preamble limiting where it supplied antecedent basis for "the user"); TomTom, Inc. v. Adolph, 790 F.3d 1315 (Fed. Cir. 2015) (one part of a preamble limited, another did not; construing one part does not make the whole preamble limiting), distinguished in Bio-Rad Labs., Inc. v. 10X Genomics Inc., 967 F.3d 1353 (Fed. Cir. 2020) (where the preamble terms that give antecedent basis are intertwined with the rest of the preamble, the court will not splice it, and the whole preamble limits)).
- **Skill rule:** Drafting: keep the preamble to a short category name ("A system," "A method of …ing a [object]") and put every feature that matters, including location or environment, in the body; never let a body term take its antecedent basis from the preamble unless you want the whole preamble to limit. Charts: before charting, decide expressly whether the preamble limits, using the Catalina guideposts, and check the prosecution history for reliance; mark the preamble row `construction_dependent` when the guideposts point both ways.

Other preamble cases named in Catalina (holdings as stated there): Bell Commc'ns Research v. Vitalink, 55 F.3d 615, 620 (Fed. Cir. 1995) (when the drafter uses both preamble and body to define the invention, the preamble limits); Pitney Bowes v. Hewlett-Packard, 182 F.3d 1298, 1305 (Fed. Cir. 1999) ("life, meaning, and vitality"); Rowe v. Dror, 112 F.3d 473, 478 (Fed. Cir. 1997) (purpose-only preamble does not limit; Jepson form signals a limiting preamble); Corning Glass Works v. Sumitomo, 868 F.2d 1251, 1257 (Fed. Cir. 1989) (no litmus test; read the whole patent); In re Gardiner, 171 F.2d 313 (CCPA 1948) (apparatus patentability lies in structure, not use).

### Transitions (note material, CB pp. 656–658)
- "Comprising" is open: an accused product with A+B+C+X infringes a claim to A+B+C (Mannesmann Demag Corp. v. Engineered Metal Prods. Co., 793 F.2d 1279 (Fed. Cir. 1986)). "Consisting of" is closed: adding any unrecited element avoids literal infringement (DOE is a separate question). "Consisting essentially of" is open only to unrecited elements that do not materially affect the "basic and novel characteristics" (U.S. Steel Corp. v. Phillips Petroleum Co., 865 F.2d 1247 (Fed. Cir. 1989)).
- **Skill rule:** Drafting: default to "comprising"; use "consisting essentially of" only when the spec states the basic and novel characteristics so the boundary can be tested (otherwise it is an indefiniteness and construction fight); use "consisting of" only where the novelty is the absence of something, and then add a parallel "comprising" claim. Charts: for a closed or hybrid claim, list every extra component of the accused product and test each against the transition.

### Claim body conventions (note material, CB pp. 658–659)
- Antecedent basis: introduce each element with "a/an/at least one/a plurality of," refer back with "the/said." A first-time "the" draws a 112(b) rejection. Courts give long-litigated words their judicial gloss ("integral," "approximately," "horizontal," "solid" each have case law). Holes and chambers are claimed by the structure that defines them ("a housing defining a chamber"). Trademarks in claims are rejected because the product behind the mark can change (Ex parte Bolton, 42 U.S.P.Q. 40 (Pat. Off. Bd. App. 1938)); see MPEP 2173.05(u). An applicant may be its own lexicographer but cannot use misdescriptive terms (Hormone Research Found. v. Genentech, 904 F.2d 1558, 1563 (Fed. Cir. 1990)).
- **Skill rule:** Drafting: run an antecedent-basis pass on every claim (each "the/said X" must trace to an earlier "a/an X" in the same claim or its parents, with the same words); claim spaces as "a [structure] defining a [space]"; never use a trademark or trade name in a claim, and use the generic term with a spec definition instead.

### Worked examples: the two class patents (U.S. 5,560,126 and 5,699,038)
Use these to practice parsing claims into chart rows. Teaching notes, not quotes; fetch the patent before quoting any of it.
- **U.S. 5,560,126 (Meschan et al., "Athletic shoe with improved sole," assignee Akeva, CIP filed 1994).** Claim 1: "A shoe comprising" an upper with a heel region; a rear sole "secured below" the heel region; and a flexible plate between rear sole and heel whose peripheral edges are restrained from movement "substantially perpendicular" to the shoe's major axis so the interior can deflect. Drills:
  - *Claim differentiation vs. spec emphasis.* Claim 1 says only "secured." Dependent claims 10 and 19 add "detachably secure[d]," claim 14 adds "means for detachably securing," and claim 19 adds selective rotation. Under Phillips/Hill-Rom, those dependents create a presumption that claim 1's "secured" also covers a permanently attached rear sole. Against that, the abstract and spec describe a "rotatable and replaceable rear sole" and use "the present invention" language. The chart must state both sides, then ask whether any passage rises to "words of manifest exclusion" (Hill-Rom). Mark the term `construction_dependent`.
  - *112(f) in a dependent.* Claim 14's "means for detachably securing the rear sole" invokes 112(f). Its scope is the disclosed securing structures (protrusion/indention engagement, interconnecting member, threaded rim, band) and their equivalents, not every way to detach a sole. Chart it by listing the spec structures first.
  - *Term of degree.* "Substantially perpendicular" in claims 1 and 25 needs a spec-based standard for the Nautilus test (see Section 4).
  - *Parallel independents.* Claims 1 and 25 differ only in how the plate edges are restrained (relative to the shoe axis vs. relative to the plate interior). This is a deliberate design-around catch: write a second independent that states the same inventive idea through a different frame of reference.
- **U.S. 5,699,038 (Ulrich et al., "Bed status information system for hospital beds," assignee Hill-Rom, CIP filed 1995).** This is the lead patent in Hill-Rom v. Stryker (Section 6). Claim 1: bed condition input signal generator on the bed; "an interface board including a processor" that turns input signals into bed condition messages; "a processing station remote from the bed and coupled with said interface board by a datalink"; humanly perceptible indication at a remote location. Drills:
  - *The dependents decide the independent.* Claim 8 ("the datalink is a serial datalink") and claim 12 (an optical isolator "in line with the datalink") narrow "datalink"; claim 7 adds an "interface unit" between board and station; claims 9 and 13–15 add messages flowing back to the board. The court relied on claims 7 and 9/13–15 (and their analogs under claim 19) to hold the "interface board" is not limited to a wall interface unit or two-way communication. For "datalink," it relied on dependents in the continuation '659 patent that recite a "wired datalink"; '038 claim 8 ("serial") plays the same role here.
  - *The dissent's point.* The same claim 12 suggests "datalink" is physical structure. Claim differentiation cuts both ways; chart both readings.
  - *Preamble.* Claim 1's preamble ("for indicating to attending personnel the status of a patient bed") is repeated in substance at the end of the body ("such that the status ... is indicated to attending personnel at a location remote from the bed"), so the purpose is in the body and limits regardless of the preamble rule.
- Charting drill for any claim: (1) split at the semicolons; (2) decide whether the preamble limits; (3) circle every term of degree, every functional phrase ("operable for," "configured to," "means for"), and every word the spec uses inconsistently; (4) give each term's spec support by column and line; (5) list what each dependent adds, since the independent is presumed not to require it.

## 3. Special claim formats

### Abbott Labs. v. Sandoz, Inc., 566 F.3d 1282 (Fed. Cir. 2009) (en banc in part) · CB p. 670
- **Facts:** Claim 1 defined crystalline cefdinir by PXRD peaks; claims 2–5 defined crystalline cefdinir as "obtainable by" listed process steps. Generic makers sold a different crystal form (Crystal B) made by different processes.
- **Holding / rule:** En banc (Part III.A.2): process terms in a product-by-process claim limit the claim for infringement. A product made by a different process does not literally infringe, even if it is identical. Scripps Clinic v. Genentech, 927 F.2d 1565 (Fed. Cir. 1991), is overruled to the extent inconsistent; Atlantic Thermoplastics v. Faytex, 970 F.2d 834 (Fed. Cir. 1992), is the rule. Panel holding: no DOE either, because Crystal B was not equivalent to Crystal A.
- **Nuance:** "Obtainable by" was treated the same as "obtained by"; wording did not save breadth. The asymmetry is the trap: for patentability and validity the claim is judged as a product (In re Thorpe, 777 F.2d 695, 697 (Fed. Cir. 1985)), so an old product made by a new process is unpatentable as a product, but for infringement the process steps must be practiced. Newman (with Mayer and Lourie) dissented, arguing the "rule of necessity" for products whose structure cannot be described should allow product-wide protection. Fact that flips the outcome: define the product by its structure or measured properties (as claim 1 did with PXRD peaks); then process does not matter.
- **Status:** Good law as of 2026-11-18 for US infringement. Not the rule at the EPO, which treats such claims as covering the product however made (US-only skill: do not apply foreign rules).
- **Skill rule:** Drafting: when a product is new, claim it by structure or by measurable properties with the test method stated in the spec; add a product-by-process claim only as a backup, plus an independent method-of-making claim. Charts/FTO: for a product-by-process claim, chart each process step against the accused maker's actual process; identical product made another way is not literal infringement. Invalidity: chart the product only, ignoring process steps unless they impart a distinct structure.

### Functional claiming background (note material, CB pp. 670–672)
- O'Reilly v. Morse, 56 U.S. (15 How.) 62 (1853): a claim to every use of electromagnetism for marking characters at a distance was too broad for what Morse described. A single-means claim (one element, purely functional) is invalid: In re Hyatt, 708 F.2d 712 (Fed. Cir. 1983) (enabling disclosure must match the claim's scope).
- Halliburton Oil Well Cementing Co. v. Walker, 329 U.S. 1 (1946): barred functional language at the exact point of novelty in a combination. Congress responded in 1952 with what is now 112(f), which allows functional combination elements but limits them to disclosed structure and equivalents (Valmont Indus. v. Reinke Mfg., 983 F.2d 1039, 1042 (Fed. Cir. 1993)).
- **Skill rule:** Drafting: never write a single-element functional claim; when the point of novelty is a structure, claim that structure, not "means for" its function.

### Al-Site Corp. v. VSI Int'l, Inc., 174 F.3d 1308 (Fed. Cir. 1999) · CB p. 682
- **Facts:** Eyeglass-display hanger patents. One claim recited "fastening means" holding an extension in a loop; the spec showed a rivet or button-and-hole. The accused tag used adhesive. Other claims recited an "eyeglass hanger member" with structure ("made from flat sheet material," "opening means formed ... below [its] upper edge") and an "attaching portion"; the district court treated them as 112(f) too.
- **Holding / rule:** (1) Use of "means" plus a function presumptively invokes 112(f); absence of "means" presumptively does not; either presumption yields to the claim's own structural content. (2) Elements that recite enough structure in the claim are not 112(f) even if they also state a function, and treating them as 112(f) wrongly narrowed them. (3) 112(f) "equivalents" (literal infringement) differ from the doctrine of equivalents: a 112(f) equivalent must be available at issuance and perform the identical function with insubstantially different structure; after-arising technology can only be a DOE equivalent, and DOE requires only substantially the same function. (4) The jury could find glue a 112(f) equivalent of a rivet on expert testimony of known interchangeability in the point-of-purchase industry.
- **Nuance:** A finding of no 112(f) structural equivalent can preclude DOE for the same element (Chiuminatta Concrete Concepts v. Cardinal Indus., 145 F.3d 1303, 1311 (Fed. Cir. 1998)); conversely, where function is identical and the technology is not after-arising, a DOE finding implies a 112(f) equivalent. The teaching point: "fastening means" with only mechanical fasteners disclosed still reached a chemical fastener. Known interchangeability is not shown just because two structures do the same job; it must be known that one substitutes for the other (Chiuminatta).
- **Status:** Good law as of 2026-11-18 on the 112(f)/DOE distinctions and on structure in the claim defeating 112(f). The presumption framework was restated in Williamson v. Citrix (2015) (the presumption against 112(f) without "means" is no longer "strong"; see the Williamson entry below).
- **Skill rule:** Drafting: if you use "means for," list in the spec every known structure that does the function (rivet, adhesive, snap, clip, weld, hook-and-loop), each linked expressly to that function, because the claim reaches only those and their equivalents as of issuance. Charts: chart a 112(f) element in two steps (identify the function exactly as claimed; identify the disclosed structure and ask whether the accused structure is the same or an insubstantially different, known-interchangeable one); put after-arising substitutes in the DOE column, not the literal column.

Note cases on recognizing 112(f) (CB pp. 683–684):
- Laitram Corp. v. Rexnord, Inc., 939 F.2d 1533, 1536 (Fed. Cir. 1991): some structure in a "means" clause does not avoid 112(f) when the structure only further specifies what the means does.
- York Prods. v. Central Tractor Farm & Family Ctr., 99 F.3d 1568 (Fed. Cir. 1996): "means" not linked to any function does not invoke 112(f).
- Greenberg v. Ethicon Endo-Surgery, 91 F.3d 1580, 1583–84 (Fed. Cir. 1996): "means for" generally invokes 112(f); a term named for its function ("detent mechanism," like "brake," "clamp," "filter") can still be structural.
- Cole v. Kimberly-Clark Corp., 102 F.3d 524, 531 (Fed. Cir. 1996): "perforation means ... for tearing" was not 112(f) because the claim recited the structure (perforations), its location, and its extent. Dissent: "perforation" is not enough structure to negate "means."
- **Skill rule:** Drafting: do not sprinkle "means" over structural elements (Cole's drafter used it 14 times); use "means for" only on purpose, and otherwise name the structure.

### Step-plus-function (note material, CB p. 704)
- Seal-Flex, Inc. v. Athletic Track & Court Constr., 172 F.3d 836 (Fed. Cir. 1999) (Rader, J., concurring): "step for" presumptively invokes 112(f); "steps of" does not, because "of" introduces acts and "for" introduces a function. Masco Corp. v. United States, 303 F.3d 1316 (Fed. Cir. 2002), applied that approach.
- **Skill rule:** Drafting: write method elements as gerund acts ("receiving ...; computing ...") after "comprising"; never write "a step for [result]" unless you want 112(f) and have disclosed the acts.

### Aristocrat Techs. Australia Pty Ltd. v. Int'l Game Tech., 521 F.3d 1328 (Fed. Cir. 2008) · CB p. 696
- **Facts:** Slot machine claim with "game control means" performing three functions. The only structure disclosed was "any standard microprocessor base[d] gaming machine" with "appropriate programming." The claim recited a formula for the number of winning arrangements; the spec gave figures and tables of example outcomes.
- **Holding / rule:** For a computer-implemented 112(f) element, the corresponding structure must be a special-purpose computer programmed with a disclosed algorithm, not a general-purpose computer. Disclosing only a computer plus "appropriate programming" is pure functional claiming and leaves the claim indefinite under 112(b). The algorithm can be stated in prose, a flowchart, a formula, or any understandable form; source code is not required.
- **Nuance:** A formula that describes the result of the function is not an algorithm for performing it; worked examples of outcomes are not algorithms. Enablement is not the test: that a skilled programmer could write the code does not supply the missing structure. In re Dossel, 115 F.3d 942 (Fed. Cir. 1997), is distinguished: there the spec gave the specific equations and left only standard mathematical solution techniques to the skilled reader. WMS Gaming v. IGT, 184 F.3d 1339, 1348–49 (Fed. Cir. 1999): the structure is the computer programmed to carry out the disclosed algorithm. Med. Instrumentation & Diagnostics v. Elekta, 344 F.3d 1205, 1211 (Fed. Cir. 2003), and Biomedino v. Waters Techs., 490 F.3d 946, 948 (Fed. Cir. 2007): structure must be clearly linked to the function in the spec.
- **Status:** Good law as of 2026-11-18. Narrow exception: functions any general-purpose computer performs without special programming ("processing," "receiving," "storing") need no algorithm (In re Katz Interactive Voice Response Patent Litig., 639 F.3d 1303, 1316 (Fed. Cir. 2011)), and that exception is read narrowly (Ergo Licensing, LLC v. CareFusion 303, Inc., 673 F.3d 1361, 1364–65 (Fed. Cir. 2012); EON Corp. IP Holdings v. AT&T Mobility, 785 F.3d 616, 621–23 (Fed. Cir. 2015)). After Williamson (2015), the rule reaches non-"means" software terms ("module," "logic," "unit") that a court finds to be 112(f) elements.
- **Skill rule:** Drafting spec: for every function recited in a software claim, write the algorithm in numbered steps (and a flowchart figure) in the spec and tie it to that function by name ("the scheduler performs the allocating function by: (1) ... (2) ..."); never rely on "conventional programming" or "a processor configured to" as the only support. Invalidity/charts: for any software claim term that may be 112(f) (Williamson), search the spec for an algorithm tied to each recited function; if there is none, flag a 112(b) indefiniteness ground.

### Williamson v. Citrix Online, LLC, 792 F.3d 1339 (Fed. Cir. 2015) (en banc in part) · not in casebook (decided after the 5th ed. text)
- **Facts:** Claim recited a "distributed learning control module" for receiving, relaying, and coordinating communications in an online classroom. No "means."
- **Holding / rule:** The en banc court dropped the "strong" presumption that a term without "means" escapes 112(f). The test is whether a skilled person reads the claim words as having a sufficiently definite meaning as the name for structure. The presumption against 112(f) is overcome if the term fails to recite sufficiently definite structure or recites function without enough structure to perform it. "Module" is a well-known nonce word, like "means"; the modifier "distributed learning control" and the claim's description of inputs and outputs did not supply internal structure. The spec disclosed no algorithm for the coordinating function, so the claim was indefinite (Aristocrat).
- **Nuance:** The en banc part addressed only the presumption; the rest was panel. Newman dissented on the en banc point. Terms that a skilled person understands as structure survive: "program" and "user interface code" (Zeroclick, LLC v. Apple Inc., 891 F.3d 1003 (Fed. Cir. 2018)); "code" and "application" described by how they operate (Dyfan, LLC v. Target Corp., 28 F.4th 1360 (Fed. Cir. 2022)). Terms held nonce after Williamson: "logic" (Egenera, Inc. v. Cisco Sys., Inc., 972 F.3d 1367 (Fed. Cir. 2020)); "user identification module" (Rain Computing, Inc. v. Samsung Elecs. Am., Inc., 989 F.3d 1002 (Fed. Cir. 2021)); "payment handler" (Fintiv, Inc. v. PayPal Holdings, Inc., No. 2023-2312 (Fed. Cir. Apr. 30, 2025)); "selecting unit" (Optis Cellular Tech., LLC v. Apple Inc., Nos. 2022-1904, -1925 (Fed. Cir. June 16, 2025)).
- **Status:** Good law as of 2026-11-18; the USPTO examines computer-implemented functional language under it (MPEP 2181; Examining Computer-Implemented Functional Claim Limitations for Compliance with 35 U.S.C. 112, 84 Fed. Reg. 57 (Jan. 7, 2019)).
- **Skill rule:** Drafting: never use "module," "unit," "logic," "handler," "component," "element," "mechanism," or "device" + "configured to [function]" as the only description of a software element. Either (a) recite structure in the claim ("a processor and memory storing instructions that, when executed, cause the processor to: [step]; [step]") or (b) accept 112(f) on purpose and disclose a step-by-step algorithm for every recited function. `check_claims.py` should WARN on each nonce word + function pair. Invalidity/charts: run the Williamson two-step on every such term (is it a nonce word? if so, what is the function, and is an algorithm for that exact function disclosed and linked?).

### Jepson claims (note material, CB pp. 705–706)
- Form: "In a [known combination A, B, C], the improvement comprising [C']." 37 CFR 1.75(e) authorizes it. The preamble is impliedly admitted to be prior art (In re Pfeiffer, cited in the casebook; MPEP 2129), so the examiner starts from A+B+C as old and need show only that C' was obvious. The preamble also limits the claim (Rowe v. Dror, 112 F.3d 473, 479 (Fed. Cir. 1997)). The admission is rebuttable where the preamble describes the inventor's own earlier work that is not statutory prior art (Reading & Bates Constr. Co. v. Baker Energy Res. Corp., 748 F.2d 645 (Fed. Cir. 1984)). Example claim: Pharmacia & Upjohn Co. v. Mylan Pharms., Inc., 170 F.3d 1373 (Fed. Cir. 1999).
- **Status:** Good law as of 2026-11-18 (MPEP 2129 III, 608.01(m)).
- **Skill rule:** Drafting: do not use Jepson form by default; it concedes the preamble as prior art for 102/103 and for every later litigation. Use it only when the user asks or a foreign-origin claim set requires it, and then never put the inventor's own unpublished or grace-period work in the preamble. Prosecution: if an examiner asks to convert a claim to Jepson form, refuse unless the preamble elements are truly in the cited art.

### Markush groups (note material, CB pp. 706)
- Form: "wherein X is selected from the group consisting of A, B, and C." Traditionally proper when the members share a common structure or class and a common use (In re Harnisch, 631 F.2d 716 (CCPA 1980); MPEP 2117). A large Markush genus in a prior publication can be cited against a later selection of one member or a sub-genus.
- Related rules (not in the casebook excerpt): a claim covering alternatives is anticipated if any one alternative is in the prior art (Brown v. 3M, 265 F.3d 1349, 1351 (Fed. Cir. 2001)); a "selected from the group consisting of" Markush group is under a very strong presumption of being closed to unlisted members, but it is not necessarily closed to blends of the listed members, especially where dependent claims recite blends (Multilayer Stretch Cling Film Holdings, Inc. v. Berry Plastics Corp., 831 F.3d 1350 (Fed. Cir. 2016)).
- **Status:** Good law as of 2026-11-18; USPTO may reject an "improper Markush grouping" when members lack a single structural similarity and common use (MPEP 2117 II).
- **Skill rule:** Drafting: write Markush groups as "selected from the group consisting of A, B, C, and combinations thereof" when mixtures should be covered; put each member in its own dependent claim as well, so one anticipated member does not take the others down; never put "comprising" inside the group. Charts: for a Markush element, the accused product meets it literally only if its component is one of the listed members or a blend of them; an unlisted member goes to the DOE column, where the closed format weighs against equivalence.

## 4. Definiteness (35 U.S.C. 112(b))

### Orthokinetics, Inc. v. Safety Travel Chairs, Inc., 806 F.2d 1565 (Fed. Cir. 1986) · CB p. 707
- **Facts:** Jepson-form wheelchair claim: the front leg portion "so dimensioned as to be insertable through the space between the doorframe of an automobile and one of the seats thereof." The district court granted JNOV for indefiniteness because the right size depended on the car model.
- **Holding / rule:** A claim is definite if those skilled in the art would understand what is claimed when it is read in light of the spec. "So dimensioned" was as precise as the subject matter permits; a skilled person could measure the target car and size the legs. The patent need not list dimensions for every car.
- **Nuance:** The district court erred twice: it required the claim to "describe" the invention (the spec's job) and applied 112(a)'s "full, clear, concise, and exact" language to claims. Fact that flips it: if the skilled reader could not tell from the spec what measurement or what reference car governs, or if the term was used to dodge the art, the functional-dimension phrasing fails.
- **Status:** Good law in result, but the governing standard is now Nautilus "reasonable certainty" (2014). Courts still cite Orthokinetics for claims tied to a variable environment.
- **Skill rule:** Drafting: a dimension defined by fit to an external object ("sized to fit through ...") is acceptable only if the spec tells the reader how to measure the object and gives at least one worked numeric example; add a dependent claim with a numeric range.

### Terms of degree (note cases, CB pp. 711–712)
- Andrew Corp. v. Gabriel Elecs., 847 F.2d 819 (Fed. Cir. 1988): "substantially," "closely approximate," and similar terms are common and acceptable when they reasonably describe the subject matter and distinguish the art.
- Seattle Box Co. v. Indus. Crating & Packing, 731 F.2d 818 (Fed. Cir. 1984): for a term of degree, ask whether the spec provides "some standard for measuring that degree."
- Amgen Inc. v. Chugai Pharm. Co., 927 F.2d 1200 (Fed. Cir. 1991): "at least about 160,000" IU/AU was indefinite where the art was near 120,000–128,620, the assay was imprecise, and nothing (spec, file history, art, or expert) said how far "about" reached; the word looked like an attempt to recapture ground between the art and the allowed number. The court said "about" is not always fatal (W.L. Gore & Assocs. v. Garlock, Inc., 721 F.2d 1540, 1557 (Fed. Cir. 1983): "at a rate exceeding about 10% per second" was definite).
- Pre-Nautilus approvals: "substantially completely wetted" (LNP Eng'g Plastics v. Miller Waste Mills, 275 F.3d 1347 (Fed. Cir. 2001)); "sufficient to increase substantially the initial catalyst activity" (Exxon Research & Eng'g v. United States, 265 F.3d 1371 (Fed. Cir. 2001)); concrete "hardened sufficiently to allow cutting" (Chiuminatta, 145 F.3d at 1306). Treat these as weaker authority now, because Exxon's "insolubly ambiguous" test was rejected in Nautilus.
- **Skill rule:** Drafting: every "about," "substantially," or "sufficient to" in a claim needs a spec sentence giving the measuring standard (for example, "about" means within ±5% or within the measurement error of [named test]), and a dependent claim with the hard number. Never put "about" on a number that sits close to the prior-art value; claim the number the data support and state the test method.

### Nautilus, Inc. v. Biosig Instruments, Inc., 572 U.S. 898, 134 S. Ct. 2120 (2014) · CB p. 712
- **Facts:** Heart-rate monitor claim: live and common electrodes on each half of a bar "mounted ... in spaced relationship with each other." The Federal Circuit had upheld the claim as not "insolubly ambiguous."
- **Holding / rule:** A claim is indefinite under 112(b) if, read in light of the spec and prosecution history, it fails to "inform, with reasonable certainty, those skilled in the art about the scope of the invention." Measured from the skilled artisan's view at the time of filing. The Federal Circuit's "amenable to construction" and "insolubly ambiguous" tests are too lenient. Remanded without deciding the claim.
- **Nuance:** The Court balanced the limits of language (some uncertainty is the price of incentives) against the public-notice function and the drafter's incentive to be vague; the drafter is best placed to fix ambiguity. That a court can assign some meaning is not enough. The Court did not decide the presumption-of-validity/clear-and-convincing interplay or the standard of review.
- **Status:** Good law as of 2026-11-18. On remand the claims were held definite under the new standard (Biosig Instruments, Inc. v. Nautilus, Inc., 783 F.3d 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015)). The USPTO applies a lower threshold during examination: a claim is indefinite if it contains words or phrases whose meaning is unclear (In re Packard, 751 F.3d 1307 (Fed. Cir. 2014); Ex parte McAward, Appeal No. 2015-006416 (PTAB Aug. 25, 2017) (precedential)). In AIA trials the Board applies Nautilus (USPTO Director memorandum, "Approach to indefiniteness in AIA post-grant proceedings," Jan. 6, 2021); Packard still governs ex parte examination and appeals.
- **Skill rule:** Drafting: for each claim term, ask "could two skilled readers draw different boundaries?" If yes, define it in the spec with an objective test, a numeric range, or worked examples on both sides of the line. Invalidity: test every term of degree, every functional phrase, and every subjective term ("aesthetically pleasing," "unobtrusive") against the reasonable-certainty standard and look for competing measurement methods that give different answers. Prosecution: answer a 112(b) rejection by amending or by pointing to the spec standard; don't argue that the term is "amenable to construction."

### "Whereby" clauses (note cases, CB pp. 720–721)
- Texas Instruments Inc. v. ITC, 988 F.2d 1165, 1172 (Fed. Cir. 1993): a "whereby" or "to preclude" clause that only states the result of the recited structure adds no limitation. Contrast Hoffer v. Microsoft Corp., 405 F.3d 1326, 1329 (Fed. Cir. 2005) (per curiam): a whereby clause that states a condition material to patentability cannot be ignored (there, it required interactive data messaging and defeated infringement); Minton v. NASD, 336 F.3d 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2003): a whereby clause stating only the intended result of a method step does not limit.
- **Skill rule:** Drafting: never rely on a "whereby" or "such that" clause to distinguish the art; recite the structure or step that produces the result. Prosecution: if you argued a whereby result to get allowance, treat it as a limitation in later charts.

## 5. Claim construction: who decides, from what, under what review

### Autogiro Co. of America v. United States, 384 F.2d 391 (Ct. Cl. 1967) · CB p. 725
- **Facts:** Government use of helicopter rotor technology; the excerpt is the court's general statement of how claims are read.
- **Holding / rule:** Courts interpret claims; they do not rework them, broaden them, or narrow them for fairness. No claim is clear on its face in the abstract, so interpretation always uses the rest of the patent: the specification (a "concordance" for the claims, since words must be used the same way in both), the drawings (same role and limits as the spec), and the file wrapper (the applicant's statements to get the patent bind it later, and cited art shows what the claims do not cover).
- **Nuance:** Autogiro distrusts canons of construction as "major canons of minor value" and aims at the claim's "felt meaning." It anticipates the modern intrinsic-evidence hierarchy and prosecution disclaimer. Its doctrine-of-equivalents discussion (not excerpted here) is governed today by Warner-Jenkinson and Festo (see the infringement file).
- **Status:** Good law on interpretation sources as of 2026-11-18; Court of Claims decisions bind the Federal Circuit (South Corp. v. United States, 690 F.2d 1368 (Fed. Cir. 1982) (en banc)). Phillips adopts its "concordance" framing.
- **Skill rule:** Analysis/charts: read every claim term against the spec, the figures, and the full file history before charting; record where the file history narrows a term. Drafting: use each claim term in the spec with the same meaning every time, because the spec is the dictionary.

### Markman v. Westview Instruments, Inc., 517 U.S. 370 (1996) · CB p. 731
- **Facts:** Dry-cleaning inventory patent; the dispute turned on whether "inventory" meant articles of clothing or also cash receivables. The jury found infringement; the judge construed the term and granted JMOL.
- **Holding / rule:** Construction of a patent claim, including terms of art, is exclusively for the court, not the jury. The Seventh Amendment does not require a jury because there was no 1791 practice of juries construing patent terms; judges are better at construing documents, credibility questions get absorbed into reading the whole document, and uniformity (stare decisis across defendants) favors judges. Applying the construed claim to the accused product (infringement) remains a jury question.
- **Nuance:** The Court called construction a "mongrel practice" with "evidentiary underpinnings," and did not say it was purely legal; the Federal Circuit's en banc opinion (52 F.3d 967 (Fed. Cir. 1995)) had called it pure law. That gap produced Cybor Corp. v. FAS Techs., 138 F.3d 1448 (Fed. Cir. 1998) (en banc) (de novo review of everything), later corrected by Teva. The court must resolve a real dispute about scope rather than leave it to the jury (O2 Micro Int'l Ltd. v. Beyond Innovation Tech. Co., 521 F.3d 1351, 1362 (Fed. Cir. 2008)).
- **Status:** Good law as of 2026-11-18; standard of review modified by Teva (2015).
- **Skill rule:** Charts and FTO: build an explicit "construction" column for every disputed term with the proposed meaning and its intrinsic support before marking any element met; never let the infringement column silently resolve a scope dispute. When a court has construed the term in earlier litigation, fetch the order and cite it, noting it binds only the parties but persuades other courts.

Examination standard (note material, CB pp. 728–729): In re Zletz, 893 F.2d 319, 321–22 (Fed. Cir. 1989): during prosecution, claims get their broadest reasonable interpretation consistent with the spec, because the applicant can amend. **Skill rule:** Prosecution: argue against a rejection under BRI (MPEP 2111), not under litigation-style Phillips construction; if the examiner's reading is reasonable, amend the claim rather than argue that a court would read it narrowly.

### Teva Pharms. USA, Inc. v. Sandoz, Inc., 574 U.S. 318, 135 S. Ct. 831 (2015) · CB p. 743
- **Facts:** Claim to copolymer-1 with "a molecular weight of 5 to 9 kilodaltons." "Molecular weight" can mean peak average, number average, or weight average. The district court credited Teva's expert on why a figure's peak appeared shifted and held the claim definite; the Federal Circuit reviewed that de novo and held it indefinite.
- **Holding / rule:** On appeal, a district court's subsidiary factual findings about extrinsic evidence made during claim construction (for example, what a term of art meant to skilled artisans at the time, or how a skilled reader would understand a chromatogram) are reviewed for clear error under Rule 52(a)(6). The ultimate construction is still a legal conclusion reviewed de novo. Where the court relied only on intrinsic evidence (claims, spec, prosecution history), review is entirely de novo.
- **Nuance:** A factual finding can be "close to dispositive" and still be reviewed only for clear error. Breyer analogized to voluntariness of a confession. Thomas and Alito dissented, arguing claim construction is like statutory interpretation. Practical effect is modest: on remand the Federal Circuit again held the claim indefinite, relying on the prosecution history (Teva Pharms. USA, Inc. v. Sandoz, Inc., 789 F.3d 1335 (Fed. Cir. 2015)). Teva superseded the de novo-for-everything rule of Cybor and Lighting Ballast Control LLC v. Philips Elecs. N. Am. Corp., 744 F.3d 1272 (Fed. Cir. 2014) (en banc).
- **Status:** Good law as of 2026-11-18.
- **Skill rule:** Drafting: when a claimed parameter has more than one accepted measurement or calculation method, name the method in the claim or define it in the spec ("molecular weight means peak average molecular weight measured by SEC-MALLS as described in Example 2"); never leave it to expert testimony. Charts: tag each construction as `intrinsic` or `extrinsic-dependent`; flag the extrinsic-dependent ones as higher-risk because the outcome turns on which expert a judge believes.

### Phillips v. AWH Corp., 415 F.3d 1303 (Fed. Cir. 2005) (en banc) · CB p. 752
- **Facts:** Claim to vandal-resistant building modules with "further means disposed inside the shell for increasing its load bearing capacity comprising internal steel baffles extending inwardly from the steel shell walls." Every figure and description showed baffles at oblique angles; the panel had limited "baffles" to non-perpendicular angles. The accused panels had baffles at 90 degrees.
- **Holding / rule:** (1) "Baffles" is structural, so 112(f) does not apply even though the phrase begins with "means ... for," because the claim itself recites the structure. (2) Claim words take their ordinary and customary meaning to a skilled person at the effective filing date, read in the context of the whole patent. (3) Source hierarchy: the claims themselves (context: "steel baffles" implies baffles need not be steel; other claims, including the presumption that a dependent claim's added limitation is absent from the independent claim); the spec, "the single best guide" and "usually dispositive," including lexicography and disavowal; the prosecution history (intrinsic, but less clear because it records a negotiation); then extrinsic evidence (dictionaries, treatises, experts), which is less reliable and cannot contradict the intrinsic record. (4) Texas Digital's dictionary-first method is rejected. (5) Do not confine claims to the embodiments, even a single one. A patent that names several objectives does not require every claim to achieve all of them. (6) Construing claims to preserve validity is a last resort for claims still ambiguous after all other tools. Result: "baffles" covers inward structures at any angle, including 90 degrees; dependent claims reciting angled, projectile-deflecting, or interlocking baffles confirm it.
- **Nuance:** No rigid order of sources is required, so long as extrinsic evidence is not used to contradict clear intrinsic meaning. Lourie (with Newman) would have affirmed because the spec described angled baffles as the invention. Mayer (with Newman) dissented that construction rests on facts and deserves deference, a view Teva partly vindicated. Fact that would flip it: a spec saying "the baffles of the present invention are oriented at an acute angle" or disparaging perpendicular baffles (disavowal).
- **Status:** Good law as of 2026-11-18. The PTAB has applied the Phillips standard in IPR, PGR, and CBM trials for petitions filed on or after Nov. 13, 2018 (83 Fed. Reg. 51,340 (Oct. 11, 2018); 37 CFR 42.100(b), 42.200(b)); before that the Board used BRI (approved in Cuozzo Speed Techs., LLC v. Lee, 579 U.S. 261 (2016)). Examination still uses BRI.
- **Skill rule:** Charts/analysis: construe in this order and record the source for each term: claim language, other claims (list every dependent that adds to the term), spec (definitions, "present invention" statements, disparagement), file history (arguments and amendments), then dictionaries or expert views, which may only confirm. Drafting: describe several embodiments of every key element (at least one that differs in the way a competitor would vary it), and write dependents that recite each specific embodiment so the independent claim is presumed broader.

Note cases on Phillips (CB pp. 755–757):
- Chef America, Inc. v. Lamb-Weston, Inc., 358 F.3d 1371, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2004): "heating the ... dough to a temperature in the range of about 400°F to 850°F" means the dough itself, even though that burns it; courts will not redraft an unambiguous claim to avoid a nonsensical result. **Skill rule:** Drafting: read every parameter clause literally for what object it modifies ("heating the dough in an oven at," not "heating the dough to").
- North American Vaccine, Inc. v. American Cyanamid Co., 7 F.3d 1571, 1575–76 (Fed. Cir. 1993) (read "a terminal portion" as singular), versus the general rule that "a"/"an" in a "comprising" claim means one or more (KCJ Corp. v. Kinetic Concepts, Inc., 223 F.3d 1351, 1356 (Fed. Cir. 2000); Electro Med. Sys. v. Cooper Life Scis., 34 F.3d 1048, 1054 (Fed. Cir. 1994)). **Skill rule:** Drafting: write "at least one" or "one or more" when plurality matters, and avoid spec language implying exactly one.
- Vitronics Corp. v. Conceptronic, Inc., 90 F.3d 1576 (Fed. Cir. 1996) (intrinsic evidence first; a construction excluding the preferred embodiment is rarely correct); Pitney Bowes v. Hewlett-Packard, 182 F.3d 1298 (Fed. Cir. 1999) (expert testimony allowed if it does not contradict the intrinsic record). Phillips: conclusory expert assertions on meaning are not useful.
- Zenith Labs. v. Bristol-Myers Squibb Co., 19 F.3d 1418 (Fed. Cir. 1994) (CB p. 752): compare the accused product to the claims, never to the patentee's commercial product. **Skill rule:** Charts: the left column is always claim language, never the patentee's product.

## 6. Departing from plain meaning: lexicography, disavowal, claim differentiation

### Hill-Rom Servs., Inc. v. Stryker Corp., 755 F.3d 1367 (Fed. Cir. 2014) (Moore, J.; Reyna, J., dissenting) · supplemental opinion (not a casebook principal case)
- **Facts:** Hospital-bed status patents (U.S. 5,699,038 and two continuations). The district court construed "datalink" as a cable, required the "interface board including a processor" to send through a wall interface unit and to receive messages, limited "message validation information" to one field verifying exact receipt, and limited "bed condition message" to unrequested messages reporting all conditions (by judicial estoppel from statements in a later, unrelated patent's prosecution). The only embodiment used a cable; the spec called the same part "datalink 39," "cable 39," and "serial datalink 39."
- **Holding / rule:** Claim terms get their plain meaning to a skilled person at the effective filing date; a court departs from it only for (1) lexicography (the patentee clearly sets out a different definition and intends to redefine) or (2) disavowal (spec or prosecution history makes clear the invention excludes a feature or is limited to a form). Both standards are "exacting." No magic words are required, but there must be clear language of limitation. "Datalink" means any link that carries data, wired or wireless. The interface board need only do what each claim says. Message validation information may span several fields and need not catch every error. "Bed condition message" carries its plain meaning.
- **Nuance:** What counts as disavowal (from the cases Hill-Rom collects): "the present invention requires/is/includes," "all embodiments of the present invention are" (Regents of Univ. of Minn. v. AGA Med., 717 F.3d 929, 936 (Fed. Cir. 2013); SciMed Life Sys. v. Advanced Cardiovascular Sys., 242 F.3d 1337, 1343–44 (Fed. Cir. 2001)); calling a step "required" for success (Andersen Corp. v. Fiber Composites, 474 F.3d 1361, 1367 (Fed. Cir. 2007)); labeling a feature "an important feature of the present invention" while disparaging the alternative (SafeTCare Mfg. v. Tele-Made, 497 F.3d 1262, 1269–70 (Fed. Cir. 2007); Inpro II Licensing v. T-Mobile, 450 F.3d 1350, 1354–55 (Fed. Cir. 2006)); repeated derogatory statements about an alternative (Chicago Bd. Options Exch. v. Int'l Sec. Exch., 677 F.3d 1361, 1372 (Fed. Cir. 2012)). What does not: a single embodiment; synonym use limited to describing one numbered part; one embodiment described as "in accordance with the present invention"; boilerplate saying figures "illustrate embodiments" and do not limit the claims. Other rules applied: claim differentiation from dependents reciting a "wired datalink," strongest when that is the only difference; enablement worries do not justify narrowing (courts "do not rewrite the claim to preserve its validity," and construction is not a validity mini-trial); defining a term by its function does not trigger 112(f) (Greenberg); an examiner's remark in a later unrelated application about what the patent "does not teach" says nothing about claim meaning; statements in a later unrelated patent's prosecution cannot narrow the claims or support judicial estoppel (Pfizer, Inc. v. Ranbaxy Labs., 457 F.3d 1284, 1290 (Fed. Cir. 2006)); the spec need not teach what is well known (wireless links were known and even described in the background). Dissent (Reyna): the intrinsic record treats the datalink as physical structure (serial, cable, Fig. 1, "electrically couple," claim 12's optical isolator "in line" with it); Hill-Rom's own later wireless application described these patents as wired; the expert spoke in the present tense, not as of 1993; the majority's purely functional reading covers future technology.
- **Status:** Good law as of 2026-11-18. Applied on both sides since: no disavowal where the spec merely preferred and contrasted a method (Continental Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788 (Fed. Cir. 2019)); disavowal where the spec consistently presented the invention as putting the detector in the wall console and criticized the alternative, without needing to call it impossible (Techtronic Indus. Co. v. ITC, 944 F.3d 901 (Fed. Cir. 2019)).
- **Skill rule:** Drafting spec: never write "the present invention is/requires/includes," "essential," "critical," "important feature of the invention," or disparaging comparisons of alternatives; write "in some embodiments" and list the alternatives (wired, wireless, optical) for every structural noun; if you want a special meaning, define it expressly ("As used herein, 'datalink' means ..."). Charts/FTO/invalidity: for each disputed term, list (a) every lexicography candidate, (b) every disavowal candidate quoted with its location, and (c) every dependent claim adding to the term; mark the term narrowed only if (a) or (b) meets the exacting standard. Prosecution: avoid characterizing "the invention" in remarks; distinguish art on claim language, because remarks in the family's prosecution and in IPR can disclaim scope.

### Thorner v. Sony Computer Entm't Am. LLC, 669 F.3d 1362 (Fed. Cir. 2012) · not in casebook excerpt (relied on in Hill-Rom)
- **Rule (as quoted and applied in Hill-Rom, 755 F.3d at 1371):** Only two exceptions to plain meaning: lexicography and disavowal, in the spec or during prosecution. Lexicography requires clearly setting out a definition other than the plain meaning and clearly expressing intent to redefine. A patentee may choose a broad term and get its full plain meaning; describing a single embodiment, or using a word the same way in every embodiment, is not enough to narrow it.
- **Status:** Good law as of 2026-11-18.
- **Skill rule:** Charts: an argument to narrow a term that rests only on "the spec always shows X" is weak; require a quote meeting Thorner before adopting it. Drafting: choose broad generic nouns in claims and keep the narrow ones in dependents.

### Claim differentiation (Phillips, Hill-Rom, and related cases)
- **Rule:** A dependent claim that adds a limitation raises a presumption that the independent claim does not have it (Phillips, 415 F.3d at 1314–15; Liebel-Flarsheim Co. v. Medrad, Inc., 358 F.3d 898, 910 (Fed. Cir. 2004)). The presumption is strongest when that limitation is the only meaningful difference between the two claims (Hill-Rom). It can be overcome by the spec or prosecution history (Seachange Int'l v. C-COR, Inc., 413 F.3d 1361, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2005)), and it cannot broaden a claim past what the spec supports (Retractable Techs., Inc. v. Becton, Dickinson & Co., 653 F.3d 1296, 1305 (Fed. Cir. 2011) ("body" limited to a one-piece body despite a dependent claim)). It is weaker between independent claims that use different words. It works across continuations that share a spec (Hill-Rom used the '659 continuation's dependents to construe the parent family's term).
- **Skill rule:** Drafting: for every narrow embodiment of a key term (wired, one-piece, detachable, serial), add a dependent claim reciting it, so the independent claim is presumed to cover the alternatives; keep each such dependent to that one difference. Charts: list every dependent that narrows the disputed term and argue the presumption; then check the spec for statements that could rebut it (Retractable).

### Other cases mentioned in the chapter (string cites, grouped)
Pennwalt v. Durand-Wayland, 833 F.2d 931 (Fed. Cir. 1987) (history of claim form); Ex parte Rice (1874) and National Tube Co. v. Mark, 216 F. 507 (6th Cir. 1914) ("substantially as described" adds nothing); Ex parte Canter, 70 U.S.P.Q. 372 (1946) (trademark in claim after spec describes the product); judicial gloss on "integral," "extrinsic," "approximately," "horizontal," "solid" (Advanced Cardiovascular, Nobell, Borg-Warner, In re Gordon, Standard Oil v. ### Prosecution disclaimer (cross-reference)
- Statements to the USPTO can narrow claims only if clear and unmistakable (Omega Eng'g, Inc. v. Raytek Corp., 334 F.3d 1314, 1325–26 (Fed. Cir. 2003)). Patent-owner statements in an IPR, including a preliminary response before institution, count (Aylus Networks, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 856 F.3d 1353 (Fed. Cir. 2017)). Statements in a related application's prosecution about a shared term carry over (Microsoft Corp. v. Multi-Tech Sys., Inc., 357 F.3d 1340, 1350 (Fed. Cir. 2004)); statements in a later unrelated application do not (Hill-Rom; Pfizer v. Ranbaxy). Computer Docking Station v. Dell and prosecution history estoppel are covered in the infringement file.
- **Skill rule:** Prosecution and IPR: before filing any argument, write down the narrowest reading a court could take from it and confirm the client accepts that scope for the whole family. Charts: pull the file history of the patent, its parents, its continuations, and any IPR before construing.

## Edge-case playbook

1. **"A processing module configured to compute the score."** Trap: no "means," so no 112(f). Correct: "module" is a nonce word; under Williamson it is a 112(f) element, and with only a general-purpose computer in the spec the claim is indefinite (Aristocrat). Recent holdings reach "logic," "unit," and "handler" (Egenera, Optis, Fintiv). Fix in drafting: recite processor + memory + steps, or disclose an algorithm for each function. Cases: Williamson; Aristocrat; Rain Computing.
2. **"Means for fastening" when the spec shows only rivets, and the competitor uses glue.** Trap: either "means covers every fastener" or "only rivets." Correct: literal scope reaches disclosed structures plus 112(f) equivalents, meaning known interchangeable substitutes as of issuance with identical function; glue qualified on expert testimony. A post-issuance fastener can only be a DOE equivalent. Cases: Al-Site; Chiuminatta.
3. **"Means" used with full structure ("perforation means extending from the leg band to the waist band ... for tearing").** Trap: "means" always triggers 112(f). Correct: enough structure in the claim rebuts the presumption, and treating it as 112(f) wrongly narrows it. Cases: Cole; Al-Site; Phillips ("means ... comprising internal steel baffles").
4. **Preamble says "for use in a retail store"; the accused system works at home.** Trap: no infringement because the preamble isn't met. Correct: intended use in a preamble of a structurally complete apparatus claim does not limit unless the body relies on it for antecedent basis, it was relied on to overcome art, or it is Jepson form; if the same phrase is in the body, it limits. Cases: Catalina; Pacing; Rowe.
5. **Identical crystalline compound made by a different process, against an "obtainable by process Y" claim.** Trap: same product, so infringement. Correct: no literal infringement because the process steps limit; but for validity, the claim is judged as a product, so an old product made by a new process is unpatentable as a product. Cases: Abbott v. Sandoz (en banc); In re Thorpe.
6. **The only embodiment uses a cable; the claim says "datalink."** Trap: read in "cable" because that's all the spec shows. Correct: no narrowing without lexicography or clear disavowal; dependents reciting "wired" confirm breadth; enablement worries don't narrow the claim. Flip: "the present invention requires a wired connection," or disparaging wireless links. Cases: Hill-Rom; Thorner; Techtronic (flip); SciMed (flip).
7. **Dependent claim 2 adds "detachable"; the defendant says claim 1 also requires detachability because the abstract stresses replaceable soles.** Trap: decide on the abstract alone. Correct: claim differentiation creates a presumption that claim 1 is broader; overcome only by clear spec or prosecution statements (Retractable shows that can happen). Chart both readings and mark `construction_dependent`. Cases: Phillips; Hill-Rom; Retractable. (Worked example: '126 patent claims 1 vs. 10, 14, 19.)
8. **"At least about 160,000 IU" where the art showed 128,620.** Trap: "about" is always fine. Correct: a term of degree near the prior-art boundary, with an imprecise assay and nothing saying how far "about" reaches, is indefinite. Fix: claim the supported number, define "about" in the spec, name the test. Cases: Amgen v. Chugai; W.L. Gore (contrast).
9. **A claimed parameter ("molecular weight," "slope of strain hardening") has several accepted measurement methods giving different results, and the patent doesn't pick one.** Trap: the expert will explain which. Correct: the claim fails reasonable certainty if the intrinsic record doesn't choose. Cases: Nautilus; Teva (remand); Dow Chem. v. Nova Chems., 803 F.3d 620 (Fed. Cir. 2015).
10. **"Unobtrusive," "aesthetically pleasing," "easy to use" in a claim.** Trap: courts tolerate words of degree. Correct: purely subjective terms with no objective spec standard are indefinite (Interval Licensing LLC v. AOL, Inc., 766 F.3d 1364 (Fed. Cir. 2014)); terms of degree with spec definitions or examples survive (Niazi Licensing Corp. v. St. Jude Med. S.C., Inc., 30 F.4th 1339 (Fed. Cir. 2022); Guangdong Alison Hi-Tech Co. v. ITC, 936 F.3d 1353 (Fed. Cir. 2019)).
11. **"A microprocessor ... said microprocessor configured to A, B, and C"; the accused phone splits A, B, C across three chips.** Trap: "a" means one or more, so three chips infringe. Correct: "a ... said" requires at least one processor able to perform all the recited functions. Fix: "one or more processors configured to, individually or collectively, ..." Case: Salazar v. AT&T Mobility LLC, 64 F.4th 1311 (Fed. Cir. 2023); KCJ (general rule).
12. **Obvious drafting slip ("heating the dough to 400–850°F").** Trap: the court will read it as intended. Correct: courts do not redraft unambiguous claims, even to avoid absurd results. A certificate of correction under 35 U.S.C. 255 helps only going forward. Fix: proofread every parameter clause for the object it modifies. Case: Chef America.
13. **Applicant's statements in different proceedings.** Trap: treat every statement the same. Correct: remarks in the patent's own prosecution and in its family members can disclaim scope if clear and unmistakable; patent-owner statements in IPR count, including preliminary responses; statements in a later unrelated patent's prosecution do not narrow the claims or support judicial estoppel. Cases: Omega; Microsoft v. Multi-Tech; Aylus; Hill-Rom; Pfizer v. Ranbaxy.
14. **Construe narrowly to dodge prior art.** Trap: the invalidity reviewer (or the patentee) narrows a term to save validity. Correct: the validity canon applies only if the term is still ambiguous after all construction tools; claims are construed the same way for infringement and validity, so a broad reading that catches the product also catches the art. Cases: Phillips; Hill-Rom.
15. **"A system ... wherein the user selects the option."** Trap: a system claim can recite user actions. Correct: a single claim covering both an apparatus and a method of using it is indefinite (IPXL Holdings, L.L.C. v. Amazon.com, Inc., 430 F.3d 1377, 1384 (Fed. Cir. 2005)); capability language ("configured to receive a user selection") is fine. Fix: claim what the system does or is configured to do, and put user steps in a method claim.
16. **"Consisting of A, B, C"; the competitor adds a trace stabilizer.** Trap: a minor additive still infringes. Correct: a closed transition is avoided by any extra element (DOE aside); "consisting essentially of" lets in only additions that don't materially affect the basic and novel characteristics; a "consisting of" Markush group is closed to unlisted members but may cover blends of listed ones. Cases: transitions notes; Multilayer Stretch Cling Film.
17. **Jepson claim drafted over the inventor's own unpublished earlier design.** Trap: harmless format. Correct: the preamble is an implied admission of prior art; the admission is rebuttable only for the inventor's own work that is not statutory prior art, and it is still a gift to the examiner and the defendant. Cases: Reading & Bates; In re Pfeiffer; MPEP 2129.
18. **"Whereby the fluid will not engage the device."** Trap: the result clause distinguishes the art. Correct: a whereby clause stating only the result of recited structure adds nothing; it limits if it states a condition material to patentability or was relied on for allowance. Cases: Texas Instruments v. ITC; Hoffer; Minton.
19. **Same claim, three forums.** Trap: one construction standard and one indefiniteness test everywhere. Correct: examination uses BRI and the Packard "unclear meaning" indefiniteness test; AIA trials (petitions from Nov. 13, 2018) use Phillips, and since Jan. 6, 2021 Nautilus for indefiniteness; district courts use Phillips and Nautilus. An examiner's broad reading is not a court's reading. Cases: Zletz; Packard; McAward; 83 Fed. Reg. 51,340; Phillips; Nautilus.
20. **"Claim 1 reads on the patentee's commercial product, and the accused product is like that product."** Trap: compare products. Correct: compare the accused product to the claim language only. Case: Zenith Labs. v. Bristol-Myers Squibb.
21. **Spec says "known algorithms can be used" for a 112(f) software function.** Trap: equal to Aristocrat's "appropriate programming." Correct: acceptable only when the spec gives the specific equations or steps and leaves only standard math to the skilled reader (Dossel); "appropriate programming" or results-only examples fail; the no-algorithm exception for functions any general-purpose computer performs ("receiving," "storing") is narrow. Cases: Aristocrat; In re Dossel; In re Katz; Ergo Licensing.
22. **Claim term coined in the 1990s now covers technology that arrived later (wireless, cloud).** Trap: meaning floats with time, or is frozen at the embodiment. Correct: plain meaning is fixed at the effective filing date; it covers later implementations if they fall within that meaning (wireless links were known in 1993, so "datalink" covered them); a 112(f) equivalent must have existed at issuance, while later substitutes go to DOE. Cases: Hill-Rom (majority and dissent); Al-Site.
23. **"Step for determining the location."** Trap: just a method step. Correct: "step for" plus a function presumptively invokes 112(f) and needs disclosed acts; "steps of" introducing acts does not. Cases: Seal-Flex (Rader, J., concurring); Masco.
24. **Omnibus claim "A widget as shown in FIG. 3" in a utility application.** Trap: a narrow picture claim is safe. Correct: indefinite under 112(b) in utility practice; allowed only in design patents. Case: Ex parte Fressola.
25. **A preamble partly needed for antecedent basis and partly intended use.** Trap: all-or-nothing. Correct: one part can limit while another does not when they separate cleanly (TomTom); when they don't separate, treat the preamble as a whole (Bio-Rad). Chart the preamble phrase by phrase.

## Post-casebook developments (verified through 2026-11-18)

### Williamson v. Citrix Online, LLC, 792 F.3d 1339 (Fed. Cir. 2015) (en banc in part)
- No more "strong" presumption against 112(f) without "means"; nonce words such as "module" trigger 112(f). Full entry in Section 3. **Skill rule:** Drafting: structure or algorithm for every functional software element.

### Biosig Instruments, Inc. v. Nautilus, Inc., 783 F.3d 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015)
- On remand, the Federal Circuit again held the claims (including "spaced relationship") not indefinite, now applying the reasonable-certainty standard. **Skill rule:** Invalidity: Nautilus is not a kill switch; a relational term tied to a disclosed function and structure can survive.

### Teva Pharms. USA, Inc. v. Sandoz, Inc., 789 F.3d 1335 (Fed. Cir. 2015)
- On remand, the claim was again indefinite even with deference to fact findings: the patent and its prosecution history pointed to different averaging methods for "molecular weight." **Skill rule:** Drafting: name the measurement method; Prosecution: never characterize a parameter inconsistently across responses.

### Interval Licensing LLC v. AOL, Inc., 766 F.3d 1364 (Fed. Cir. 2014); Dow Chem. Co. v. Nova Chems. Corp., 803 F.3d 620 (Fed. Cir. 2015)
- First post-Nautilus applications: "unobtrusive manner" was indefinite as highly subjective; a parameter with at least four measurement methods giving different values, and no intrinsic guidance on which, was indefinite (reversing a supplemental damages award). **Skill rule:** Invalidity: list every alternative test method for each claimed number and show they give different results.

### Aylus Networks, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 856 F.3d 1353 (Fed. Cir. 2017)
- Patent-owner statements in IPR, including a preliminary response, can create prosecution disclaimer if clear and unmistakable. **Skill rule:** Prosecution/IPR: treat every patent-owner paper as claim-construction evidence.

### Zeroclick, LLC v. Apple Inc., 891 F.3d 1003 (Fed. Cir. 2018); Dyfan, LLC v. Target Corp., 28 F.4th 1360 (Fed. Cir. 2022)
- "Program," "user interface code," and "code"/"application" described by their operation were understood as structure, so 112(f) did not apply. **Skill rule:** Drafting: prefer art-recognized software nouns with recited operations over generic "module/unit."

### PTAB claim construction rule, 83 Fed. Reg. 51,340 (Oct. 11, 2018) (37 CFR 42.100(b), 42.200(b))
- AIA trials on petitions filed on or after Nov. 13, 2018 use the Phillips standard, not BRI; prior district court constructions in the record are considered. **Skill rule:** Invalidity/IPR: build one construction for both the IPR and the litigation.

### USPTO, Examining Computer-Implemented Functional Claim Limitations for Compliance with 35 U.S.C. 112, 84 Fed. Reg. 57 (Jan. 7, 2019)
- Examiners apply Williamson to computer-implemented functional terms and require a disclosed algorithm for 112(f) elements (MPEP 2181). **Skill rule:** Prosecution: respond to a 112(f) interpretation by pointing to the algorithm by paragraph and figure, or amend to add structure.

### Continental Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788 (Fed. Cir. 2019); Techtronic Indus. Co. v. ITC, 944 F.3d 901 (Fed. Cir. 2019) (No. 2018-2191)
- Disavowal requires expressions of manifest exclusion; describing a preferred method and contrasting it with prior art was not enough (Continental). But consistently presenting one placement as "the present invention" and criticizing the alternative disavowed it, without saying it was impossible (Techtronic). **Skill rule:** Drafting spec: describe advantages as "some embodiments may," and do not criticize alternatives you may later want to cover.

### Guangdong Alison Hi-Tech Co. v. ITC, 936 F.3d 1353 (Fed. Cir. 2019); Niazi Licensing Corp. v. St. Jude Med. S.C., Inc., 30 F.4th 1339 (Fed. Cir. 2022) (No. 2021-1864)
- Terms of degree ("lofty ... batting," "resilient," "pliable") are definite when the spec gives a definition, functional characteristics, or examples; broad is not the same as uncertain. **Skill rule:** Drafting: for each term of degree, give a definition plus at least two concrete examples and a counterexample.

### Bio-Rad Labs., Inc. v. 10X Genomics Inc., 967 F.3d 1353 (Fed. Cir. 2020)
- Distinguishes TomTom: a preamble can be partly limiting only when it divides cleanly; where the terms giving antecedent basis are intertwined with the rest, the whole preamble limits (there, to on-chip reactions). **Skill rule:** Charts: decide preamble effect phrase by phrase, then check whether the phrases can actually be separated.

### Egenera, Inc. v. Cisco Sys., Inc., 972 F.3d 1367 (Fed. Cir. 2020); Rain Computing, Inc. v. Samsung Elecs. Am., Inc., 989 F.3d 1002 (Fed. Cir. 2021)
- "Logic to modify" was a black-box nonce term under 112(f); "user identification module configured to control access" was 112(f) with no algorithm, so the claims were indefinite. **Skill rule:** `check_claims.py` WARN list should include logic, module, unit, element, mechanism, component, handler.

### Amgen Inc. v. Sanofi, 598 U.S. 594 (2023)
- Functional genus claims (antibodies defined by what they bind and block) must be enabled across their full scope; roadmaps and trial-and-error research assignments are not enough. Not a 112(f) case, but it is the enablement ceiling on functional claiming without "means." **Skill rule:** Drafting: when a claim defines a genus by function, anchor it with structural features shared by the members, or claim the disclosed species and close variants.

### Salazar v. AT&T Mobility LLC, 64 F.4th 1311 (Fed. Cir. 2023)
- "A microprocessor" followed by "said microprocessor" performing several functions requires at least one microprocessor capable of all of them. **Skill rule:** Drafting: use "one or more processors ... individually or collectively configured to" for distributed hardware.

### Fintiv, Inc. v. PayPal Holdings, Inc., No. 2023-2312 (Fed. Cir. Apr. 30, 2025) (precedential); Optis Cellular Tech., LLC v. Apple Inc., Nos. 2022-1904, -1925 (Fed. Cir. June 16, 2025)
- "Payment handler" was a nonce term like "module"; the spec's "two-step algorithm" just restated the claim, so the claims were indefinite. "Selecting unit" invoked 112(f) (remanded to look for corresponding structure). **Skill rule:** Drafting spec: the algorithm must say how, not restate the claimed result; write it as steps a programmer could follow.

### USPTO Director memorandum on indefiniteness in AIA post-grant proceedings (Jan. 6, 2021)
- PTAB applies Nautilus in IPR/PGR; Packard (and Ex parte McAward) still governs examination and ex parte appeals. **Skill rule:** Prosecution: answer 112(b) rejections under the stricter Packard test by amending for clarity, not by citing Nautilus.

## Checklist for the skill

Drafting claims (Phase 3, Step 2):
- Keep each claim one sentence with "comprising" unless a reason for a closed or hybrid transition is written down.
- Keep preambles to a category name; put location, environment, and use that matter in the body. No body term may take antecedent basis from a preamble you don't want to limit.
- Run an antecedent-basis pass: every "the/said X" traces to "a/an X" in the same claim or a parent, with identical words.
- Flag every nonce word + function pair (module, unit, logic, handler, mechanism, element, component, device "configured to"); rewrite with structure or confirm a linked algorithm exists for each function.
- Use "means for" only on purpose; for each such element, list all known alternative structures in the spec and link each to the function.
- Use "one or more processors ... individually or collectively" for distributed computing elements.
- For each term of degree, require a spec definition or measuring standard plus a dependent claim with a number.
- For each numeric parameter, name the test method; never put "about" next to a value close to the art.
- Define products by structure or measured properties; product-by-process only as a backup alongside a method-of-making claim.
- No omnibus, Jepson (unless asked), trademark, "whereby"-only, or mixed apparatus/user-action claims.
- Markush: "selected from the group consisting of ... and combinations thereof," and one dependent per member.
- For every narrow embodiment of a key term, add a one-difference dependent claim (claim differentiation).
- Write a parallel second independent claim that expresses the inventive idea through a different frame (the '126 claims 1/25 pattern).

Drafting the spec (Phase 3, Step 3):
- Search the draft for "present invention," "the invention is," "essential," "critical," "important feature," "must," "required," and disparaging comparisons; replace with "in some embodiments."
- Give at least two alternatives for every structural noun and every claimed element (wired/wireless; one-piece/multi-piece).
- Add express definitions ("As used herein, X means ...") for construction-dependent terms.
- For every software function in a claim, write numbered algorithm steps and a flowchart tied to that function by name.

Charts, FTO, invalidity (modules/analyze.md, Phase 2):
- Fetch the full file history of the patent, its parents and continuations, and any IPR before construing.
- Decide preamble effect per phrase with the Catalina guideposts.
- For each disputed term: plain meaning at the effective filing date, every dependent that narrows it, lexicography and disavowal candidates with quotes, file-history statements, then extrinsic sources; mark `construction_dependent` when the record points both ways.
- Chart 112(f) elements as function plus disclosed structure plus equivalents as of issuance; put after-arising substitutes in DOE.
- Chart product-by-process claims against the accused maker's actual process.
- Test every term of degree and functional phrase for Nautilus indefiniteness, and every nonce term for a missing algorithm.
- Compare the accused product to the claims only, never to the patentee's product.
- Use the same construction for infringement and validity.

Prosecution (modules/prosecution.md):
- Argue under BRI and the Packard indefiniteness test; amend rather than argue that a court would read it narrowly.
- Distinguish art on claim words; never characterize "the invention" in remarks; check that each argument is a scope the client accepts for the whole family and any later IPR.
- If a phrase is argued for allowance (preamble, whereby clause), treat it as a limitation from then on.
