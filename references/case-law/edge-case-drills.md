# Edge-case drills

**Purpose.** Law-school patent exams are built from the same few traps, year after year. A good exam writer hides the decisive fact in a date, a priority claim, a forum rule, or one amended word, and rewards the answer that finds it. This file teaches the skill to look for those traps in real matters. It has five parts:

1. An issue frequency table (what recurs, and how often).
2. Date-mechanics drills (D-1 to D-26).
3. Issue-spotting drills that braid validity and infringement (I-1 to I-22).
4. How a strong answer is structured, turned into output rules.
5. A checklist for the skill.

**Copyright and privacy.** Every fact pattern below is original: new technology, names, numbers and dates. Only the *issue patterns* were abstracted from the source exams. No exam text, answer text, examiner, student, or school is reproduced or named.

**How to use.** Load this file (a) before any output that computes a date, a bar, a priority, a term, or a PTAB window, and (b) at the self-review step of every phase. Run the checklist in Part 5 against the facts. Each drill names the trap a naive analyst falls into; if the matter matches a trap, say so in the report and mark the item `[review]`. Exam drills ignore the weekend and holiday roll; real matters must not (35 U.S.C. 21(b); `references/deadlines.md`; `scripts/_dates.py`).

**Reach a supported assessment.** These are teaching hypotheticals, not verified real-matter outcomes. State scope, strongest objection, supported response and resolution. Give a candid conditional judgment when justified; refresh primary law before reuse.

## Part 1. Issue frequency table

**Source set.** 11 distinct final or mock exams in US patent law from two course sequences (one exam appeared twice and is counted once), 5 top-graded answers, and 1 class handout of infringement hypotheticals. "Exams" = how many of the 11 tested the doctrine. "Instances" = approximate count of separate questions or sub-questions that turned on it. The handout is noted where relevant but not counted.

**What recurs most (build checks around these first):** which-law-applies (10), claim construction (10), § 101 (10), DOE (9), obviousness (9), grace-period math (8), and then a cluster at 7: Paris priority, term, reissue, PTAB timing and grounds, 102(a)(2) and its foreign-priority dating, pre-AIA antedating, on-sale, public use, 112(f), Festo.

**Errors found even in top-graded answers** (the skill must not repeat them): measuring the broadening-reissue window from publication; saying a patent with no maintenance fees expires on the original filing anniversary; requiring a fee or oath for a filing date; missing 102(b)(2)(B) and concluding "neither party gets a patent"; saying an amendment that cured indefiniteness was "broadening" and created no estoppel; applying 112(f) to method steps with no "step for"; treating a specialty firm's purchase from its contract manufacturer as an on-sale event; overlooking that an IPR was filed inside the PGR window; describing pre-AIA "first to invent" contests for applications filed after 3/16/2013.

## Part 2. Date-mechanics drills

**Method for every date question (do it in this order, in writing):**
1. List every event with its date: conception or build only if pre-AIA; each disclosure, sale, offer, use; each filing (provisional, foreign, PCT, US, continuation, reissue); publication; grant; service of a complaint; PTAB filings.
2. Decide which law governs each claim (AIA or pre-AIA; drill D-19). Effective filing date is claim by claim (100(i)).
3. Classify each reference: 102(a)(1) category and date, or 102(a)(2) and its effective date (102(d)). Then test the exceptions.
4. Compute windows with the statute's own words ("within," "not later than," "after," "more than 1 year"). Roll weekend and holiday deadlines for acts in the USPTO (21(b)); never roll an expiry date.
5. State the result, the rule, and what fact would flip it.

Exam questions ignore weekends and holidays; the dates below note the roll where it matters. Computed with `scripts/_dates.py` (add_months, roll_forward).

### D-1. Continuation term and a missed maintenance fee
**Facts.** A nonprovisional on a modular beehive is filed 2016-04-11. A continuation claiming 35 U.S.C. 120 benefit is filed 2019-02-04 and issues 2020-09-15 with no PTA. Nobody pays a maintenance fee.
**Q.** When does the continuation patent end?
**A.** Base term: 20 years from the earliest 120 application, 2036-04-11 (154(a)(2)). The first fee is payable 2023-09-15 to 2024-03-15, then with surcharge until the 4th anniversary of grant (41(b)(1)-(2); 37 CFR 1.362). Unpaid, the patent expires at the end of that grace period: 2024-09-15. That day is a Sunday, so a fee with surcharge paid Monday 2024-09-16 would still have been timely (1.362(f); 21(b)). After lapse, only a petition to accept delayed payment (1.378) can revive it, and intervening rights may apply (41(c)(2)).
**Trap.** Counting 20 years from the continuation's own filing date (2039), from grant, or saying the patent ends at the 3.5-year due date.

### D-2. Paris priority, PTA, and a terminal disclaimer
**Facts.** A Japanese application on a seaweed bioplastic is filed 2021-06-08. A US application claiming 119(a) priority is filed 2022-06-02. To overcome double patenting, the applicant files a terminal disclaimer over its own US patent that expires 2041-12-01. The patent issues 2025-03-11 with 140 days of PTA.
**Q.** Latest possible expiry?
**A.** Priority was timely (12 months ran to 2022-06-08). Term runs from the US filing, not the Japanese date (154(a)(3)): 2042-06-02. PTA adds 140 days: 2042-10-20. But PTA cannot extend past a disclaimer date (154(b)(2)(B)), so the patent expires 2041-12-01. Without the disclaimer, 2042-10-20. The disclaimer also makes enforceability depend on continued common ownership (37 CFR 1.321(c)).
**Trap.** Counting from the foreign date (2041-06-08) or stacking PTA on top of a disclaimer.

### D-3. Provisional dates count for priority, not for term, and only claim by claim
**Facts.** Provisional for a hydrogel wound dressing filed 2023-01-17. Nonprovisional claiming 119(e) filed 2024-01-16. Claim 1 is fully described in the provisional; claim 7 (a silver-ion variant) appears first in the nonprovisional. The inventor gave a public talk on the silver variant on 2023-03-01.
**Q.** Term and effective filing dates; is the talk a problem?
**A.** Term: 20 years from 2024-01-16, so 2044-01-16 plus PTA (119(e) not counted, 154(a)(3)). EFD of claim 1 = 2023-01-17; claim 7 = 2024-01-16 (100(i); *New Railhead*, `../disclosure-112.md`). The talk is within a year of claim 7's EFD, so it is excepted under 102(b)(1)(A) for claim 7. It also shields claim 7 against a later third-party disclosure of the same silver variant (102(b)(1)(B)). It is fatal to foreign filings in absolute-novelty countries.
**Trap.** Giving the whole application the provisional date.

### D-4. PCT national stage: deadline and term
**Facts.** Korean application filed 2021-05-12. PCT filed 2022-05-10 designating the US and claiming the Korean priority.
**Q.** Last day to enter the US national stage, and the term base?
**A.** 30 months from priority = 2023-11-12, a Sunday. Friday 2023-11-10 was the observed Veterans Day holiday, so the next business day is Monday 2023-11-13 (1.495; 21(b)). Term runs 20 years from the PCT international filing date, 2042-05-10, because the international application has the effect of a US filing as of that date (35 U.S.C. 363; 154(a)(2)), plus PTA.
**Trap.** Counting term from national-stage entry or from the Korean date.

### D-5. Reissue keeps the original term; broadening needs the 2-year window
**Facts.** Original application on a cordless soldering iron filed 2015-08-20, granted 2018-02-06. Reissue application with a broadened claim 12 filed 2019-11-01; reissue granted 2021-07-13.
**Q.** Is claim 12 allowed in principle, and when does the reissue expire?
**A.** Broadening is allowed only if the reissue was applied for within 2 years from grant of the original, i.e., by 2020-02-06 (251(d)); 2019-11-01 qualifies. The reissue runs for "the unexpired part of the term of the original patent" (251(a)): 2035-08-20 plus any original PTA. New or amended claims carry intervening-rights limits (252).
**Trap.** Treating reissue as a fresh 20-year term or as a term extension.

### D-6. Broadening reissue of a continuation patent
**Facts.** Parent filed 2017-03-02. After a final rejection, a continuation is filed 2023-10-05, published 2024-04-11, and granted 2024-12-17. A competitor designs around the claims on 2025-06-01. No family application is still pending.
**Q.** Last day to file a broadening reissue?
**A.** 2026-12-17: two years from grant of the patent being reissued (251(d)). Not from the parent's filing, not from publication. The broadened claims must still be supported by the original disclosure and must not recapture subject matter surrendered in prosecution (`prosecution.md`). If a family member were pending, a new continuation would be the simpler path.
**Trap.** Measuring from the publication date or from the parent's grant.

### D-7. Suing after expiry; reissue claims and intervening rights
**Facts.** A grain-moisture-sensor patent filed 2014-09-09 issued 2017 with no PTA; fees were paid. It was reissued 2022-05-03 with claim 1 unchanged and a new claim 15. A competitor sold sensors from 2015 through 2036.
**Q.** Last day to sue, and what can each claim reach?
**A.** The patent expires 2034-09-09. Section 286 is not a statute of limitations on filing suit: it bars recovery for infringement more than 6 years before the complaint. A complaint filed in early September 2040 can still reach the last days of the term. Claim 1 is substantively identical to an original claim, so damages run from the original grant, within the 6-year window (252). Claim 15 is new: damages only from 2022-05-03, and the competitor may have absolute and equitable intervening rights for things made, bought, or prepared before reissue (252). Sales after expiry, and sales before the 2017 grant (absent provisional rights), are not infringement.
**Trap.** "The patent expired, so it can't be enforced."

### D-8. Damages window: § 286, marking, provisional rights
**Facts.** A published application (2016-06-02) on a lockable bike-share dock issues 2018-10-09 with claims substantially identical to the published claims. The patentee sells unmarked docks. It sends a notice letter 2021-01-15 and sues 2025-03-03. The accused product launched 2016-01.
**Q.** What period can damages cover?
**A.** Section 286: nothing before 2019-03-03. Marking: the patentee sells an unmarked patented article and asserts apparatus claims, so damages start only on actual notice, 2021-01-15 (287(a)). Provisional rights (154(d)) for 2016-06-02 to 2018-10-09 require actual notice of the published application, substantially identical claims, and suit within 6 years of grant (by 2024-10-09); suit in 2025 is too late. If only method claims are asserted, marking does not apply and damages could start 2019-03-03 (`remedies.md`, marking).
**Trap.** Ignoring marking, or assuming pre-grant activity is automatically compensable.

### D-9. PGR deadline and the first IPR day
**Facts.** An AIA patent (EFD in 2021) on a soil-moisture probe is granted 2025-07-15. No litigation.
**Q.** Last day for PGR; first day for IPR?
**A.** PGR "not later than" 9 months after grant: 2026-04-15 (321(c)). IPR "after the later of" 9 months after grant or termination of an instituted PGR (311(c); 37 CFR 42.102(a)(1), (3)): 2026-04-16 if no PGR is instituted. PGR allows any 282(b)(2)-(3) ground (101, 102 including public use and sales, 103, 112 other than best mode) (321(b)); IPR allows only 102/103 on patents and printed publications (311(b)). PGR estoppel reaches grounds that "reasonably could have" been raised (325(e)).
**Trap.** Writing "9 months" for both, or advising an IPR on sales or public use.

### D-10. A pre-AIA patent has no 9-month wait and no PGR
**Facts.** An application filed 2012-10-01, never containing a claim with a later EFD, issues 2016-05-03. In June 2016 a competitor asks for the earliest IPR date.
**A.** Any time after grant: 42.102(a)(2) applies the 9-month wait only to AIA patents (3(n)(1)). PGR is unavailable for pre-AIA patents. The 315(b) one-year bar still applies if the competitor is served with a complaint.
**Trap.** Applying 311(c)'s 9-month period by rote.

### D-11. The 315(b) clock runs from service; DJ actions and counterclaims
**Facts.** Complaint filed 2025-02-10, served 2025-02-24. In 2024 the defendant had filed a declaratory-judgment action seeking only a declaration of non-infringement. It now counterclaims for invalidity.
**Q.** IPR deadline; do the DJ action or counterclaim bar IPR?
**A.** 1 year from service: 2026-02-24 (315(b)). An IPR is barred only if the petitioner, before petitioning, *filed a civil action challenging validity* (315(a)(1)); a non-infringement-only DJ is not that, and a validity counterclaim never counts (315(a)(3)). A time-barred party may still seek joinder to an instituted IPR (315(b), last sentence). Treat a complaint later dismissed without prejudice as possibly starting the clock [review]: the Federal Circuit had held that it does, but the Supreme Court vacated that judgment, holding only that the Board's 315(b) call is unreviewable on appeal (*Thryv v. Click-to-Call*, 590 U.S. 45 (2020); `ptab.md` playbook item 4).
**Trap.** Counting from the filing date; assuming any DJ bars IPR.

### D-12. Full PTAB calendar for a newly sued client
**Facts.** AIA patent granted 2026-01-20. Complaint served 2026-02-02. The client asks on 2026-03-01 what it can file and when.
**A.** PGR window: until 2026-10-20 (321(c)), all grounds including on-sale, public use, 101, and 112; filing a DJ action of invalidity first would bar it (325(a)(1)). IPR window: opens 2026-10-21 (311(c)) and closes 2027-02-02 (315(b)), so it is only about 3.5 months wide. Institution practice has changed repeatedly since 2025 (Director-level discretionary screen, "settled expectations," parallel-litigation factors, the Nov. 2026 memo returning merits review to panels): run the current screen in `ptab.md` §8 before advising and mark it `[review]`.
**Trap.** Telling the client to "file an IPR now."

### D-13. The grace period rolls over weekends and holidays
**Facts.** An inventor posts a public demo video on 2025-02-14 and files a US application on 2026-02-17.
**A.** One year ends 2026-02-14, a Saturday; Monday 2026-02-16 is Washington's Birthday. The grace period extends to the next business day, 2026-02-17 (MPEP 2153.01; 21(b)), so the video is excepted under 102(b)(1)(A). Foreign rights in absolute-novelty countries were lost when the video went up.
**Trap.** Calling the filing late, or relying on the roll when an earlier filing was possible. Never apply US roll rules to foreign deadlines.

### D-14. The 102(b)(1)(B) shield covers the same subject matter only
**Facts.** An inventor publishes a bicycle-frame joint using a titanium sleeve on 2024-05-01. An independent rival publishes on 2024-07-15 the same titanium joint plus an alternative carbon-fiber sleeve. The inventor files 2025-04-20 with claim 1 (sleeve of any material), claim 2 (titanium), claim 3 (carbon fiber).
**A.** The inventor's own article is excepted (102(b)(1)(A)). The rival's titanium disclosure is excepted (102(b)(1)(B)): same subject matter, earlier publicly disclosed by the inventor. The rival's carbon-fiber disclosure was never disclosed by the inventor, so it is prior art (MPEP 2153.02: even trivial or obvious variants are not shielded). It anticipates claim 3, and as a species it anticipates genus claim 1. Claim 2 survives this reference.
**Trap.** Treating the whole rival paper as shielded because "the inventor published first."

### D-15. Foreign priority dates a 102(a)(2) reference under the AIA, not under pre-AIA law
**Facts.** A rival files in Germany 2022-03-01, then in the US 2023-02-27 claiming that priority; the US application publishes 2023-09-07. The client, with no disclosures, filed in the US 2022-11-10. The German filing describes the client's claimed subject matter.
**A.** The rival's US filing was within 12 months (by 2023-03-01). Under the AIA its publication is 102(a)(2) art effective 2022-03-01 (102(d)(2)), before the client's EFD. The client's only outs are 102(b)(2)(A)-(C). If the client's claims were pre-AIA (shift every date back to 2010-2011), the same reference would date only from its US filing under 102(e) (*In re Hilmer*, `novelty-and-bars.md`) and would not be prior art. If the German filing did not describe the subject matter, the reference dates from 2023-02-27 and is not art either way.
**Trap.** Using *Hilmer* for an AIA patent, or the foreign date without checking that the foreign filing describes the relied-on subject matter.

### D-16. A foreign-only application is never 102(a)(2) art
**Facts.** A rival's Chinese application is filed 2021-08-01 and published 2023-02-01. It never enters the US or the PCT. The client filed in the US 2022-06-01.
**A.** 102(a)(2) covers only US patents, published US applications, and PCT applications designating the US that are deemed published under 122(b). The Chinese document is prior art only under 102(a)(1), as a printed publication, from 2023-02-01: after the client's EFD, so not prior art. Had the rival filed a PCT designating the US and it published, it would be 102(a)(2) art from its earliest describing priority date.
**Trap.** Importing the European "whole contents" idea (EPC Art. 54(3)) and dating a foreign application from its filing.

### D-17. Missing the 12-month Paris deadline is not always fatal
**Facts.** EP filing 2024-03-05. US filing claiming that priority on 2025-04-28. An independent article disclosing the same invention appeared 2024-11-12.
**A.** The 12 months ran to 2025-03-05. The US filing is within the 2 extra months (by 2025-05-05), so priority may be restored by petition if the delay was unintentional (119(a); 37 CFR 1.55(c)). Restored: EFD 2024-03-05, and the article is not prior art. Not restored: EFD 2025-04-28, and the article is prior art with no exception (the inventor made no earlier public disclosure). The priority claim itself must also be made on time (1.55(d)).
**Trap.** "Over 12 months, priority lost." Check restoration first.

### D-18. Same text is not a priority claim
**Facts.** Singapore filing 2023-07-03. US filing 2024-06-24 with identical text, but the ADS claims no priority. A third-party article appeared 2024-01-10.
**A.** Until priority is claimed, the EFD is 2024-06-24 and the article is prior art. The claim deadline is the later of 4 months from US filing (2024-10-24) or 16 months from the foreign filing (2024-11-03, a Sunday, so 2024-11-04) (1.55(d); `../deadlines.md`). A corrected ADS by then restores the 2023-07-03 EFD and removes the article. Later, only a petition for an unintentionally delayed claim (1.55(e)).
**Trap.** Assuming "identical disclosure" gives the foreign date.

### D-19. Mixed applications: one later claim makes everything AIA
**Facts.** Provisional 2012-11-05 on a trailer-hitch damper. Nonprovisional 2013-10-30 adds a new elastomer embodiment and claim 9 to it. Claim 9 is cancelled in 2015; the patent issues 2016 with claims 1-8, all supported by the provisional.
**A.** Claim 9 had an EFD of 2013-10-30. An application that "contains or contained at any time" a claim with an EFD on or after 2013-03-16 is examined and judged under AIA 102/103 for all claims, even after cancellation (AIA 3(n)(1); MPEP 2159.02). Because other claims have pre-2013 EFDs, pre-AIA 102(g) also applies to every claim (3(n)(2); MPEP 2159.03). Consequences: foreign public uses and sales before the EFD count; no swearing behind; the 102(b)(1) exceptions govern. Continuations of this application are AIA too.
**Trap.** Analyzing claims 1-8 under pre-AIA law because the provisional supports them. The opposite also holds: if claim 9 had been supported by the provisional, everything would be pre-AIA.

### D-20. Pre-AIA: antedate 102(a) and 102(e) art, never 102(b) art
**Facts.** Pre-AIA application filed 2011-09-14 on a seed planter. The examiner cites (a) an article published 2011-02-01, (b) an article published 2010-08-01, and (c) another's US patent filed 2010-12-01 that discloses but does not claim the invention.
**A.** Critical date: 2010-09-14. (b) is more than a year before filing: a 102(b) bar that cannot be antedated. (a) is 102(a) art: antedate under 37 CFR 1.131 by showing actual reduction to practice before 2011-02-01, or conception before it plus diligence from just before 2011-02-01 to reduction to practice (actual, or constructive by filing). (c) is 102(e) art as of 2010-12-01: antedate that date. If (c) *claimed* the same invention, Rule 131 would not be available and contest procedures would apply [review].
**Trap.** Swearing behind (b), or dating (c) from its publication.

### D-21. Pre-AIA priority contest: diligence is measured at the rival's conception
**Facts (pre-AIA).** A conceives 2010-01-10, works sporadically, works continuously from 2010-05-03, builds a working unit 2010-09-01, files 2011-01-15. B, independently, conceives 2010-04-20 and files 2010-07-01 without building anything.
**A.** B reduced to practice first (constructively, by filing). A conceived first but reduced to practice last, so A prevails only by showing reasonable diligence from just before B's conception (2010-04-20) to A's reduction to practice. A was idle 2010-04-20 to 2010-05-03; absent an excuse, A is not diligent at the critical time, and B prevails. A's four-month gap between building and filing is not, on these facts, abandonment, suppression, or concealment. Still relevant today for pre-AIA patents in force (filed before 2013-03-16; most expire by early 2033 absent PTA) and through pre-AIA 102(g) in mixed applications.
**Trap.** "First to conceive wins," "first to file wins," or applying this analysis to an AIA application.

### D-22. A private sale starts your clock but does not shield you (*Sanho*)
**Facts.** Inventor P sells one solar charger privately to a distributor on 2024-01-20, without an NDA; nothing is shown publicly. Independent inventor Q files a US application 2024-03-01 that later publishes. P files 2024-09-30.
**A.** Against P: Q's application is 102(a)(2) art as of 2024-03-01. P can escape only if P "publicly disclosed" the subject matter first (102(b)(2)(B)). A private sale that taught no one the claimed features is not a public disclosure (*Sanho Corp. v. Kaijet*, Fed. Cir. 2024, `novelty-and-bars.md`), so P is blocked on what Q discloses. P's sale is within P's own grace period (102(b)(1)(A)), which does not help against Q. Against Q: whether a third party's confidential sale is prior art against an independent inventor under the AIA is unsettled; flag it rather than resolve it.
**Trap.** Equating "sale" with "public disclosure," or concluding Q is clearly safe.

### D-23. Sometimes nobody gets a patent
**Facts.** R publishes 2023-02-10 and files in the US 2024-03-01. S, independently, files in France 2023-06-15 and in the US 2024-06-10 claiming that priority.
**A.** R's grace period ended 2024-02-10 (a Saturday, rolled to Monday 2024-02-12). Filing 2024-03-01 is late, so R's own article bars R (102(a)(1)). S's EFD is 2023-06-15; R's earlier article is prior art to S with no exception. Neither obtains a patent on what the article discloses; it is in the public domain.
**Trap.** Assuming the contest must have a winner, or letting S benefit from R's self-inflicted bar.

### D-24. Submission is not publication; online-first beats the print date
**Facts.** An inventor submits a manuscript under confidential review on 2024-02-01. The journal posts it online-first 2024-08-20; the print issue is dated October 2024. The inventor files 2025-08-25.
**A.** Confidential submission is not a disclosure. The disclosure date is the earliest public availability, 2024-08-20. The grace period ended 2025-08-20 (a Wednesday), so the filing is late and the article bars what it discloses. A preprint-server posting would have started the clock even earlier.
**Trap.** Measuring from submission (too early) or from the print issue date (too late).

### D-25. What a filing date needs
**Facts.** An inventor uploads a specification and drawings 2025-05-06 with no claims, no fee, and no inventor declaration. Claims follow 2025-06-10, fees 2025-06-20, the declaration 2025-09-01.
**A.** Filing date 2025-05-06: the date "a specification, with or without claims, is received" (111(a)(4), for applications filed on or after 2013-12-18). Missing claims, fees, and the declaration draw a notice and surcharges (37 CFR 1.53(f)). Claims added later must be supported by the specification as filed (no new matter; 112(a), 132).
**Trap.** Requiring claims, fee, or oath for a filing date. That was the old rule.

### D-26. Filter references by forum before analyzing them
**Facts.** An AIA patent on a wearable posture sensor was filed 2023-04-03 (no priority) and granted 2025-01-14. In November 2025 a challenger lists: (a) the patentee's live trade-show demo 2022-03-01, no handouts; (b) the patentee's brochure handed out at a show 2022-10-01; (c) a third party's product sold 2023-01-10; (d) a third party's manual stamped confidential, given to 5 customers in December 2022 and posted publicly 2023-05-01; (e) a third-party journal article 2022-02-15.
**A.** The PGR window closed 2025-10-14, so the PTAB route is IPR (or ex parte reexam), limited to patents and printed publications under 102/103 (311(b)). (a) is prior art (public use, more than a year before EFD) but not an IPR ground; it needs court. (b) is the patentee's own printed publication within the year: excepted (102(b)(1)(A)). (c) is a sale: not an IPR ground. (d) was not publicly accessible before the EFD (confidential, limited distribution) and became public only after it: not prior art. (e) is a printed publication more than a year before EFD: IPR-eligible. Only (e) can be an IPR ground. Applicant-admitted prior art cannot be "the basis" of an IPR ground either (`../novelty-and-obviousness.md`, *Qualcomm v. Apple*).
**Trap.** Listing everything that is prior art as if all of it could go into an IPR.

## Part 3. Issue-spotting drills (validity and infringement braided)

**Method for every fact pattern.** (1) Timeline and governing law (Part 2 method). (2) Parse each claim into lettered elements. (3) Construe the disputed terms (preamble, words of degree, numeric ranges, transitions, means-plus-function). (4) Validity, ground by ground: 101, 102/103 with each reference's category and date, 112(a), 112(b), then forum (court, PGR, IPR, reexam). (5) Infringement: actor by actor, literal element by element, then DOE limited by estoppel, dedication, and vitiation; then indirect and extraterritorial theories. (6) Defenses (273, exhaustion, licenses) and remedies (286, marking, provisional rights, injunction). (7) Name the missing facts that would flip the outcome.

### I-1. Multi-actor platform method on an old patent
**Facts.** A patent filed 2011 and granted 2014 claims: "A method of operating an automated system for lending power tools to members, comprising: (a) providing a central server; (b) providing a plurality of tools at dispersed lockers; (c) providing wireless devices for communication among members, lockers, and the server; (d) sending a request for a tool; (e) assigning a tool to the requester; and (f) charging the requester." The specification has detailed locker-assignment algorithms and a flowchart; none are claimed. The patentee no longer operates and has licensed two locker companies. The accused platform lets members request tools in its app; independent couriers fetch tools from their own garages and deliver them; the platform charges members.
**Issues.** Governing law and term; 101; preamble and "lockers"; who performs which step; divided infringement; inducement; marking and damages window.
**Analysis.**
1. Pre-AIA patent (EFD 2011): pre-AIA 102/103 govern validity. Term ends 2031 plus any PTA; confirm fees were paid.
2. 101: step one likely finds the claim directed to matching a request to an available item and billing for it, a fundamental economic practice, on generic components (*Alice*, `eligibility-and-utility.md`). Step two: the unclaimed algorithms cannot supply an inventive concept; eligibility turns on the claims. High 101 risk.
3. Construction: "members" in the preamble supplies antecedent basis for step (c), which favors treating the preamble as limiting (*Catalina*, `claims-and-construction.md`). "Dispersed lockers" vs couriers' garages is a genuine construction dispute; literal coverage is doubtful, DOE possible.
4. Actors: platform performs (a), (e), (f) and part of (c); members perform (d); couriers perform (b).
5. Divided infringement: one entity is liable only if the others' steps are attributable to it, because it directs or controls them (agency or contract), or conditions participation or a benefit on performing a step and sets its manner or timing (*Akamai*, en banc 2015, `infringement.md`). Members must use the app's request flow to get a tool; couriers work under platform contracts and standards. Attribution is plausible but fact-intensive.
6. Inducement needs a single direct infringer (*Limelight*, 2014). If attribution fails, inducement fails too.
7. Method-only claims: § 287(a) marking does not apply, so the licensees' unmarked lockers do not cut damages (`remedies.md`, *Hanson*). Section 286 limits recovery to 6 years before suit.
**Trap.** Declaring "no single actor, no infringement" without running *Akamai*; rescuing 101 with unclaimed spec details; forgetting that marking is irrelevant to method-only patents.

### I-2. Combination dosing: treatment claims, a diagnostic-style claim, and a generic label
**Facts.** A patent (filed 2021) covers treating chronic gout with infused drug G. Claim 1: (a) administering 8-10 g of supplement S (sold over the counter) no more than 12 hours before administering G; (b) administering G. Claim 2: (a) administering G; (b) measuring the plasma level of G; (c) wherein a level below 3 µg/mL indicates a need to give 8-10 g of S within 12 hours before the next dose. Claim 3: (a) instructing the patient to take 8-10 g of S within 12 hours before G; (b) confirming the patient did so; (c) administering G. A generic maker files an ANDA for G; its proposed label says, "Your physician may recommend supplement S before infusion." Clinics infuse G; patients take S at home.
**Issues.** 101 for each claim; obviousness of the dose; who directly infringes each claim; inducement through the label; the ANDA act of infringement; skinny-label risk.
**Analysis.**
1. 101: claims 1 and 3 are methods of treatment with specific drugs, doses, and timing, the kind held eligible at step one (*Vanda v. West-Ward*, Fed. Cir. 2018; `eligibility-and-utility.md`). Claim 2's "wherein ... indicates a need" clause states a natural correlation without requiring any treatment step, close to *Mayo*: high risk. The clause may also carry no patentable weight, leaving "administer G and measure its level," which old practice may anticipate.
2. 103: S and G are each known; the question is whether the dose and timing were routine optimization or produced unexpected results (`obviousness.md`). Ask for the data.
3. Direct infringement: claim 1 splits between patient (S) and clinic (G). The patient's step is attributable to the physician if the physician conditions G treatment on taking S and sets dose and timing (*Eli Lilly v. Teva Parenteral*, 845 F.3d 1357 (Fed. Cir. 2017), applying *Akamai*). Claim 3 is performed entirely by the physician: no divided-infringement issue.
4. Inducement: a label that merely says a physician "may recommend" S is weaker evidence of intent than a label that instructs physicians to direct S at a stated dose; vague statements that require going outside the label have been held insufficient (the *Lilly* court, quoting *Takeda*). Expect a fight over this wording.
5. Filing the ANDA is itself an act of infringement for any claim the label would induce (271(e)(2), `pharma-and-sep.md`); carve-out and skinny-label law is unsettled there.
**Trap.** Treating every medical method as *Mayo*-ineligible, or concluding "no infringement because the patient does a step."

### I-3. Which law applies decides whether a foreign public use counts
**Facts.** Claim 1: "A bicycle restraint area for a rail car, comprising: (a) a wall-mounted cradle; (b) a ratchet strap; (c) a floor channel with a spring latch; (d) wherein the front wheel sits in the channel and the latch engages it." Every embodiment is on a rail car; one sentence says, "The restraint may be adapted for ferries, buses, or aircraft." Provisional filed 2012-09-04; nonprovisional 2013-08-20. Through 2011-2012 the inventor kept prototypes in a retired tram inside his paid-admission sculpture garden; the tram was unlocked. An independent Norwegian ferry line publicly used an identical restraint from 2012-06-10. The accused, a US ferry operator, installs the same system on ferries.
**Issues.** AIA or pre-AIA; the inventor's own prototypes as public use; the Norwegian use; the preamble; DOE and dedication.
**Analysis.**
1. Governing law turns on a missing fact: did every claim the nonprovisional ever contained find support in the provisional? If yes, pre-AIA; if any claim did not, AIA for all claims plus pre-AIA 102(g) (D-19). Get the provisional and the full claim history.
2. Inventor's prototypes: a display open to paying visitors with no confidentiality and an unlocked door can be a public use (`novelty-and-bars.md`, public use). Any public use before 2011-09-04 (one year before the provisional) bars the claims it embodies under either regime; dates of the first accessible prototype matter. Experimental-use negation needs inventor control and a testing purpose.
3. Norwegian public use: pre-AIA, a foreign public use is not prior art (pre-AIA 102(a)/(b) require use "in this country"). AIA: it is prior art before the EFD unless 102(b)(1)(B) applies, which requires an earlier inventor *public* disclosure of the same subject matter; whether the garden display "publicly disclosed" the claimed features is a *Sanho* question.
4. Preamble "for a rail car": intended-use language is usually not limiting, but a preamble that the specification treats as defining the invention can be (*Catalina*). Arguable both ways.
5. If the preamble limits, ferries need DOE. But the specification names ferries as an alternative and the claims do not: disclosed-but-unclaimed subject matter is dedicated to the public and cannot be recaptured by DOE (*Johnson & Johnston*, en banc 2002, `infringement.md`). A specific named alternative, as here, is the strong case for dedication.
**Trap.** Deciding the foreign-use question before settling AIA vs pre-AIA; running DOE without the dedication check.

### I-4. Genus narrowed to species for enablement, then a near-identical species
**Facts.** An AIA patent (filed 2022-05-09, granted 2026-10-27) claims "A two-part adhesive for floor tiles, comprising: (a) at least one of resins R-11, R-12, and R-17; (b) hardener H-2 or H-3; and (c) silica nanofiller." As filed, (a) and (b) read "an epoxy resin" and "an amine hardener"; the examiner rejected for lack of enablement, and the applicant narrowed to the tested species. The client's adhesive uses resin R-14 (a structural isomer of R-11 that chemists swap freely; not named in the patent), H-2, and silica; half its sales are for wall panels. The client began selling in 2022-08. Known art: (i) an academic's talk with slides, not distributed, to 300 chemists in Seoul on 2021-03-15, describing epoxy plus amine plus silica nanofiller; (ii) a foreign company's internal use of the combination in closed factories since 2019, never sold. The client received a demand letter today, 2026-11-18.
**Issues.** Literal infringement; DOE and Festo; the preamble; prior art status of (i) and (ii); § 273; forum.
**Analysis.**
1. Literal: R-14 is not in the Markush-style list. No literal infringement of (a).
2. DOE: the amendment narrowed (a) to overcome an enablement rejection. Any narrowing amendment made to satisfy a patentability requirement, 112 included, raises the *Festo* presumption of surrender (`infringement.md`). Rebuttal fails if R-14 was a known, foreseeable substitute at the time of amendment, and here it was. DOE for (a) is likely barred.
3. Preamble "for floor tiles" is intended use, usually not limiting; wall-panel sales are not a safe harbor if the composition otherwise reads.
4. (i) Under the AIA, an oral presentation anywhere is "otherwise available to the public" if it enables the claimed subject matter (`novelty-and-bars.md`); it is more than a year before EFD, so no exception. It discloses the genus, not the claimed species, so it supports 103 rather than 102. Whether displayed-but-undistributed slides are a "printed publication" matters for IPR eligibility (*Klopfenstein* factors). (ii) Secret internal use is not public and, for a third party, is not prior art under the traditional rule; AIA treatment is unsettled: flag it.
5. § 273: the client's commercial use began after the EFD. No defense.
6. Forum: PGR is open until 2027-07-27 (321(c)) and can use the oral talk and 112 grounds; IPR could use only printed publications.
**Trap.** Thinking an amendment made for enablement creates no estoppel; dismissing a foreign oral talk as non-prior art (pre-AIA thinking); invoking § 273 for use that started after the EFD.

### I-5. An IPR petition with five defects
**Facts.** An AIA patent on a folding e-scooter with a "bistable spring latch" that holds the stem upright or folded was filed 2023-09-12 and granted 2025-02-03. A competitor filed an IPR petition on 2025-10-20 asserting: (1) "latch means associated with the stem" is indefinite; (2) obviousness over a hobby magazine whose cover says "February 2023" (folding scooter held by a strap) plus marine-hatch bistable springs sold by a boat-hardware firm since 2022; (3) obviousness over the magazine plus the hatch maker's installation manual, stamped confidential, given to 12 dealers in 2022 and posted online 2023-10-30; (4) anticipation by the inventor's demo at a Milan trade show on 2023-03-20; (5) inequitable conduct because the inventor blogged about the magazine but never cited it. A third party's ex parte reexamination on the magazine was ordered 2025-09-30.
**Analysis.**
1. Timing: for an AIA patent an IPR may be filed only after 9 months from grant (2025-11-03) (311(c); 42.102(a)(1)). On 2025-10-20 the PGR window was open; a PGR would have allowed grounds (1) and the sales-based theory. Refile as PGR by 2025-11-03, or as IPR afterward.
2. Ground (1): indefiniteness is not an IPR ground (311(b)). It is a PGR ground (321(b)). "Means" also triggers 112(f); if the specification discloses no corresponding structure, the claim is indefinite.
3. Ground (2): the magazine is within a year before EFD and the inventor's demo came later, so no 102(b)(1)(B) shield: it is 102(a)(1) art if its public-accessibility date is proven; a cover date alone is weak proof, so gather distribution evidence. The hatch springs are known only through product sales, which cannot be the basis of an IPR ground (311(b)). Analogous art: a marine hatch is outside the field but arguably reasonably pertinent to holding a hinged member stable in two positions (*In re Bigio*, *In re Clay*, `obviousness.md`).
4. Ground (3): a confidential manual given to a limited group was not publicly accessible; the public posting came after the EFD. Not prior art.
5. Ground (4): the inventor's own disclosure within the year is excepted (102(b)(1)(A)), and a demo is not an IPR ground anyway.
6. Ground (5): inequitable conduct is a court defense, never an IPR ground; it needs but-for materiality and specific intent as the single most reasonable inference (*Therasense*, `prosecution.md`). Knowing of a reference is not enough.
7. Parallel reexam on the same art invites denial under 325(d) (*Advanced Bionics* two-part framework) and stay or consolidation (315(d)). Run the current discretionary screen (`ptab.md` §8).
**Trap.** Filing an IPR inside the PGR window, padding it with non-IPR grounds, and ignoring 325(d).

### I-6. Product-by-process, "about," "consisting essentially of," and a contract manufacturer
**Facts.** A pre-AIA patent (filed 2010-06-01, granted 2012-02-07) on a vaccine adjuvant has claim 1: "A lyophilized adjuvant batch prepared by a process comprising: (i) dissolving adjuvant A in buffer; (ii) adding trehalose; (iii) freeze-drying; wherein residual moisture is about 2%." Step (ii) originally read "adding a stabilizing sugar"; it was narrowed to trehalose to overcome an obviousness rejection. To overcome an indefiniteness rejection the applicant argued that "about 2%" means 1.5-2.5%. Claim 2: "A composition consisting essentially of adjuvant A and trehalose." The specification omits a well-known textbook formula the inventor used to set the sugar ratio. Starting 2009-03 the patentee, which has no plant, paid a contract manufacturer to make batches; title stayed with the patentee and batches sat in quarantine; first commercial sales were in 2010-09. The accused product uses sucrose, has 3.1% moisture, and contains a surfactant; the accused has used its secret process in the US since 2008 and sold the product publicly since 2008.
**Analysis.**
1. Claim 1 infringement: process steps limit a product-by-process claim for infringement (*Abbott v. Sandoz*, en banc 2009, `claims-and-construction.md`). Sucrose is not trehalose: no literal infringement. DOE for (ii) is presumptively barred by *Festo* (narrowed over prior art; sucrose was foreseeable).
2. "About 2%": the applicant's own construction (1.5-2.5%) binds it through prosecution disclaimer and argument-based estoppel; 3.1% is outside, literally and by equivalents.
3. Claim 2: "consisting essentially of" admits unlisted ingredients that do not materially affect the basic and novel characteristics; whether the surfactant does is a fact question. The accused has no trehalose, so no literal infringement; DOE for sucrose faces the same surrender logic if claim 2 was added in response to the same rejection [review].
4. On-sale bar (pre-AIA 102(b), critical date 2009-06-01): paying a contract manufacturer for manufacturing services, with title retained and product stockpiled, is not a commercial sale of the invention (*Medicines Co. v. Hospira*, en banc 2016, `novelty-and-bars.md`). Likely no bar.
5. The accused's prior activity: its secret process is not prior art against another's patent (*Gore*). For validity a product-by-process claim is judged as a product (`claims-and-construction.md`), so the accused's publicly sold product would anticipate only if it is the same product; with sucrose and 3.1% moisture it is not (103 remains).
6. 112(a): a specification need not teach what skilled artisans already know, so omitting a textbook formula rarely defeats enablement. Best mode is still required at filing, but failure to disclose it is not an invalidity ground in litigation (282(b)(3)(A)).
7. § 273: the patent issued after 2011-09-16, and the accused's good-faith US commercial use of the process began more than a year before the EFD, so the prior-use defense is likely available for the process (clear and convincing proof; limited to the specific subject matter, 273(e)(3)).
**Trap.** Treating contract manufacturing as a sale; running DOE before estoppel; calling best mode an invalidity ground; treating a third party's secret process as prior art; forgetting § 273.

### I-7. A machine claim can still be "directed to" an abstract idea
**Facts.** Claim: a rowing machine with a frame, sliding seat, flywheel with adjustable magnetic resistance, and handle; a computer that downloads coached routines specifying stroke rate, resistance, and posture; sensors tracking power; and a display showing the routine and the user's power "thereby simulating an instructor-led class." The specification's stated benefit is user engagement.
**Analysis.** Step one asks what the claim's advance is, not whether the claim recites hardware. Courts have found claims to a digital camera and to a garage-door opener directed to abstract ideas when the claimed advance was itself the idea: taking two pictures and using one to enhance the other (*Yu v. Apple*, Fed. Cir. 2021, over a dissent), and wirelessly communicating status information (*Chamberlain v. Techtronic*, 935 F.3d 1341 (Fed. Cir. 2019)); see also *American Axle* (a driveshaft-manufacturing method held directed to a natural law), `eligibility-and-utility.md`. Here the advance is presenting coaching content and metrics: likely abstract. Step two: the rowing hardware, sensors, computer, and display are conventional as claimed. The patentee's best argument is the claim as a whole is a specific exercise machine; it improves if the routine *automatically* drives the magnetic resistance through a specific control scheme (a technological improvement in how the machine works, *Diamond v. Diehr*). USPTO examination guidance can differ from court outcomes (`../eligibility-101.md`).
**Trap.** "It's a machine, so it passes step one." Also: deciding 101 by novelty; keep 102/103 separate.

### I-8. Wirelessly reporting device status
**Facts.** Claim: "An irrigation valve controller comprising: a controller having a plurality of operating states; a valve interface coupled to the controller; and a wireless transmitter coupled to the controller that transmits a status signal corresponding to a present operating state." The specification says the transmitted status lets remote devices react.
**Analysis.** This is the pattern of *Chamberlain v. Techtronic*: claims to a conventional device plus wireless transmission of its status were directed to the abstract idea of wirelessly communicating status information, and the only allegedly inventive feature was that idea itself, so there was no inventive concept. High 101 risk. Eligibility improves only with a claimed technical solution (a specific signal structure that solves a stated technical problem, a sensor arrangement that changes how the valve operates). Drafting lesson for Phase 3: claim *how* the device improves, not *that* it reports.
**Trap.** Treating a claimed physical controller as automatically eligible.

### I-9. Means-plus-function software claims with no algorithm (asked only about 101)
**Facts.** Claim 1: "A system for maintaining an aquarium, comprising: (a) means for receiving, by a mobile device, sensor data including images, temperature, pH, and turbidity; (b) means for analyzing the sensor data to determine a fish-stress level based on thresholds; (c) means for generating a care recommendation; and (d) means for displaying the recommendation." Claim 2: "The system of claim 1, wherein the mobile device automatically doses buffer through a networked pump and adjusts a heater in response to the recommendation." The specification shows app screens and says "any suitable software" may be used; no algorithm is disclosed. The user asks only whether the claims are patent-eligible.
**Analysis.**
1. 101, claim 1: receiving, analyzing, and displaying information on generic devices is the classic abstract-idea pattern (`eligibility-and-utility.md`); step two finds only generic components. High risk.
2. 101, claim 2: closing the loop to physical actuation (dosing, heating) is the patentee's best argument for a practical application (*Diehr*-style control of a physical process), but "networked pump" and "heater" are generic and the claim states results, not how. Moderate risk.
3. The dispositive issue the user did not ask about: "means for" presumptively invokes 112(f). For computer-implemented functions the corresponding structure is the disclosed algorithm; "any suitable software" is no algorithm, so the claims are likely indefinite under 112(b) (*Aristocrat*; *Williamson*; `claims-and-construction.md`). Say so in one line even though the question was 101.
4. Forum note: indefiniteness cannot be an IPR ground, but a claim the Board cannot construe may block an IPR on the merits; in court and PGR it is a direct invalidity ground.
**Trap.** Answering only the question asked when a different defect controls; or assuming 112(f) narrowing helps 101 (with no algorithm there is no structure to narrow to).

### I-10. Sued over a process you have run secretly for years
**Facts.** Since 2012 the client has made a dental bleaching gel in its Ohio plant by a secret process: mix A, B, and C, then add a purified catalyst heated to 160-170 °C. It has sold the gel publicly since 2012. A competitor's AIA patent (filed 2019-05-01, granted 2021) claims (1) "a composition comprising A, B, C, and an unpurified catalyst" and (2) "a method comprising mixing A, B, and C; heating a catalyst to about 145 °C; and combining."
**Analysis.**
1. Claim 1: "unpurified" is a specific limitation; a purified catalyst does not meet it literally, and treating purified as equivalent would erase the word (vitiation, `infringement.md`). Low literal and DOE risk.
2. Claim 2: whether 160-170 °C is "about 145 °C" is construction first (the specification and prosecution decide the range), then DOE if no estoppel attaches.
3. Prior art from the client's own activity: its public gel sales are prior art against claim 1 only if the gel is the claimed composition (it is not: purified catalyst), so at most a 103 reference. Its secret process is not public, and a third party's secret commercial use has traditionally not been prior art against another's patent (*W.L. Gore*, `novelty-and-bars.md`); AIA status is unsettled: flag, do not promise.
4. § 273 is the real shield for claim 2: good-faith commercial use of the process in the US, beginning at least a year before the EFD, in a patent issued after 2011-09-16. The client must prove it by clear and convincing evidence; the defense is personal, tied to the specific subject matter and sites, and transferable only with the whole business (273(b), (e)). Whether it reaches claim 1 (a composition sold as the end product rather than "used in a manufacturing or other commercial process") is doubtful [review].
5. Mirror image: had the *client* patented its own process in 2019, its own sales since 2012 of a product made by the secret process would bar its process patent (*Metallizing*; *Celanese v. ITC*, Fed. Cir. 2024, AIA; cert. denied 2025).
**Trap.** Calling the client's secret process "invalidating prior art"; forgetting § 273; or stretching § 273 to a product claim it may not cover.

### I-11. A supply-install-license deal more than a year before filing
**Facts.** On 2020-11-02 a water-treatment company signed a contract with a city utility: the company installs its proprietary ozone-dosing skid at its own cost, licenses the utility to use the technology, and the utility buys a minimum volume of treatment chemicals from it each year. After installation the utility runs a 15-day acceptance test of the skid's integration with its existing control system, and the company may adjust the skid within 30 days. The company filed an application (AIA) on 2022-01-14 claiming the skid and a dosing method; the patent issued 2026-08-04. A competitor started using a similar skid in 2023-06 and was served with an infringement complaint on 2026-11-02.
**Analysis.**
1. On sale: whether there was a commercial sale or offer is judged by general contract law (`novelty-and-bars.md`, *Pfaff*, *Group One*). A bare license to a process may not be a sale of the invention (*In re Kollar*), but this deal installs the claimed apparatus for consideration (the chemical purchase commitment) and licenses its use: a commercial transaction in the claimed skid. Secrecy does not matter (*Helsinn*).
2. Ready for patenting: the contract describes a developed system; the acceptance test concerns integration with the utility's unclaimed control system, not the claimed features, so it does not make the sale experimental.
3. Date: 2020-11-02 is more than a year before 2022-01-14, so no grace period applies. The apparatus claims face a strong on-sale challenge; the method claims need a separate look (performing or offering to perform a process for pay, `novelty-and-bars.md` on process sales) [review].
4. § 273 is unavailable to the competitor (use began after the EFD).
5. Forum: an on-sale ground is available in court or PGR (open until 2027-05-04), never in IPR. IPR window: 2027-05-05 to 2027-11-02.
**Trap.** "It was only a license," "the testing makes it experimental," or pleading the on-sale bar in an IPR.

### I-12. A deceptive-purpose method, an accidental web posting, and a long-time secret user
**Facts.** A patent filed 2012-12-03 (pre-AIA; granted 2015) claims "a method of making a reconditioned lead-acid battery pass a load test, comprising (a) adding additive P to the electrolyte immediately before testing; and (b) applying a 30-second surface-charge pulse 24 hours later." The specification says the point is that worn batteries pass the standard dealer test, and mentions lab calibration. The client, a battery reconditioner, has used the method in its back room since 2007; customers never knew. On 2012-04-02 the client accidentally put its process notes on an unlinked page of its website for three days; server logs show no outside visits, but search engines could index it. A 1999 trade article reports forklift-battery refurbishers adding additive Q (which chemists use interchangeably with P) before testing and pulsing later. Unconfirmed rumor says an overseas shop did it earlier.
**Analysis.**
1. Utility: a deceptive or unsavory purpose does not defeat utility (*Juicy Whip*, `eligibility-and-utility.md`). Not a promising defense.
2. Web page: public accessibility, not proof of actual viewing, is the test; indexing and searchability favor accessibility, three days and no links cut against (*Klopfenstein* factors, `novelty-and-bars.md`). Under pre-AIA law it is 102(a) art at most (within the year before filing), which the patentee can try to antedate with proof of earlier invention.
3. Client's secret use since 2007: not a public use, and a third party's secret commercial use is not prior art against another's patent (*Gore*). As prior invention under pre-AIA 102(g)(2), years of deliberate secrecy invite a finding of suppression or concealment.
4. Overseas rumor: pre-AIA public use counts only "in this country," and undocumented use cannot be corroborated. Drop it.
5. Obviousness: the 1999 article plus known interchangeability of Q and P is a strong predictable-substitution case (*KSR*); forklift and automotive lead-acid batteries share a field.
6. Infringement: the client performs every step; direct infringement is straightforward.
7. § 273: the patent issued after 2011-09-16; the client's good-faith US commercial use began in 2007, more than a year before the 2012 EFD. This is likely the client's strongest defense (clear and convincing proof; personal; limited to the specific process).
**Trap.** Arguing lack of utility; treating "nobody visited the page" as decisive; overlooking § 273 while chasing invalidity.

### I-13. A distributed inspection system: who "uses" it, and where
**Facts.** An AIA patent (filed 2019-03-04, granted 2023-06-13) claims "a system for inspecting the interior of a pipeline, to be carried by a crawler, comprising: (a) a structured-light projector; (b) a camera generating images; and (c) a processor analyzing the images to determine defect characteristics." The specification adds that the system "may be used in other industries where surfaces require inspection." Accused A installs projectors, cameras, and storage on utilities' crawlers; the utilities run them in US pipelines and upload images to A's servers in Texas, where A analyzes them. Since 2024 A also inspects ship ballast tanks with the same rig. Accused B runs its own crawlers and sends images for analysis to a contractor in Canada on some jobs and California on others. In 2018-10 A showed a 60-second animation of the concept to about 25 engineers at an industry meeting. The patentee sells unmarked crawler kits and sued in 2026-02.
**Analysis.**
1. Preamble: "interior of a pipeline" and "carried by a crawler" may limit if they define the invention (*Catalina*). Ballast tanks are not pipelines: no literal coverage if limiting; DOE possible.
2. Dedication: a generic "other industries" sentence does not dedicate any specific unclaimed alternative; dedication needs a disclosure specific enough to identify the alternative (*SanDisk v. Kingston*, 695 F.3d 1348 (Fed. Cir. 2012); *Johnson & Johnston*).
3. Use of a system: a party uses a system when it puts the system as a whole into service, controlling it and obtaining benefit from it (*Centillion*, `infringement.md`). A customer that initiates the process and receives the results can be the user even if another party runs a component. Making requires assembling the whole combination; installing part of it is not making.
4. Cross-border: for a system, use occurs where control is exercised and beneficial use obtained, so B's Canadian processing does not defeat US use (*NTP v. Research In Motion*, 418 F.3d 1282 (Fed. Cir. 2005)). A method claim would differ: every step must be performed in the US.
5. The 2018 animation, five months before the EFD by a third party, is 102(a)(1) art under "otherwise available to the public" if it enabled the claimed system. A brief video is a weak "printed publication," so it is unlikely to support an IPR; the PGR window closed 2024-03-13, so court is the forum.
6. Damages: the patentee sells unmarked kits and asserts apparatus claims, so damages begin only with actual notice or the complaint (287(a)); § 286 limits the lookback to 2020-02.
**Trap.** "The processor is abroad, so no infringement" (true for methods, not systems); "nobody makes the whole system, so no infringement" (use); treating a generic sentence as dedication; forgetting marking.

### I-14. Exporting one component of a kit claim
**Facts.** Claim: "A kit for assembling a racing drone, comprising: a plurality of frame parts in a case; and a SpiralLock torque driver in the case," with the novel driver defined in detail in the specification. Exporter X makes exact replicas of the driver in Ohio and ships them, alone, to an assembler in Mexico, which packs them with frame parts and sells kits in Latin America. X knows of the patent.
**Analysis.**
1. 271(a): no one makes, sells, or uses the claimed kit in the US.
2. 271(f)(1): requires "all or a substantial portion of the components"; a single component does not qualify (*Life Technologies v. Promega*, 580 U.S. 140 (2017), `infringement.md`).
3. 271(f)(2): requires a component "especially made or especially adapted" and "not a staple article ... suitable for substantial noninfringing use." A torque driver can be used as a driver outside any kit, so it likely has substantial noninfringing uses. Knowledge and intent are met but do not cure that.
4. Any kits imported into the US infringe under 271(a).
5. Drafting lesson for Phase 3: claim the novel component by itself in an independent claim, then the kit; foreign lost profits for 271(f) infringement are possible (*WesternGeco*, `remedies.md`).
**Trap.** Assuming 271(f)(2) applies whenever a novel component is exported.

### I-15. Refilling single-use cartridges
**Facts.** Claim 1: "A dishwasher cleaning cartridge comprising a dissolvable-membrane housing and an enzyme tablet sealed inside." Claim 2: "A method of cleaning comprising: (a) loading an enzyme tablet into a dissolvable-membrane housing; (b) running a dishwasher with the loaded housing; and (c) discarding the spent housing." A café first bought cartridges from an unlicensed overseas maker, then from the patentee (box: "single use only"). The café mails each spent cartridge frame to refiller R, which fits a new membrane and tablet and sends it back; the café runs it.
**Analysis.**
1. Unlicensed cartridges: no authorized first sale, so nothing is exhausted. The café's use infringes claim 1 (direct infringement is strict liability); R "makes" claim 1 when it refills.
2. Patentee's cartridges: the authorized sale exhausts the patent in those items, and a "single use only" notice does not avoid exhaustion (it may support a contract claim) (*Impression Products*, `defenses.md`). Replacing spent, unpatented consumables is permissible repair (*Aro*; *Jazz Photo*, refurbished single-use cameras, first sale in the US). But if the claimed housing itself is essentially rebuilt (new membrane = new housing), it may be reconstruction, which infringes [review: what part survives].
3. Claim 2: R performs step (a), the café (b) and (c). A party that contracts with another to perform a step is liable for that step (*Akamai*), so the café can directly infringe claim 2. An authorized sale of an item that substantially embodies the method can exhaust it for that item (*Quanta*, `defenses.md`), which helps only for cartridges the patentee sold and only if refilling is repair.
4. R faces contributory (271(c)) and induced (271(b)) liability for refills sent to the café.
**Trap.** Believing the "single use" label defeats exhaustion, or that every refill is repair; missing the contracting route to method liability.

### I-16. Supervised field tests, a prototype sale, and functional claims
**Facts.** Under a written 2023-01-10 agreement, an inventor tested ultrasonic dish-cleaning prototypes at a café: each ran one day while the inventor watched and logged results. The final design (a 40 kHz, 300 W transducer array) was built 2023-06-01 and ran daily at the café through 2023-08-01 with excellent results. On 2023-08-15 the inventor sold that prototype to the café for $1, on a promise to use it only there. The inventor filed 2024-07-01: claim 1 "a dish-cleaning unit comprising a stainless tank with two compartments and a cleaning element including an acoustic emitter"; claim 2 "a dish-cleaning unit comprising a stainless tank with two compartments and means for cleaning dishes"; claim 3 "the unit of claim 2, wherein the means comprises a transducer array operating at about 38-42 kHz and 300 W." The examiner rejects all claims over (A) a third-party article of 2024-04-30 describing a four-compartment tank with a 60 kHz, 350 W transducer, and (B) the inventor's public use before 2023-07-01. Later an appliance company sells retrofit kits with a 40 kHz, 300 W array and instructions for converting ordinary sinks.
**Analysis.**
1. (B) Public use before the critical date (2023-07-01): the January-May tests have the hallmarks of experimental use (written agreement, inventor control, monitoring, records, testing the claimed features) (*City of Elizabeth*, `novelty-and-bars.md`). Once the design was finished and working (2023-06-01), further use is not experimental: experimental use cannot negate a public use once the invention has been reduced to practice (*In re Omeprazole Patent Litig.*, 536 F.3d 1361, 1372 (Fed. Cir. 2008); *Clock Spring, L.P. v. Wrapmaster, Inc.*, 560 F.3d 1317 (Fed. Cir. 2009)). The June 2023 café use is therefore a public use more than a year before filing, and it embodies claim 3. Whether experimental use survives at all under AIA 102(a)(1) is undecided: flag it. The 2023-08-15 sale is within the year and excepted (102(b)(1)(A)).
2. (A) The article is within the year and by a third party. The 102(b)(1)(B) shield needs an earlier inventor *public* disclosure of the *same* subject matter; the article's four compartments and 60 kHz differ, so it is not shielded.
3. Claim 1: "two compartments" in a "comprising" claim may or may not read on four (construction: does "two" mean exactly two here?). "Acoustic emitter" names structure, so 112(f) likely does not apply (*Williamson*). If "two" means "at least two," the article anticipates.
4. Claim 2: "means for cleaning dishes" invokes 112(f): it covers the disclosed 40 kHz, 300 W array and equivalents. For anticipation the article must show the identical function and an identical or equivalent structure; 60 kHz, 350 W is a fact question. Claim 3's 38-42 kHz range excludes 60 kHz: not anticipated by (A), but barred by the June public use.
5. Retrofit kits: kit sellers do not make the claimed unit; users who convert sinks do. The kit seller faces contributory infringement (a component especially adapted, with no substantial noninfringing use) and inducement (instructions plus knowledge of the patent; willful blindness suffices, *Global-Tech*; a good-faith belief of invalidity is no defense, *Commil*; `infringement.md`).
**Trap.** Treating all supervised use as experimental (it stops at reduction to practice); assuming an earlier, different inventor use shields a third-party article; missing 112(f) in claim 2 and how it changes anticipation.

### I-17. An after-arising equivalent, an amended limitation, and an adjacent-field reference
**Facts.** A US application filed 2020-03-02 claims priority to an EP filing of 2019-04-01; it issued 2026-06-02. Claim: "An insulated shipping container comprising: (a) double steel walls; (b) a frame; (c) aerogel fill between the walls; and (d) a fluorosilicone gasket between the walls and the frame forming an airtight seal." As filed, (d) read "an elastomeric gasket." The examiner combined a 2016 article on aerogel in cold rooms with a Japanese application published 2009 teaching polymer draft strips (which can be, but are not said to be, elastomeric). The applicant argued the Japanese reference did not necessarily teach an elastomer, amended (d) to fluorosilicone, and argued fluorosilicone resists cold cracking better than other polymers. A new search finds a 2018 journal article on fluorosilicone freezer-door seals. The accused container (complaint served 2026-11-02) uses fumed-silica fill, which became cheap in 2025 and is now used interchangeably with aerogel, and a rigid PTFE gasket (not an elastomer) that seals airtight.
**Analysis.**
1. Element (c): no literal match. (c) was never amended, so no *Festo* presumption. Equivalence is judged at the time of infringement, so interchangeability that arose after filing supports it (*Warner-Jenkinson*, `infringement.md`). Check ensnarement: a hypothetical claim covering fumed silica must not read on the prior art.
2. Element (d): no literal match. The narrowing amendment over prior art raises the *Festo* presumption; the argument that fluorosilicone beats other polymers adds argument-based estoppel; PTFE is not even within the original "elastomeric" scope. DOE for (d) is very unlikely. One missing element means no infringement (all-elements rule).
3. Validity: the 2016 article, the 2009 Japanese publication, and the 2018 freezer-seal article all predate the 2019 EP priority. The freezer-seal article is outside shipping containers but reasonably pertinent to airtight cold sealing (analogous art, `obviousness.md`). Note the trap variant: a foreign publication dated after the priority date (for example 2020-01) would not be prior art if priority is valid.
4. Priority check: the US filing came within 12 months of the EP filing, but the EP application must itself describe a *fluorosilicone* gasket. If it said only "elastomeric," claim 1's EFD is 2020-03-02 and anything published in between becomes art (100(i)).
5. Forum: PGR open until 2027-03-02; IPR window 2027-03-03 to 2027-11-02 (315(b)).
**Trap.** Measuring interchangeability at filing; applying *Festo* to an unamended limitation (or ignoring it for the amended one); assuming the priority date covers a limitation the foreign filing never described.

### I-18. A remote-monitoring method performed partly by the patient and partly abroad
**Facts.** Claim: "A method for remotely monitoring sleep apnea, comprising: (a) attaching an adhesive sensor patch to a patient's chest; (b) detecting respiratory effort with the patch; (c) transmitting the data to a remote server; (d) analyzing the data on the server to identify apnea events; and (e) sending an alert to a clinician when an event is identified." The accused company sells patches to consumers; each patch uploads automatically through the user's phone to servers in Ireland, which analyze the data; clinicians who subscribe receive alerts. Users attach the patches themselves.
**Analysis.**
1. Actors: the patient performs (a); the company's device and firmware perform (b) and (c); the company's servers perform (d) and (e). The patient's step can be attributed to the company if it conditions use of its service on attaching the patch as instructed and sets the manner and timing (*Akamai*).
2. Territory: a method is used in the US only if every step is performed in the US (*NTP v. Research In Motion*). Step (d) runs in Ireland, so there is no 271(a) direct infringement of this method claim, and therefore no inducement either. Ask whether any US server performs the analysis.
3. Contrast: a system claim could be infringed where control is exercised and beneficial use obtained (*NTP*); a claim written to the server-side steps alone would avoid the patient-step problem.
4. Drafting lesson for Phase 3: include single-actor claims and system claims; do not rely only on a multi-actor, multi-country method.
**Trap.** Forgetting the territorial rule for method claims, or assuming the patient's step alone defeats infringement.

### I-19. An amendment that cured indefiniteness still creates estoppel
**Facts.** A pre-AIA patent filed 2012-05-14 claims a method: maintaining a messaging platform with users; receiving a message containing a parcel tracking number; automatically converting the number into a hyperlink; and displaying the message to recipients, the link opening the shipment's status. The examiner rejected "social message" as indefinite; the applicant changed it to "in-app direct message," and the claim was allowed. The accused online marketplace has public seller-review forums whose posts auto-link tracking numbers; it has no direct messaging.
**Analysis.**
1. 101: auto-linking an identifier in a message to display related information, on generic components, is high risk under *Alice*.
2. Construction: forum posts are not "in-app direct messages." No literal infringement.
3. DOE: a narrowing amendment made to satisfy any patentability requirement, including 112(b), raises the *Festo* presumption; public posts were a foreseeable alternative to direct messages. DOE is likely barred. The amendment narrowed the claim, even though it replaced a vaguer phrase.
4. 112(f): the steps recite acts ("receiving," "converting," "displaying"); step-plus-function treatment needs "step for" language without the acts (`claims-and-construction.md`). Not 112(f).
5. Pre-AIA validity rules; term to 2032-05-14 plus PTA.
**Trap.** Calling the amendment "broadening" because the new phrase is clearer; inventing 112(f) for ordinary method steps.

### I-20. Numeric ranges, an unamended medium, a named alternative, and imported products
**Facts.** An AIA patent (filed 2018-11-20, granted 2021-12-21) claims, as amended: "A process for pretreating coffee beans before roasting, comprising: (a) immersing the beans in water; (b) applying ultrasound at 20-40 kHz for 30-90 seconds; (c) such that bean temperature rises less than 2 °C; (d) performed before roasting." Step (b) originally read "applying ultrasound"; it was narrowed to overcome a 2015 paper using 100 kHz to sterilize roasted beans, and (c) replaced an indefinite "low temperature rise." The specification says the process "may be applied to cocoa beans and other seeds." Accused A uses water at 18 kHz for 120 seconds; B uses salt brine at 30 kHz for 60 seconds; C treats cocoa beans in water at 30 kHz for 60 seconds. A US roaster also imports beans pretreated in Colombia by the claimed process.
**Analysis.**
1. A: outside both ranges. The ranges were added to avoid prior art, so *Festo* presumptively bars equivalents, and stretching a bounded range to 18 kHz and 120 seconds would erase the bounds (vitiation). Low risk for A.
2. B: (a) was never amended, so no *Festo* presumption for "water." First construe "water" (does an aqueous brine meet it?); if not, function-way-result likely favors equivalence. B is the highest-risk competitor.
3. C: the claim covers coffee beans; cocoa is named in the specification but not claimed, so it is dedicated to the public and DOE cannot reach it (*Johnson & Johnston*).
4. Importer: importing, selling, or using in the US a product made abroad by a patented process infringes (271(g)), unless the product was "materially changed by subsequent processes" or became a trivial component of another product; whether later roasting counts is a question to flag [review]. Retail sellers of the product face no remedy unless there is no adequate remedy against the importer (271(g)).
5. Validity: the 2015 paper (100 kHz, roasted beans) does not anticipate the amended claim; obviousness turns on whether optimizing frequency and time was routine (`obviousness.md`, ranges).
**Trap.** Treating all three competitors alike; applying *Festo* to the unamended "water"; overlooking the dedicated alternative and 271(g).

### I-21. A business practice and a game of chance on a kiosk
**Facts.** A client wants patents on two parking-kiosk features. Claim 1: "A parking kiosk comprising: payment means; a ticket printer; an input for registering a prepaid bundle of N sessions tied to a card number; storage holding the bundle data; and a processor that, when the card is tapped, decrements the bundle instead of charging." Claim 2: "A parking kiosk with a front panel, comprising: payment means; a ticket printer; a ring of LEDs with numbered covers; a driver circuit lighting the LEDs in sequence after each payment; and a bonus circuit producing a win signal when the sequence stops at a preset LED, whereby a free session is issued." The specification has a flowchart for claim 1 and circuit diagrams for claim 2.
**Analysis.**
1. Claim 1: a prepaid discount bundle is a fundamental economic practice implemented on a generic kiosk, storage, and processor: high 101 risk (*Alice*).
2. Claim 2: a chance-based promotional reward is a way of organizing human activity (compare *In re Smith*, 815 F.3d 816 (Fed. Cir. 2016): a method of conducting a wagering game with a standard deck of cards held directed to an abstract idea); sequentially lit LEDs and functional "circuits" add little as claimed. Eligibility improves only if the specification supports a specific hardware improvement that the claim recites.
3. 112(f): "payment means" invokes 112(f) (structure: the disclosed card reader and equivalents). "Driver circuit" and "bonus circuit" may or may not be structural names to a skilled reader (*Williamson*) [review].
4. Advice: identify any real technical improvement and claim it; otherwise say plainly that patent protection is doubtful and that other protection is outside this skill's scope.
**Trap.** Believing hardware labels make a business or game method eligible; overlooking 112(f) in "means" elements.

### I-22. Disclosed abroad before filing: what the US grace period does and does not save
**Facts.** A US startup demonstrates its battery-swap cart at a German trade fair on 2026-03-10, without an NDA, handing out spec sheets. It files a US provisional on 2026-09-01 and plans a PCT application on 2027-09-01. A Chinese competitor posted a video of a similar cart on 2026-06-15. An investor asks whether the company can still patent in the US and Europe.
**Analysis.**
1. US, claims supported by the provisional: the trade-fair demo is within a year before the provisional, so it is excepted (102(b)(1)(A)); location abroad does not matter.
2. US, claims added later: a claim first supported in a later filing gets the later EFD. Any claim with an EFD after 2027-03-10 loses the exception, and the demo becomes prior art against it. Put all new matter on file by 2027-03-10.
3. Competitor video (2026-06-15, before the provisional): prior art unless 102(b)(1)(B) applies; the inventor's earlier public demo shields only the identical subject matter (D-14).
4. Europe and other absolute-novelty systems: the company's own demo is likely novelty-destroying; Paris or PCT priority from the 2026-09-01 provisional cannot cure a disclosure that predates it. Give these facts and refer to local counsel (SKILL.md US-utility scope).
5. Deadlines: PCT and any US nonprovisional claiming the provisional by 2027-09-01 (a Wednesday). Banner both the US clock and the foreign-rights loss in every output (SKILL.md date-screen requirements).
**Trap.** Telling the client the US grace period protects foreign rights, or that claims added later keep the grace-period exception.

## Part 4. How a strong answer is structured (output rules for the skill)

The top-graded answers shared a shape. The weaker parts of those same answers show what to avoid. Rewritten as rules for this skill's reports:

1. **Bottom line first, then a roadmap.** Open with a one- or two-sentence outlook per question, then list the issues in the order they will be taken. (Matches SKILL.md "Bottom line first.")
2. **Timeline before law.** Put a dated event table at the top of any validity or PTAB analysis, and state which law governs each claim before applying it. Most lost points came from a date or regime error, not from bad doctrine.
3. **Rule, then application, then conclusion, for each issue.** State the controlling rule with its authority (statute, CFR, MPEP, case), apply the specific facts, and end with a one-line outlook. Never apply a rule that has not been stated.
4. **Element-by-element, always.** Parse every claim into lettered elements. Construe disputed terms first, then compare. Use a table: element, accused feature or reference passage, literal (met / not met / unknown / construction-dependent), DOE (available / barred, and why). This is what `scripts/chart.py` is for.
5. **Classify each reference before using it.** For each piece of prior art give: category (patent, printed publication, public use, on sale, otherwise available, 102(a)(2)), date, which exception might apply, and which forum can use it (court, PGR, IPR). The best answers did this reference by reference; the weakest skipped straight to "obvious."
6. **Both sides, then a call.** For every close question, give the strongest argument for each side, then say which is likelier and why. One-sided advocacy and "it could go either way" both lost points.
7. **Alternative holdings.** When the outcome turns on a construction or an unknown fact, analyze both branches: "if the preamble limits, ...; if not, ...." Do not stop at the first fork.
8. **Name the missing fact.** State exactly which fact is missing, why it matters, and how it flips the outcome (the provisional's text, the server location, who holds title, the date a document became accessible). Then ask for it (SKILL.md "No silent supplement").
9. **Run the limits on DOE in order.** Literal first; then DOE; then check prosecution history estoppel, argument-based estoppel, disclosure-dedication, vitiation, and ensnarement. Strong answers ran these limits; weak ones found "equivalence" and stopped.
10. **Infringement is actor by actor and place by place.** Identify who performs each step and where. Then run direct infringement, attribution (*Akamai*), inducement and contribution, and territorial rules (271(f), 271(g), *NTP*).
11. **Separate validity from infringement, and patentability from FTO.** Separate sections with separate outlooks (SKILL.md Phase 2 rule). Do not let an invalidity view leak into the infringement chart, or the reverse.
12. **End with forum and remedies.** Close with the practical options and their windows: PGR or IPR dates, 315(b), design-around, license, § 273, marking and § 286 limits, injunction factors (*eBay*). The best answers closed with a recommendation tied to dates.
13. **Short answers stay short.** For a pure date or eligibility-of-reference question: the answer, the one-line rule with cite, and the trap avoided. No essay.
14. **Assumptions are explicit.** If facts are inconsistent or missing, say what was assumed and why, and mark it `[review]`.
15. **No invented doctrine.** Do not attach 112(f) to method steps without "step for," do not call best mode an invalidity ground, do not swear behind AIA art, do not use pre-AIA conception for AIA filings. These were the most common errors even in strong answers.

## Part 5. Checklist for the skill

Run before any output that involves dates, validity, infringement, or PTAB options. Each item maps to drills above.

**Dates and governing law**
- Build the event timeline first; never compute from memory. Use `scripts/state.py` for the bar clock and `scripts/_dates.py` for month math and the weekend/holiday roll.
- Decide AIA or pre-AIA for every claim; check whether the application "contains or contained at any time" a claim with an EFD on or after 2013-03-16 (D-19).
- Compute EFD claim by claim; confirm each priority document describes the relied-on limitation (D-3, I-17).
- Measure the grace period from the earliest public availability, not submission or print date; roll a weekend or holiday end (D-13, D-24).
- Test 102(b)(1)(B) and 102(b)(2)(B) whenever an inventor disclosure precedes a third-party disclosure or filing; require a *public* disclosure (*Sanho*) of the *same* subject matter (D-14, D-22).
- Date 102(a)(2) references by their earliest describing US or foreign priority (102(d)); never treat a foreign-only application as 102(a)(2) art; use *Hilmer* only for pre-AIA claims (D-15, D-16).
- Check Paris priority: filed within 12 months, restorable within 2 more months if unintentional, and actually claimed on time (D-17, D-18).
- Consider "neither party" as a possible outcome in every priority contest (D-23).

**Term, reissue, damages**
- Compute term from the earliest US nonprovisional (or PCT) filing claimed under 120/121/365(c)/386(c); ignore foreign and provisional priority; add PTA; cap at any terminal-disclaimer date; check maintenance-fee lapse (D-1 to D-4).
- Keep reissue to the original term; measure the broadening window from grant of the patent being reissued; flag intervening rights for new or amended claims (D-5 to D-7).
- Apply § 286 as a lookback, not a deadline to sue; check marking for apparatus claims and provisional-rights conditions (D-7, D-8, I-13).

**PTAB and forum**
- For AIA patents: PGR "not later than" 9 months from grant; IPR only "after" that date or after an instituted PGR terminates. For pre-AIA patents: IPR any time after grant, no PGR (D-9, D-10, I-5).
- Count 315(b) from service of the complaint; check 315(a)/325(a) for prior DJ actions (D-11, D-12).
- Filter every ground by forum: IPR takes only 102/103 on patents and printed publications; sales, public use, 101, 112, and inequitable conduct need PGR or court (D-26, I-5, I-11).
- Check 325(d) (same art already before the Office), parallel reexam, and current discretionary-denial practice in `ptab.md`; mark `[review]`.

**Prior art categories**
- Distinguish third-party secret use (traditionally not prior art) from the patentee's own secret commercial exploitation (a bar; *Celanese* under the AIA) (I-10).
- Treat contract-manufacturing purchases as presumptively not on-sale (*Medicines Co.*); treat bundled install-and-license deals for consideration as likely sales (I-6, I-11).
- End experimental use at reduction to practice; flag AIA survival as undecided (I-16).
- Run a public-accessibility analysis for talks, posters, slides, web files, confidential manuals, cover dates, and investor decks; under the AIA also ask "otherwise available to the public" (I-4, I-12, I-13).
- Ask whether each obviousness reference is analogous art, and whether interchangeability was known (I-5, I-12, I-17).

**Claims, construction, and 112**
- Construe disputed terms before comparing: preambles (antecedent basis, defines the invention), words of degree, numeric ranges, transitional phrases, product-by-process limits (I-1, I-3, I-6, I-13).
- Check 112(f) whenever a claim uses "means," a nonce word plus function, or functional software language; require an algorithm for computer functions; do not apply it to method steps that recite acts (I-9, I-16, I-19).
- Raise a controlling 112 defect even if the user asked only about 101 (I-9).
- Do not treat best mode as an invalidity ground in litigation (282(b)(3)(A)) (I-6).

**101**
- Ask what the claimed advance is; a machine or device claim can still be directed to an abstract idea (I-7, I-8, I-21).
- Separate treatment claims (often eligible) from diagnostic or correlation claims (high risk) (I-2).
- Never rescue eligibility with unclaimed specification detail (I-1).

**Infringement**
- Chart element by element; one missing element ends literal infringement (I-17).
- Before any DOE conclusion, check in order: amendment estoppel (including 112 amendments), argument-based estoppel, dedication, vitiation, ensnarement; measure equivalence at the time of infringement (I-4, I-17, I-19, I-20).
- List who performs each step and where; run *Akamai* attribution; for methods require every step in the US; for systems apply *Centillion* and *NTP* (I-1, I-13, I-18).
- For indirect infringement, confirm a single direct infringer, knowledge of the patent, and (for 271(c)) no substantial noninfringing use (I-2, I-16).
- Check 271(f) and 271(g) whenever components cross borders or products made by a process are imported (I-14, I-20).
- Check exhaustion (authorized sale only; post-sale notices do not avoid it) and repair vs reconstruction (I-15).

**Defenses and remedies**
- Check § 273 whenever the accused practiced commercially before the EFD: at least 1 year before, good faith, US, clear and convincing; personal and scope-limited; patents issued on or after 2011-09-16 (I-4, I-6, I-10, I-12).
- Remember method-only patents need no marking (I-1).
- Close every analysis with dated options (PGR, IPR, design-around, license) and a list of missing facts.

**Output discipline**
- Use the Part 4 structure. Give candid conditional assessments with scope, evidence and uncertainty. Cite cases only as this folder states them; anything else goes through SKILL.md source-verification requirements or is tagged `[memory – verify]`.
