# How to use this file

Load it at **Phase 1 Check A** (statutory bar), at **Phase 2** whenever a reference's prior-art status or date is in play, in **invalidity mode** (public use, on sale, publication, 102(a)(2), pre-AIA 102(g)), in **prosecution** (1.130 declarations, Rule 131 for pre-AIA cases, 102(b)(2)(C) common ownership), and in **portfolio** work on older patents.

**Division of labor.** Statute text, MPEP pin cites, and the 102 chart template live in [`../novelty-and-obviousness.md`](../novelty-and-obviousness.md). Deadlines and fee-side dates live in [`../deadlines.md`](../deadlines.md). The bar clock is computed by `scripts/state.py bar`. Anticipation mechanics (one reference, every element, inherency) are in `anticipation.md`. Provisional support and written description are in `specification-112.md`. The §273 prior-user defense is in `defenses.md`. This file covers the cases behind the bars and priority rules, where each rule stops, and the date traps.

**Four framing points the cases force.**
1. **Two statutes are live.** AIA 102 governs claims with an effective filing date (EFD) on or after March 16, 2013. Pre-AIA 102 governs older patents that are still in force (the last of them expire in the early-to-mid 2030s, later with term adjustment) and supplies 102(g) for "mixed" applications. Pick the statute claim by claim before analyzing anything.
2. **The inventor's own conduct is the most common bar.** Most bar cases are about what the inventor or the inventor's company did: gave a device away, sold or offered it, used it commercially in secret, showed it at a trade show, posted it. Ask about all of these, in the inventor's own terms, before searching.
3. **"Public" does not mean what it sounds like.** Under the case law, a single unrestricted use, a hidden use, a confidential sale, or a poster shown for a few days can bar a patent. Never translate "public use" or "on sale" into plain English for the user without the case-law meaning.
4. **Dates decide.** Bars run from the claim's EFD, computed per claim, with weekend and holiday rules, priority chains, and transition rules. Compute; never eyeball.

**Regime triage (run first, record in `state.json`).**

## 1. Public use — AIA 102(a)(1); pre-AIA 102(b)

### Egbert v. Lippmann, 104 U.S. (14 Otto) 333 (1881) · CB p. 199
- **Facts:** Inventor made corset springs for his friend (later his wife), who wore them for years. He imposed no restriction and patented eleven years later, by which time the design was in general use.
- **Holding / rule:** One embodiment given or sold to one person, to be used without "limitation or restriction, or injunction of secrecy," is a public use, even if the use is hidden from view and known to one person. A use made in good faith to test the invention is not a public use.
- **Nuance:** "Public" here means not restricted and not secret, not "seen by the public." The invention was complete; she used it as intended; nothing was being tested. Justice Miller's dissent: a hidden use that teaches no one is not "public." Facts that would flip it: an express or implied confidentiality obligation and inventor control (see *Moleculon*, below), or genuine testing of claimed features (*City of Elizabeth*).
- **Status:** Good law for pre-AIA 102(b); the Federal Circuit still cites it for the point that even a limited disclosure can be public, while holding that an express or implied confidentiality obligation defeats public use (*Barry v. Medtronic*, 914 F.3d 1310 (Fed. Cir. 2019), citing *Delano Farms Co. v. Cal. Table Grape Comm'n*, 778 F.3d 1243 (Fed. Cir. 2015)). Under AIA 102(a)(1), whether a non-informing hidden use still counts is **open**: the USPTO reads AIA public use as limited to uses "available to the public" (MPEP 2152.02(c)) but also says the AIA term has the same scope as pre-AIA case law; no court has decided the point for secret non-sale uses. As of 2026-11-18.
- **Skill rule:** Lite search Check A: treat any pre-filing gift, loan, or sale of a working embodiment to anyone without a written or clearly implied confidentiality obligation as a public use that starts the clock, even if the device was never seen by the public. Never accept "only one person used it" or "it was hidden inside the product" as a reason to clear the bar.

**Note cases (public use)**
- *Moleculon Research Corp. v. CBS, Inc.*, 793 F.2d 1261 (Fed. Cir. 1986) · CB pp. 204, 228 — inventor's personal use of a puzzle prototype and brief showing to a boss and colleagues was not public use because he kept control; a written confidentiality agreement is not required. Also: assigning patent rights in the invention is not putting the invention on sale. **Skill rule:** when the user has no NDA, ask who saw or used it, the relationship, and whether the inventor kept control; record those facts rather than concluding "barred" or "safe."
- *Hall v. Macneale*, 107 U.S. 90 (1883) · CB pp. 203, 206 — bolts hidden inside safes were in public use because sold safes were used without restriction. *New Railhead Mfg. v. Vermeer Mfg.*, 298 F.3d 1290 (Fed. Cir. 2002) · CB p. 203 — drilling method used underground, unseen, was a public use (and the provisional did not support the claims, so the use fell outside the grace period; see §8). *W.L. Gore & Assocs. v. Garlock*, 721 F.2d 1540 (Fed. Cir. 1983) — non-secret use of a process in ordinary commercial production is public use. *Rosaire v. Baroid*, 218 F.2d 72 (5th Cir. 1955) — similar for underground methods. **Skill rule:** never treat a process or internal component as safe because the customer cannot see it.
- *TP Labs., Inc. v. Professional Positioners, Inc.*, 724 F.2d 965 (Fed. Cir. 1984) · CB pp. 203, 254 — what bars is a public use or sale, not public knowledge of the invention (see also §4).
- *Delano Farms Co. v. Cal. Table Grape Comm'n*, 778 F.3d 1243 (Fed. Cir. 2015) (post-casebook; not in CB) — giving plants to a cousin who was told to keep them to themselves was not a public use (as described in *Minerva*, No. 2021-2246, quoting 778 F.3d at 1248).

### Metallizing Engineering Co. v. Kenyon Bearing & Auto Parts Co., 153 F.2d 516 (2d Cir. 1946) · CB p. 204
- **Facts:** Inventor used his secret metal-conditioning process commercially to rebuild customers' worn parts for more than a year before filing. Customers could not learn the process from the parts.
- **Holding / rule:** An inventor who exploits the invention competitively, even in secret, for more than the grace period forfeits the right to a patent. The inventor must choose secrecy or a patent, not secrecy first and a patent later. L. Hand framed it as a condition on the inventor's right: no competitive exploitation "after it is ready for patenting."
- **Nuance:** Forfeiture applies to the **inventor's own** commercial exploitation. A **third party's** secret commercial use is not prior art against someone else (*Gillman v. Stern*, §7). Private, non-commercial use for the inventor's own enjoyment is not forfeiture. Hand overruled his own *Peerless Roll Leaf* (secret machine whose output was sold) as having confused the two doctrines.
- **Status:** Adopted by the Federal Circuit (*D.L. Auld Co. v. Chroma Graphics*, 714 F.2d 1144 (Fed. Cir. 1983); *Woodland Trust v. Flowertree Nursery*, 148 F.3d 1368 (Fed. Cir. 1998), which *Helsinn* cites for the rule that an inventor's secret commercial use may be a public use or sale). **Extended to the AIA on-sale bar:** *Celanese Int'l Corp. v. ITC*, 111 F.4th 1338, No. 2022-1827 (Fed. Cir. Aug. 12, 2024), held that pre-critical-date sales of products made by a secret process still bar a patent on that process under AIA 102(a)(1); cert. denied, No. 24-635 (Apr. 28, 2025). The USPTO's 2013 view that the AIA ended secret-use and secret-sale prior art (78 Fed. Reg. 11059) is overtaken for sales by *Helsinn* and *Celanese*; for secret **non-sale** commercial use (no product or service sold), no appellate court has ruled under the AIA. As of 2026-11-18.
- **Skill rule:** Lite search Check A: ask whether the user has sold products made with, or services performed using, the method or machine; if yes, date the first such sale or offer as the trigger even though the method itself stayed secret. In "mine" mode and trade-secret discussions, warn that a company cannot run a secret process commercially for more than a year and then patent it.

**Note cases (forfeiture line)**
- *D.L. Auld Co. v. Chroma Graphics Corp.*, 714 F.2d 1144 (Fed. Cir. 1983) · CB p. 209 — the patentee's offer to sell products made by the secret method more than a year before filing barred the method patent; a third party's sale of products of its own secret method would not bar a different inventor.
- *Pennock v. Dialogue*, 27 U.S. (2 Pet.) 1 (1829); *Shaw v. Cooper*, 32 U.S. (7 Pet.) 292 (1833); *Kendall v. Winsor*, 62 U.S. (21 How.) 322 (1858) · CB pp. 205-206, 262 — the historical roots: an inventor may not profit from public sale and use and then patent; acquiescence in others' use can forfeit; an inventor may abandon by express statement or conduct, but stolen and published knowledge does not by itself abandon. Pre-AIA 102(c) (abandonment) did not survive the AIA.
- *Macbeth-Evans Glass*, 246 F. 695 (6th Cir. 1917); *Grasselli Chemical*, 26 F.2d 305 (2d Cir. 1928); *Allinson Mfg.*, 21 F.2d 22 (8th Cir. 1927) — string cites in *Metallizing* for sale of a secret process's product as a bar.

## 2. On sale — AIA 102(a)(1); pre-AIA 102(b)

### Pfaff v. Wells Electronics, Inc., 525 U.S. 55 (1998) · discussed CB pp. 216-217, 226-231 (note case, central)
- **Rule:** The on-sale bar applies when, before the critical date, the claimed invention was (1) the subject of a commercial offer for sale and (2) ready for patenting, shown by reduction to practice or by drawings or descriptions specific enough to enable a skilled person. Replaced the Federal Circuit's "totality of the circumstances" test. An inventor can understand and control the timing of the first commercial marketing; a sale experimental in character is different.
- **Skill rule:** Lite search Check A: date the first **offer** (quote, proposal, purchase-order acceptance, RFP response, preorder page), not the first delivery, and ask whether enabling drawings or specs existed then; a product that was never built can still be "ready for patenting."

### The Medicines Co. v. Hospira, Inc., 827 F.3d 1363 (Fed. Cir. 2016) (en banc) · CB p. 211
- **Facts:** MedCo, with no plants of its own, paid a contract manufacturer about $347,500 to make three commercial-size validation batches of its improved drug (market value over $20 million) before the critical date. MedCo kept title; the batches sat in quarantine; the manufacturer had no right to sell.
- **Holding / rule:** To be "on sale," the invention must be the subject of a commercial sale or offer that bears the general marks of a sale under the UCC (passing of title for a price). A contract manufacturer's sale of **manufacturing services** to the inventor, where neither title nor the right to market passes to the supplier, is not an invalidating sale. Stockpiling, alone, is preparation, not commercial marketing. Commercial benefit is not the test.
- **Nuance:** No blanket "supplier exception": a supplier transaction can still be a sale if the supplier holds title, gets blanket authority to market the product or disclose the process, or the product is sold at full market value. Confidentiality and lack of title transfer weigh against commercialization but are not decisive ("not talismanic"). Product-by-process claims are product claims for validity, so making the product is not selling the claimed invention. Distinguished the method cases (*Metallizing*, *D.L. Auld*, *Plumtree*, *Scaltech*), where the inventor charged customers to perform the claimed process. Overruled language in *Brasseler*, *Special Devices*, and *Hamilton Beach* to the extent inconsistent.
- **Status:** Good law; consistent with *Helsinn* (which concerned an inventor's sale to a distributor, not a purchase of services). **Remand history:** on remand the panel held MedCo's separate distribution agreement with its exclusive distributor **was** a commercial offer for sale, affirmed that the invention was ready for patenting, and sent back whether that offer covered the claimed invention (*Medicines Co. v. Hospira*, Nos. 2014-1469, -1504 (Fed. Cir. Feb. 6, 2018)). As of 2026-11-18.
- **Skill rule:** Lite search Check A: when a user outsourced manufacturing before filing, ask who held title, whether the supplier could sell to others, the price relative to market value, and confidentiality terms; record them and flag "on-sale risk: fact-dependent." Separately, always ask about distribution, supply, and licensing agreements with downstream partners: those are the transactions that bar (*Helsinn*, *Medicines II*).

### Helsinn Healthcare S.A. v. Teva Pharmaceuticals USA, Inc., 139 S. Ct. 628 (2019) · supplemental opinion (CB pp. 198, 231 discuss the Federal Circuit decision and the cert grant)
- **Facts:** In 2001, about two years before its January 30, 2003 provisional, Helsinn signed license and supply agreements with a U.S. distributor for a 0.25 mg palonosetron dose, with confidentiality obligations. The deals were announced, but the dose was not disclosed. The patent in suit, filed in May 2013 in the 2003 family, was governed by the AIA.
- **Holding / rule:** Congress did not change the meaning of "on sale" when it reenacted the phrase in AIA 102(a)(1). An inventor's sale "to a third party who is obligated to keep the invention confidential can qualify as prior art." A sale need not make the invention available to the public. Adding "or otherwise available to the public" is a catch-all for material outside the listed categories; it does not narrow "on sale."
- **Nuance:** Unanimous (Thomas, J.). The Court relied on the settled-construction canon and cited *Pfaff*, *City of Elizabeth*, and the Federal Circuit's secret-sale cases (*Special Devices*, *Woodland Trust*). Helsinn did not contest that the deal was "on sale" under pre-AIA law, so the opinion does not revisit *Pfaff*. The Court did **not** decide whether secret **public use** survives the AIA, and the Federal Circuit had expressly declined to decide it (855 F.3d 1356, 1368-69 (Fed. Cir. 2017)). The holding is framed around an inventor's sale; third-party secret sales are a separate question.
- **Status:** Good law; applied and extended in *Celanese v. ITC* (Fed. Cir. 2024) to products of a secret process. MPEP 2152.02(d) adopts it and confirms no geographic limit on AIA sales. As of 2026-11-18.
- **Skill rule:** Lite search Check A: treat any pre-filing commercial agreement for the invention (supply, distribution, license-with-supply, purchase order, signed quote), confidential or not, U.S. or foreign, as a potential on-sale trigger; never clear a bar because "the deal was under NDA" or "nobody knew the details." Note for the user that a confidential sale may still leave foreign rights intact (foreign law differs), but the U.S. clock runs.

**Note cases (on sale)**
- *Group One, Ltd. v. Hallmark Cards, Inc.*, 254 F.3d 1041 (Fed. Cir. 2001) · CB p. 217 — whether there was a commercial offer is Federal Circuit law under general contract principles (UCC as guide); only an offer the other side could turn into a binding contract by simple acceptance counts. *Linear Tech. v. Micrel*, 275 F.3d 1040 (Fed. Cir. 2001) · CB p. 223 — announcing that a product will soon be available is preparation, not an offer. *Intel v. ITC*, 946 F.2d 821 (Fed. Cir. 1991); *In re Kollar*, 286 F.3d 1326 (Fed. Cir. 2002) · CB pp. 219-222 — preparations are not offers; licensing rights to a process with expectation of future royalties is not a sale of the process, but a "license" that is really a sale of an embodiment (standard software license) can be. *Trading Techs. v. eSpeed*, 595 F.3d 1340 (Fed. Cir. 2010) — must be a sale in the commercial-law sense. **Skill rule:** when the user describes a "quote," "proposal," or "LOI," ask for the document and check price, quantity, delivery, and acceptance terms; a signed purchase order accepted by the inventor ends the inquiry.
- *Special Devices, Inc. v. OEA, Inc.*, 270 F.3d 1353 (Fed. Cir. 2001); *In re Caveney*, 761 F.2d 671 (Fed. Cir. 1985); *Gould Inc. v. United States*, 579 F.2d 571 (Ct. Cl. 1978); *Hobbs v. AEC*, 451 F.2d 849 (5th Cir. 1971) · CB p. 221 — a single sale, a secret military contract, or a secret stockpiling purchase can bar.
- *Brasseler, U.S.A. I v. Stryker Sales*, 182 F.3d 888 (Fed. Cir. 1999) · CB pp. 224, 256-257 — 3,000 saws ordered from a supplier for resale, invoiced as a sale: barred; contrasted with an inventor paying a fabricator for a few samples. *Hamilton Beach Brands v. Sunbeam*, 726 F.3d 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2013) · CB pp. 229-230 — an overseas supplier's offer to the patentee to make the slow-cooker was a commercial offer (Reyna, J., dissenting); limited by *Medicines* en banc to the extent inconsistent.
- *Ferag AG v. Quipp Inc.*, 45 F.3d 1562 (Fed. Cir. 1995) · CB p. 229 — sale to a 50%-owned exclusive distributor the patentee could not control was a sale between separate entities; the test for related parties is whether the seller controls the buyer so the invention stays out of public hands. *Continental Can v. Monsanto*, 948 F.2d 1264 (Fed. Cir. 1991); *Baker Oil Tools v. Geo Vann*, 828 F.2d 1558 (Fed. Cir. 1987) · CB p. 229 — confidential customer development project that never bore commercial fruit was not on sale (pre-*Pfaff* factor analysis; use cautiously).
- *UMC Elecs. v. United States*, 816 F.2d 647 (Fed. Cir. 1987) · CB p. 226 — pre-*Pfaff* "substantially complete / obvious" test; **superseded by *Pfaff*.** *Scaltech v. Retec/Tetra*, 269 F.3d 1321 (Fed. Cir. 2001) (and 156 F.3d 1193 (1998)) · CB p. 228 — a process offered for sale that inherently met every claim limitation was on sale even if the parties did not recognize it. *Tec Air v. Denso*, 192 F.3d 1353 (Fed. Cir. 1999) — the thing offered must fall within the claim. *Robotic Vision Sys. v. View Eng'g*, 249 F.3d 1307 (Fed. Cir. 2001) · CB p. 228 — inventor's enabling description to a skilled coworker made the invention ready for patenting before the critical date. **Skill rule:** compare what was offered against the claims as drafted (and as they may be amended): a later claim can read on a product offered years earlier.
- *Plumtree Software v. Datamize*, 473 F.3d 1152 (Fed. Cir. 2006); *Atlanta Attachment v. Leggett & Platt*, 516 F.3d 1361 (Fed. Cir. 2008) · CB pp. 219, 221 — offering to perform a claimed method for pay triggers the bar; the core concern is commercializing beyond the statutory period.

**Post-casebook on-sale cases (details in "Post-casebook developments")**: *Junker v. Medical Components* (Fed. Cir. 2022) (a letter labeled "quotation" was a commercial offer); *Celanese v. ITC* (Fed. Cir. 2024) (AIA keeps the *Metallizing* secret-process sale bar).

## 3. Printed publication and "otherwise available to the public"

### In re Lister, 583 F.3d 1307 (Fed. Cir. 2009) · CB p. 232
- **Facts:** Inventor deposited his golf-method manuscript with the Copyright Office in 1994 and filed his patent application in 1996. The Copyright Office's own catalog was searchable only by author or first title word; Westlaw and Dialog later let users keyword-search titles. No evidence showed when the commercial databases added the entry.
- **Holding / rule:** A reference is a printed publication if it was disseminated or otherwise made available so that persons interested and skilled in the art, exercising reasonable diligence, can locate it. Two parts: (1) could an interested researcher **examine** it, and (2) could one **learn it exists and is relevant** before the critical date. Inspection-only access (no copies), travel to Washington, and zero actual readers do not defeat accessibility. A title-keyword database in which a diligent searcher would try "golf" with "handicap" made it findable. But the date it became findable was unproven, so the rejection was vacated.
- **Nuance:** The diligent-researcher standard is not "one search using claim words": a reasonable researcher tries several keyword combinations, including the applicant's own vocabulary. Accessibility needs a **date**: without evidence of when indexing happened, or of the indexer's general practice (as in *In re Hall*), there is no prima facie case and no burden shift. An undated reference is no reference. The government conceded the author/first-word catalog alone was not enough.
- **Status:** Good law. Under the AIA the same accessibility analysis applies to "printed publication" and informs the "otherwise available to the public" catch-all (MPEP 2152.02(e)). As of 2026-11-18.
- **Skill rule:** Deep search and invalidity: every non-patent reference needs a provable accessibility date (archive capture, catalog record, indexing date, conference date, declaration on general practice); log "date basis" next to each NPL item and downgrade undated items to `[date unproven]`, never to prior art. Prosecution: when an examiner relies on a database record, ask what proves the record existed before the critical date (*Lister*).

**Note cases (printed publication)**
- *In re Hall*, 781 F.2d 897 (Fed. Cir. 1986) · CB p. 235 — a single thesis in a German university library, cataloged and shelved, was a printed publication; evidence of the library's general cataloging practice can supply the date. *In re Bayer*, 568 F.2d 1357 (CCPA 1978) · CB p. 236 — uncataloged, unshelved thesis known only to the committee was not. *In re Cronyn*, 890 F.2d 1158 (Fed. Cir. 1989) · CB p. 236 — theses indexed only by student name on cards were not meaningfully indexed, so not accessible. **Skill rule:** for theses and library items, get the catalog record and its subject indexing, not just the deposit date.
- *In re Klopfenstein*, 380 F.3d 1345 (Fed. Cir. 2004) · CB p. 243 — 14-slide poster displayed for about three days at a professional meeting, with no bar on copying or notes, was a printed publication even though never distributed or indexed; factors: audience expertise, display length, expectation of confidentiality, ease of copying. *Massachusetts Inst. of Tech. v. AB Fortia*, 774 F.2d 1104 (Fed. Cir. 1985) · CB p. 243 — paper orally presented to about 500 skilled people, with at least six copies handed out, was a publication. **Skill rule:** lite search intake: ask about posters, talks, slide decks, and handouts at meetings, and treat them as dated publications unless a confidentiality restriction was announced.
- *SRI Int'l v. Internet Security Systems*, 511 F.3d 1186 (Fed. Cir. 2008) · CB p. 243 — paper on an FTP server for seven days was not shown accessible on summary judgment where an outsider would have needed the exact path. *Suffolk Techs. v. AOL*, 752 F.3d 1358 (Fed. Cir. 2014) · CB p. 244 — Usenet post in a hierarchical newsgroup that drew replies within a week was a publication even though unsearchable. *Kyocera Wireless v. ITC*, 545 F.3d 1340 (Fed. Cir. 2008) · CB p. 236 — source of the "interested and ordinarily skilled ... exercising reasonable diligence" formulation. *Northern Telecom v. Datapoint*, 908 F.2d 931 (Fed. Cir. 1990) · CB p. 237 — reports held in a library open only to authorized persons were not generally available. **Skill rule:** for web material, establish both posting date and findability (links, indexing, audience); a public GitHub release, arXiv preprint, or app-store listing is usually both; an unlinked URL or an access-controlled repo may be neither.
- *Cordis Corp. v. Boston Scientific*, 561 F.3d 1319 (Fed. Cir. 2009) (not in CB; cited in *Sanho*) — academic monographs given to a few colleagues and institutions under academic norms of confidentiality were not publicly accessible despite no legal confidentiality obligation. Contrast *Weber v. Provisur* (2024, post-casebook): operating manuals distributed to customers were printed publications despite restrictive notices.
- **Catch-all.** AIA 102(a)(1) adds "otherwise available to the public." MPEP 2152.02(e) treats it as a catch-all for disclosures that fit no listed category (examples: thesis, poster, internet posting) and focuses on availability, not the form. Per *Helsinn*, it does not shrink the listed categories. **Skill rule:** do not spend effort labeling a disclosure (publication vs. public use vs. catch-all) before asking whether it was available to the public on a provable date.

## 4. Experimental use (negates public use and on sale)

### City of Elizabeth v. American Nicholson Pavement Co., 97 U.S. (7 Otto) 126 (1877) · CB p. 249
- **Facts:** Nicholson laid a 75-foot stretch of his wooden pavement on a toll road in 1848 to test durability under heavy traffic, at his own expense, inspected it almost daily, called it an experiment, and filed six years later.
- **Holding / rule:** Use by the inventor, or by others under the inventor's direction, in good faith to test the invention or bring it to perfection is not a public use, even if the public sees it and benefits incidentally, and even if testing takes years because durability is the quality at stake. It becomes public use if the inventor lets others use it generally, with or without pay, or puts it on sale for such use. Using it for profit, not by way of experiment, beyond the grace period bars the patent.
- **Nuance:** Control and purpose decide it: the inventor kept the pavement under his own eyes and let no one else build it. Public knowledge of the construction is irrelevant; use or sale is what bars. The nature of the invention can require a public setting (a road can only be tested on a road). The same opinion says a use for profit, not experiment, beyond the statutory period bars (quoted in *Pfaff*).
- **Status:** Good law; quoted in *Pfaff* (1998) and cited in *Helsinn* (2019); the Federal Circuit applies the negation to both bars (*Barry v. Medtronic*, 914 F.3d 1310 (Fed. Cir. 2019)). Whether experimental use survives under AIA 102(a)(1) has not been decided by an appellate court; no case found holding otherwise. As of 2026-11-18.
- **Skill rule:** Lite search Check A: when the user says a public showing or customer trial was "just testing," ask what claimed feature was being tested, who controlled the units, whether records and feedback were kept, whether users were told it was experimental, whether anyone paid, and when testing ended; record the answers and still report the earliest date as the conservative trigger. Drafting: advise filing a provisional before any field trial instead of relying on the negation.

**Note cases (experimental use)**
- *TP Labs., Inc. v. Professional Positioners*, 724 F.2d 965 (Fed. Cir. 1984) · CB pp. 253-254 — experimental use is not a separate exception the patentee must prove; the one question is whether there was a public use, with the challenger bearing the burden of proof; the dentist-patient relationship supplied control and confidentiality.
- *In re Theis*, 610 F.2d 786 (CCPA 1979); *LaBounty Mfg. v. ITC*, 958 F.2d 1066 (Fed. Cir. 1992); *In re Brigance*, 792 F.2d 1103 (Fed. Cir. 1986) · CB p. 254 — experimentation on **unclaimed** features does not negate a bar. But *Grain Processing v. American Maize-Products*, 840 F.2d 902 (Fed. Cir. 1988) and *Manville Sales v. Paramount Systems*, 917 F.2d 544 (Fed. Cir. 1990) · CB pp. 254-256 accepted testing for general workability (food interactions; a highway luminaire's survival of a Wyoming winter at a rest area not yet open, with confidentiality notices) without those features being claimed. **Skill rule:** tie the claimed experiment to a claim limitation or to whether the invention works for its intended purpose; testing customer appeal or price does not count.
- *In re Smith*, 714 F.2d 1127 (Fed. Cir. 1983) · CB p. 256 — consumer in-home tests of a carpet deodorizer measuring purchase intent and pricing were market testing, so a public use. *Brasseler* (above) — large supplier order for resale is commercial, not experimental. **Skill rule:** treat beta programs, pilot customers, crowdfunding, and focus groups as commercial unless the facts show control over a test of claimed features.
- *EZ Dock v. Schafer Systems*, 276 F.3d 1347 (Fed. Cir. 2002) · CB pp. 230-231 — experimental use survives *Pfaff*; Linn, J., concurring, shifts focus to the purpose of the transaction. *Lough v. Brunswick*, 86 F.3d 1113 (Fed. Cir. 1996) · CB p. 231 — mentioned for contrast (inventor gave prototypes to others with no control or records; barred).
- *Barry v. Medtronic*, 914 F.3d 1310 (Fed. Cir. 2019) (post-casebook) — surgeon's use of his spinal-correction tools in patient surgeries, with follow-up to confirm results, was experimental and negated both bars despite his normal surgical fee; Prost, C.J., dissented in part.

## 5. Secret prior art: earlier-filed patents and applications — AIA 102(a)(2); pre-AIA 102(e)

### Alexander Milburn Co. v. Davis-Bournonville Co., 270 U.S. 390 (1926) · CB p. 245
- **Facts:** Clifford's application, filed before Whitford's, fully described Whitford's later-claimed invention but did not claim it; Clifford's patent issued after Whitford filed.
- **Holding / rule:** A patent's disclosure is prior art as of its **filing** date, even though secret until issue and even though unclaimed. Patent Office delay should not cut down the effect of what the earlier applicant did; the question is whether the earlier disclosure made it impossible for the later applicant to claim the invention, not who invented first.
- **Nuance:** Holmes assumed an uncommunicated private description in a drawer would not bar, and accepted the Office's practice of ignoring abandoned applications. The earlier application must actually describe the subject matter (whole contents, not just claims). Under both statutes, an application that never publishes or issues is not prior art under this rule.
- **Status:** Codified in pre-AIA 102(e) and now AIA 102(a)(2) plus 102(d). The AIA changed the date: 102(d) gives the reference its earliest U.S. or foreign priority date that describes the subject matter, overruling *Hilmer* for AIA cases. As of 2026-11-18.
- **Skill rule:** Deep search and Phase 1: the 18-month blind spot is real; say that unpublished applications filed in the last 18 months (longer with nonpublication requests) can later surface as prior art dated to their earliest filing. Analysis: for every patent reference, compute its 102(a)(2) date from the earliest priority document that describes the cited passage, and check that the priority document actually contains it (102(d)(2) text; *In re Riggs*, 2025, applied the same check under pre-AIA 102(e), see `../novelty-and-obviousness.md`).

**Note cases (102(a)(2)/(e))**
- *In re Hilmer*, 359 F.2d 859 (CCPA 1966) · CB p. 307 — under pre-AIA 102(e), a U.S. patent is prior art only from its **U.S.** filing date, not its foreign priority date. **Overruled for AIA cases by 102(d).** **Skill rule:** for a pre-AIA patent under analysis, date a foreign-priority reference at its U.S. filing date; for AIA, at the foreign date if the foreign document describes the subject matter.
- *In re Zletz*, 893 F.2d 319 (Fed. Cir. 1989); *In re McKellin*, 529 F.2d 1324 (CCPA 1976) · CB p. 271 — pre-AIA: a 102(e) reference can be antedated by Rule 131 unless it **claims** the same invention, in which case priority requires an interference.
- *Merck Serono S.A. v. Hopewell Pharma Ventures*, Nos. 2025-1210, -1211 (Fed. Cir. Oct. 30, 2025) (post-casebook) — pre-AIA: for a reference to be excluded as not "by another," the relied-on portions must reflect the collective work of the **same inventive entity** named in the patent; any mismatch, whether inventors are added or subtracted, makes the reference "by another." There, the patentee could not corroborate that one named inventor (De Luca) contributed to the relied-on passage of the earlier publication, so it stayed prior art. The court limited its views to the pre-AIA text.
- *Lynk Labs v. Samsung* (Fed. Cir. 2025) — a published application is pre-AIA 102(e) art as of its filing date in IPR; digested in `../novelty-and-obviousness.md`.
- Comparative (CB pp. 247-248): EPC Art. 54(3) treats earlier-filed, later-published European applications as state of the art for novelty only ("whole contents"); the U.S. uses them for obviousness too. Do not import EPO novelty-only treatment into U.S. analysis.

## 6. The AIA grace period and its exceptions — 102(b)(1), 102(b)(2)

**How the exceptions work (own summary; statute in `../novelty-and-obviousness.md` §3).**
- **(b)(1)(A) own-disclosure exception:** a disclosure made 1 year or less before the EFD by the inventor, a joint inventor, or someone who got the subject matter from them is not 102(a)(1) art. "Disclosure" covers the 102(a)(1) categories, so per *Helsinn* and *Sanho* it includes the inventor's own sales and offers, confidential or not.
- **(b)(1)(B) first-to-publicly-disclose shield:** a third party's 102(a)(1) disclosure within the year is not art if the **same subject matter** had earlier been **publicly disclosed** by the inventor (or one who got it from the inventor).
- **(b)(2)(A)-(C):** an earlier-filed U.S. patent or application is not 102(a)(2) art if its subject matter was obtained from the inventor, if the inventor publicly disclosed it before the reference's effective filing date, or if it and the claimed invention were commonly owned or subject to an obligation of assignment to the same person not later than the claim's EFD (joint research agreements count via 102(c)).
- **What the grace period does not do:** it never excuses an **independent** third party's earlier disclosure or filing; it does not protect foreign rights; and (b)(1)(B) shields only against the same subject matter, read narrowly by the USPTO.

**Casebook illustrations (own facts, same logic as CB pp. 197, 257-258).**
- Inventor publishes on day 0 and files on day 365: own publication excepted by (b)(1)(A).
- Inventor publishes day 0; an independent rival publishes the identical thing on day 30; inventor files day 365: rival's paper excepted by (b)(1)(B). If the rival also **filed** on day 60, the rival's application is excepted from 102(a)(2) by (b)(2)(B) because the inventor's public disclosure came first.
- Same, but the rival's paper discloses a close **variant** of the inventor's publication: under the USPTO's reading, the variant is prior art and can support a 103 rejection; the inventor's earlier publication does not shield it (MPEP 2153.02). A species publicly disclosed by the inventor shields a later third-party genus disclosure; a genus disclosed by the inventor does not shield a later species.

### Sanho Corp. v. Kaijet Technology International Ltd., 108 F.4th 1376 (Fed. Cir. 2024) (post-casebook; the leading appellate decision construing "publicly disclosed")
- **Facts:** Patentee relied on its inventor's private, non-confidential sale of a docking-hub product (arranged through private messages, before the Kuo application's filing date) to exclude Kuo under 102(b)(2)(B).
- **Holding / rule:** "Publicly disclosed" is narrower than "disclosed." It requires that "it is reasonable to conclude that the invention was made available to the public." A private sale that taught no one the claimed features is not a public disclosure, even without an NDA, so Kuo stayed prior art.
- **Nuance:** The court contrasted *Helsinn*: a sale can be a 102(a)(1) "disclosure" without making the invention available to the public, which is exactly why it is not automatically a "public" disclosure. It expressly did not decide what a sale must involve to publicly disclose subject matter, or whether the printed-publication accessibility standard applies. Its reasoning treats (b)(1)(B) and (b)(2)(B) alike.
- **Status:** Good law (precedential; no cert. petition found on the Supreme Court docket). As of 2026-11-18.
- **Skill rule:** Prosecution and invalidity: to use the first-to-publicly-disclose shield, require evidence that the inventor's earlier act made the claimed subject matter available to the public (publication, public demo showing the features, public product whose features are evident); a private sale, confidential pitch, or NDA demo starts the inventor's own clock but does not shield against third parties.

**Open questions the skill must flag, not resolve (as of 2026-11-18).**
1. **Secret non-sale use under AIA.** Whether an inventor's (or third party's) secret commercial use, with no sale, is "public use" under AIA 102(a)(1). *Helsinn* and *Celanese* reached only sales; the Federal Circuit declined to decide public use in *Helsinn*; USPTO says AIA public use is limited to uses available to the public.
2. **Obvious variants and (b)(1)(B).** The USPTO denies the shield for trivial or obvious variants (MPEP 2153.02; 77 Fed. Reg. 43759). No appellate court has tested that reading.
3. **What makes a sale or use a "public disclosure."** *Sanho* left the test open.
4. **Experimental use under AIA.** Not decided; assume courts will keep it, but do not promise it.
5. **(b)(1)(A) and (b)(2)(A) for co-authored papers.** A paper naming non-inventor co-authors needs a showing (1.130(a) declaration with explanation) that the relied-on part is the inventor's work or was obtained from the inventor; *Merck Serono* (pre-AIA) shows courts demand that the relied-on passage reflect exactly the named inventive entity.
6. **Third-party secret sales.** Pre-AIA, a third party's sale could bar (and a third party's secret process whose product was sold did not bar another inventor, per *D.L. Auld*). Under the AIA, whether a third party's confidential sale is prior art against an independent inventor is unsettled; flag it.

## 7. Pre-AIA prior invention, priority contests, and derivation — pre-AIA 102(a), (e), (f), (g); 35 U.S.C. 135, 291

**Why this still matters (as of 2026-11-18).** (1) Pre-AIA patents still in force are judged under pre-AIA 102: in litigation, all of it, including 102(g)(2) prior invention by another; in IPR and reexamination, only patents and printed publications, which the patent owner may antedate under pre-AIA 102(a)/(e) by proving an earlier invention date (Rule 131 in reexamination). (2) AIA applications that ever contained a pre-March 16, 2013 claim carry pre-AIA 102(g) for every claim (MPEP 2159.03). (3) Conception, corroboration, and communication rules from interference law drive **derivation** proceedings (35 U.S.C. 135; petition within 1 year after the earlier patent's grant or the earlier application's publication, whichever is first, 135(a)(2)) and derivation suits (35 U.S.C. 291), plus inventorship disputes. (4) The §273 prior-commercial-use defense needs dated, corroborated proof of commercial use at least one year before the patent's EFD or the patentee's 102(b) disclosure, whichever is earlier (273(a)(2)).

**Pre-AIA framework in brief.** 102(a): known or used by others in the U.S., or patented or published anywhere, before the applicant's **invention date**. 102(b): patented, published, or in public use or on sale in the U.S. more than one year before the U.S. filing date (a true bar; cannot be antedated). 102(c): abandonment. 102(d): own foreign patent on an application filed more than 12 months before the U.S. filing that issued before the U.S. filing (both conditions required). 102(e): earlier-filed U.S. patent or published application by another. 102(f): derivation. 102(g)(1) interference priority; 102(g)(2) prior invention in the U.S. by another not abandoned, suppressed, or concealed. Priority rule: first to reduce to practice wins, unless the first to conceive was diligent from just before the other's conception until his own reduction to practice (actual or constructive).

### Woodcock v. Parker, 30 F. Cas. 491 (C.C.D. Mass. 1813) (Story, J.) · CB p. 263
- **Facts:** Defendants claimed through Parker, who allegedly invented, used, and patented the machine before the plaintiff, a later independent inventor.
- **Holding / rule:** The first inventor who reduces to practice and patents keeps the right; a later independent inventor's patent cannot oust the first inventor or support an action against him. Left open whether an inventor who abandoned and never reduced to practice would defeat a later inventor.
- **Nuance:** Founding statement of first-to-invent: novelty measured from the invention date, not the filing date. Parker had put the machine into operation and patented it, so no abandonment issue.
- **Status:** Pre-AIA principle (codified in pre-AIA 102(a) and (g)). **Superseded by the AIA** for claims with EFD on or after March 16, 2013: invention dates are irrelevant, and an earlier secret inventor's only protection is the §273 prior-user defense. As of 2026-11-18.
- **Skill rule:** Portfolio and FTO: when a user says "we invented it first" against an AIA patent, explain that invention date is irrelevant under the AIA; route to prior-art status of their **public** activity, derivation (if the patentee got it from them), or §273 (if they commercially used it a year before the patentee's EFD or disclosure).

### Gillman v. Stern, 114 F.2d 28 (2d Cir. 1940) (L. Hand, J.) · CB p. 266
- **Facts:** Before the patentee, Haas built and used quilting "puffing" machines in a locked shop, swore employees to secrecy, and openly sold the quilted product; only selling agents saw the machine work.
- **Holding / rule:** A third party's secret use, even commercial and with the product sold openly, is neither a "public use" nor makes the user a "first inventor"; it is not prior art against another's patent. Earlier discarded experiments are abandoned and immaterial.
- **Nuance:** Hand separated two things: a **non-informing public use** (counts, *Hall v. Macneale*) and a **secret use** (does not). In *Metallizing* he kept *Gillman* as right: the forfeiture rule applies only to the patentee's own exploitation, not to a stranger's secret use.
- **Status:** Good law for pre-AIA 102(a)/(g) (courts read in a public-accessibility requirement: *Levi Strauss*, *National Tractor Pullers*, below). Under the AIA, a third party's secret use is likely not prior art (not "available to the public"; MPEP 2152.02(c)); the third party's remedy is §273. As of 2026-11-18.
- **Skill rule:** Invalidity mode: do not chart a competitor's secret internal use as prior art against someone else's patent (pre-AIA or AIA); look instead for anything that was public (sales literature, manuals, public products, publications). FTO: if the user is the secret prior user, raise §273 and preserve dated records.

**Note cases (prior knowledge and use)**
- *Levi Strauss & Co. v. Golden Trade*, 1995 WL 710822 (S.D.N.Y. 1995); *National Tractor Pullers Ass'n v. Watkins*, 205 U.S.P.Q. 892 (N.D. Ill. 1980) · CB p. 269 — pre-AIA 102(a) "known or used" means publicly accessible to the skilled; drawings on the underside of a tablecloth, later destroyed, were not prior knowledge.
- *Gayler v. Wilder*, 51 U.S. (10 How.) 477 (1851) · CB p. 267 — an earlier inventor who kept his work private does not defeat a later patentee. *Christiana v. Rice*, [1931] A.C. 770 (P.C.) · CB p. 269 — old Canadian law (secret foreign prior invention barred), now history.
- *Kendall v. Winsor* (1858) · CB p. 262 — pre-AIA 102(c) abandonment; gone under the AIA.
- **Pre-AIA 102(d)** (CB p. 263) — own foreign patent bar; both prongs needed (foreign application filed more than 12 months before the U.S. filing **and** foreign patent granted before the U.S. filing). Repealed by the AIA.
- **Rule 131** (CB pp. 270-271) — antedating a pre-AIA reference by proof of earlier reduction to practice, or earlier conception plus diligence, in the U.S., a NAFTA country, or a WTO country (subject to the 1993/1996 dates, per the rule text quoted in the casebook; check the current 37 CFR 1.131 text before relying on the country list). Not available against a statutory bar (pre-AIA 102(b)), or against a U.S. reference claiming the same invention (interference instead). Not available at all for AIA applications.

### Oka v. Youssefyeh, 849 F.2d 581 (Fed. Cir. 1988) · CB p. 276
- **Facts:** Interference over a genus of ACE-inhibitor compounds. Senior party Oka relied on an October 31, 1980 Japanese priority date. Junior party Youssefyeh had the idea of one class of compounds (2-indanyl) in February 1980 but no workable way to make them; by October 10, 1980 it had a method for a different compound, and first had the idea of a species in a second class (5-indanyl) in "the last week of October 1980."
- **Holding / rule:** Conception of a chemical compound requires both the idea of its structure and possession of an operative method of making it. A method later shown to work does not move the conception date back to when the method was known if the idea of the compound came later. Evidence placing an act within a period proves only the last day of that period (*Haultain v. DeWindt*). On a tie, the senior party wins.
- **Nuance:** Conception of a species within a genus can be conception of the genus, but each species needs both parts. Where making the compound is routine, describing it is enough. The casebook note criticizes converting the junior party's burden into a substantive tie-goes-to-senior rule.
- **Status:** Good law within pre-AIA priority practice; the conception standard remains the law for derivation and inventorship. As of 2026-11-18.
- **Skill rule:** Analysis and prosecution (pre-AIA patents, derivation, inventorship): when dating conception, require the complete idea **and** an operative way to make or practice it, corroborated; when a record says "late October" or "Q3," use the **last** day for the party relying on it, and the **first** day when the date is used against the user (for example, a bar trigger).

**Note cases (conception)**
- *Townsend v. Smith*, 36 F.2d 292 (CCPA 1929) · CB p. 277 — conception has a directing idea and a selected means. *Coleman v. Dines*, 754 F.2d 353 (Fed. Cir. 1985) · CB p. 279 — conception must be proved by corroborated disclosure of every feature of the count, detailed enough to enable; letters showing "intermingled" ideas did not show who was the "spark." *Haultain v. DeWindt*, 254 F.2d 141 (CCPA 1958) · CB p. 279 — last-day-of-period rule.
- *Amgen, Inc. v. Chugai Pharm.*, 927 F.2d 1200 (Fed. Cir. 1991) · CB p. 288 — where an inventor cannot define a gene except by its function, conception happens only with isolation (simultaneous conception and reduction to practice); a wish for any material with a property is not conception. **Skill rule:** for biologics and unpredictable arts, do not date conception before the structure was actually known.

### Scott v. Finney, 34 F.3d 1058 (Fed. Cir. 1994) · CB p. 281
- **Facts:** Junior party Scott relied on actual reduction to practice of a self-contained penile implant: a video of the prototype implanted in an anesthetized patient, inflating and deflating through the skin, plus leakage tests, proven materials, and expert testimony. The Board demanded testing under actual use conditions over time.
- **Holding / rule:** Actual reduction to practice requires showing that the embodiment works for its intended purpose; the testing needed depends on the invention's complexity and the problem solved, judged by common sense. Testing must show utility beyond a probability of failure, not beyond any possibility; commercial perfection and human-use trials are not required (FDA handles safety and efficacy). Very simple devices may need only construction.
- **Nuance:** Complex inventions with many variables may need tests closely replicating working conditions (*Elmore v. Schmitt*); simple ones little or none (*Sellner v. Solloway*). Only the new hydraulics needed testing because the materials and designs were already proven. Remanded on abandonment, suppression, or concealment.
- **Status:** Good law for actual reduction to practice under pre-AIA 102(g); the "works for its intended purpose" standard also appears in *Pfaff*'s ready-for-patenting prong and in experimental-use analysis. As of 2026-11-18.
- **Skill rule:** Lite search Check A: when deciding whether an invention was "ready for patenting" at a sale or offer, treat a working prototype tested enough to show it works for its purpose (not commercial readiness or regulatory approval) as a reduction to practice that starts the on-sale analysis.

**Note cases (reduction to practice)**
- *Travis v. Baker*, 137 F.2d 109 (CCPA 1943) · CB p. 280 — constructive reduction to practice requires an application enabling the full count. *In re Costello*, 717 F.2d 1346 (Fed. Cir. 1983) · CB p. 287 — an abandoned application is not a constructive reduction to practice (only evidence of conception). **Skill rule:** a provisional that is not followed up within 12 months gives no filing-date benefit; treat it as evidence of conception only.
- *King Instrument v. Otari*, 767 F.2d 853 (Fed. Cir. 1985); *Eastern Rotorcraft v. United States*, 384 F.2d 429 (Ct. Cl. 1967) · CB pp. 284-288 — simple devices: complete construction can show workability. *Elmore v. Schmitt*, 278 F.2d 510 (CCPA 1960); *Sellner v. Solloway*, 267 F.2d 321 (CCPA 1959); *Engelhardt v. Judd*, 369 F.2d 408 (CCPA 1966) · CB pp. 284-286 — testing scaled to complexity; animal tests can suffice for drugs.

### Gould v. Schawlow, 363 F.2d 908 (CCPA 1966) · CB p. 289
- **Facts:** Laser interference. Both sides relied on filing dates (constructive reduction to practice). Junior party Gould had to prove reasonable diligence from just before Schawlow's July 30, 1958 filing to his own April 6, 1959 filing; he offered general testimony that he worked on the laser every week, days off from his job, and conversations at beach parties.
- **Holding / rule:** The party charged with diligence must account for the **entire** critical period with specific, corroborated acts tied to reducing the invention to practice and dated; "no week passed without work" is not proof, and gaps within vaguely dated activity defeat diligence. An inventor need not quit his job, but must show what was done and when.
- **Nuance:** Attorney preparation of the application can be diligence if the case is taken up in reasonable order (later *Gould v. General Photonics*, 534 F. Supp. 399 (N.D. Cal. 1982), found the attorney diligent; *Mendenhall v. Astec*, 13 U.S.P.Q.2d 1913 (E.D. Tenn. 1988), aff'd, 887 F.2d 1094 (Fed. Cir. 1989), did not, where the attorney took later matters first, sat for a bar exam, and vacationed). Only the first-to-conceive, last-to-reduce party's diligence matters, and only from just before the rival's conception. The court noted Gould could simply have filed.
- **Status:** Good law in pre-AIA priority practice. As of 2026-11-18.
- **Skill rule:** Drafting and portfolio: advise inventors that a filing (even a provisional) is the cheapest proof of an invention date; for any pre-AIA or derivation dispute, build a dated, corroborated activity log covering every week of the critical period.

### Apotex USA, Inc. v. Merck & Co., 254 F.3d 1031 (Fed. Cir. 2001) · CB p. 297
- **Facts:** Merck made Vasotec tablets by a process it never patented, commercially from 1983, and later published the ingredients (French drug dictionary, 1988; Canadian product monograph, 1992-94) and narrated the process in a 1994 Canadian trial. Days later Apotex's executive conceived the same process and patented it; Apotex sued Merck.
- **Holding / rule:** Pre-AIA 102(g)(2) invalidates a patent if another made the invention in the U.S. first and did not abandon, suppress, or conceal it. The challenger must prove prior invention by clear and convincing evidence; the burden of **production** then shifts to the patentee to show suppression or concealment; the challenger keeps the burden of persuasion. Unreasonable delay in making the invention public can raise an inference of suppression, but a prior inventor who **resumes** activity and makes the invention public before the patentee's entry into the field is not barred (*Paulik v. Rizkalla*). "In this country" modifies only where the invention was made; foreign disclosures can rebut concealment.
- **Nuance:** A prior inventor need not file a patent application; failure to file is relevant mainly if it reflects a trade-secret choice. Commercial use precludes concealment only if it lets the public learn the invention (footnote; *Palmer v. Dudzik*). The patentee's own admissions that the process was obvious from the disclosures helped Merck.
- **Status:** Good law for pre-AIA 102(g); no AIA counterpart (a secret prior inventor's AIA remedy is §273). As of 2026-11-18.
- **Skill rule:** Invalidity mode, pre-AIA patents only: consider a 102(g)(2) prior-invention defense based on a third party's earlier U.S. commercial practice, and gather proof that the practice was made public before the patentee's conception; for AIA patents, drop 102(g) and evaluate §273 instead.

**Note cases (abandonment, suppression, concealment)**
- *Paulik v. Rizkalla*, 760 F.2d 1270 (Fed. Cir. 1985) (en banc) · CB pp. 304-305 — an inventor who sat on a reduced-to-practice invention can rely on renewed activity begun before the rival's earliest date and carried diligently to filing; Friedman, J., dissented (statute allows no redemption).
- *Peeler v. Miller*, 535 F.2d 647 (CCPA 1976) (4-year unexplained delay after reduction to practice); *Shindelar v. Holdeman*, 628 F.2d 1337 (CCPA 1980) (2 years 5 months unexplained) · CB p. 302 — inferred suppression. *Int'l Glass Co. v. United States*, 408 F.2d 395 (Ct. Cl. 1969); *Palmer v. Dudzik*, 481 F.2d 1377 (CCPA 1973) · CB p. 302-303 — no steps to make the invention public within a reasonable time means suppression. *Young v. Dworkin*, 489 F.2d 1277 (CCPA 1974) · CB p. 301 — in interferences, the second inventor bears the burden on suppression. *Fujikawa v. Wattanasin*, 93 F.3d 1559 (Fed. Cir. 1996) · CB p. 302 — two types: active concealment and inferred from delay. *Mason v. Hepburn*, 13 App. D.C. 86 (1898); *Woofter v. Carlson*, 367 F.2d 436 (CCPA 1966) · CB p. 309 — "spurring": a first inventor who files only after learning of a rival's application or product invites a finding of suppression.
- *Christie v. Seybold*, 55 F. 69 (6th Cir. 1893) (Taft, J.) · CB p. 274 — the classic statement of the priority and diligence rule; no race of diligence between the two.
- **Pre-AIA 102(f) and derivation** (CB pp. 307-308) — no AIA analog in 102; inventorship is enforced through 101/115, derivation proceedings (135), and 291 suits.

## 8. Date mechanics the skill must compute

Run these with `scripts/state.py bar` and record each input and its evidence. When an input is uncertain, compute both the conservative and the favorable answer and show both.

**8.1 Effective filing date (EFD), per claim** (35 U.S.C. 100(i); `../novelty-and-obviousness.md` §1)
- EFD = the earliest date of an application (provisional, foreign, parent, PCT) to which the claim is entitled, and a claim is entitled only if that earlier application supports it under 112(a) (written description and enablement; best mode excluded for 119(e)/120). Compute it **per claim**; dependent claims that add new matter get the later date.
- Provisional benefit (119(e)): nonprovisional or PCT within 12 months (rolled forward past weekends and D.C. federal holidays, 119(e)(3)); at least one inventor in common ("by an inventor or inventors named in the provisional"); specific reference in the ADS within the later of 4 months from filing or 16 months from the provisional (37 CFR 1.78(a)(4)); restoration if filed within 2 more months and the delay was unintentional (1.78(b)). Details in `../deadlines.md`.
- Foreign priority (119(a)-(d)): U.S. filing within 12 months of the earliest foreign filing for the same invention, restorable by 2 months (1.55(c)). Under the AIA the foreign date counts as the EFD for **all** of 102 (the old 119(a) proviso was struck by AIA 3(g)(6)). Under pre-AIA law it did **not** move the 102(b) critical date.
- Continuity (120/121/365(c)): requires copendency and a proper benefit claim. A break in copendency resets the EFD to the child's actual filing date, and the parent's own publication can then become prior art against the child.

**8.2 AIA grace window** (102(b)(1); MPEP 2153.01)
- An inventor-originated disclosure dated D is excepted for a claim with EFD E if D is 1 year or less before E. Equivalent preliminary filing-date screen = D + 12 months; if that day is a Saturday, Sunday, or D.C. federal holiday, it moves to the next business day (35 U.S.C. 21(b); MPEP 2153.01 citing *Ex parte Olah*).
- **Leap day:** a disclosure on Feb. 29 gives a raw anniversary that does not exist; `state.py` clamps to Feb. 28 (conservative). No case or MPEP rule found on this; report Feb. 28 as the conservative screening date and flag it.
- The window runs to the EFD of **the claim**, not the application. A claim supported only by the nonprovisional has a later EFD and may fall outside the window even if the provisional was timely.
- The window protects only against the inventor's own (or derived) disclosures and, via (b)(1)(B), against later third-party disclosures of the same subject matter after the inventor's **public** disclosure. It never covers an independent third party's earlier disclosure.

**8.3 Pre-AIA critical date** (pre-AIA 102(b))
- Critical date = 1 year before the **U.S.** filing date, counting 119(e) provisional and 120 parent dates (and the PCT international date for a U.S. national stage), but **not** a 119(a) foreign priority date. 21(b) applies (MPEP 2133 for pre-AIA).
- Public use and on-sale had to be **in this country** pre-AIA; publications and patents anywhere. AIA has no geographic limit (MPEP 2152.02(d)).
- Pre-AIA 102(a) and (e) are measured from the applicant's **invention date** (antedatable by Rule 131); 102(b) is not.

**8.4 Reference dates**
- AIA 102(a)(1) publication-type art: the date it became publicly accessible (online-first, not print issue; poster display date; catalog date for theses). Undated or unprovable means not usable (*Lister*).
- AIA 102(a)(2) U.S. patent or published application (and WIPO-published PCT designating the U.S., any language): the earliest filing date, U.S. or foreign, of an application that **describes** the relied-on subject matter (102(d)); check that the priority document actually contains it (102(d)(2); *In re Riggs* applied the same check under pre-AIA 102(e)).
- Pre-AIA 102(e): the **U.S.** filing date only (*Hilmer*); a PCT application gets its international filing date only if filed on or after Nov. 29, 2000, designated the U.S., and published in English (MPEP 2136.03). A published application is 102(e) art as of filing (*Lynk Labs*).
- A published application is **both** 102(a)(1) art (as of its publication date) and 102(a)(2) art (as of its effective filing date). Run each exception against the right date: (b)(2)(C) common ownership cures only the (a)(2) status, never the (a)(1) status of a publication more than a year before the EFD.

**8.5 AIA vs. pre-AIA status of the application** (AIA 3(n); MPEP 2159)
- AIA if it **contains or contained at any time** a claim with EFD on or after March 16, 2013, or ever claimed benefit of an application that did. Adding one unsupported claim, even briefly and even by preliminary amendment, converts the application permanently.
- Mixed (AIA plus a pre-2013-EFD claim): pre-AIA 102(g) applies to every claim (MPEP 2159.03).
- Transition statement under 37 CFR 1.55(k), 1.78(a)(6), or 1.78(d)(6) when a post-2013 application claiming pre-2013 priority contains or contained a post-2013-EFD claim.
- Example: the *Helsinn* '219 patent, filed in 2013 in a family dating to a 2003 provisional, was governed by the AIA (139 S. Ct. at 631).

**8.6 Evidence of dates**
- When a record places an act within a period ("March 2025," "Q3," "late October"), the party proving its own early date gets only the **last** day (*Oka*, *Haultain*). For bar triggers against the user, use the **first** day of the period until evidence narrows it.
- Online material: prefer server-side or third-party timestamps (archive captures, registry release dates, arXiv v1 date, app-store release history, journal "published online" date) over editable ones (file metadata, commit author dates, which can be backdated). Note the time zone when a date falls near midnight and use the earlier possible date for bar purposes (practice guidance, not case law).
- Submission to a journal, or confidential peer review, is not publication; posting a preprint is. An embargoed press release is published when released. A thesis with a library embargo becomes accessible when the embargo lifts and it is cataloged.

**8.7 Clocks after filing (for derivation and older patents)**
- Derivation petition: within 1 year after the earlier patent's grant or the earlier application's publication, whichever is earlier (135(a)(2)).
- Pre-AIA patents: last ones (filed before March 16, 2013, plus pre-AIA continuations) expire by about 2033 plus patent term adjustment, later for pharmaceutical term extensions; check each patent's actual term before treating it as pre-AIA history.

## Edge-case playbook

1. **The foreign priority date that rescues an AIA claim.** Facts: inventor publishes Jan. 10, 2025; files in Germany June 1, 2025; files in the U.S. May 29, 2026 claiming the German priority. Trap: "the U.S. filing is more than a year after the publication, so barred." Correct: under the AIA the EFD is June 1, 2025 (if the German filing supports the claims), the publication is within one year of the EFD, and 102(b)(1)(A) excepts it (the old 119(a) proviso is gone). The German and EPO rights are a separate, likely lost, question (absolute novelty). Under **pre-AIA** law the same facts were a 102(b) bar because foreign priority did not move the critical date. Cases/rules: 102(b)(1)(A); 35 U.S.C. 119(a) as amended by AIA 3(g)(6); MPEP 2153.01.
2. **The provisional that does not support the claim.** Facts: inventor demos the device publicly on day 0, files a thin provisional on day 300, and files the nonprovisional on day 600 with claims to features first described there. Trap: "provisional within the grace period, so safe." Correct: claims lacking provisional support get the day-600 EFD; the day-0 demo is more than a year earlier and bars those claims (public use or catch-all). Claims supported by the provisional keep day 300. Chart each claim against the provisional. *New Railhead*; 100(i); `specification-112.md`.
3. **Broken continuity.** Facts: parent application published in 2022; parent abandoned on March 1, 2025; continuation filed March 3, 2025. Trap: "continuation inherits the parent date." Correct: no copendency, so no 120 benefit; the EFD is March 3, 2025, and the parent's 2022 publication is the inventor's own disclosure more than a year earlier, so it is 102(a)(1) art against every claim. Check abandonment and filing dates to the day. 35 U.S.C. 120.
4. **"It was a confidential deal."** Facts: before filing, the company signed a supply agreement under NDA with a distributor for the product, with no public announcement. Trap: "secret sale isn't public, and the AIA requires public availability." Correct: an inventor's confidential sale can be prior art under AIA 102(a)(1); the clock started on the first commercial offer if the invention was ready for patenting. *Helsinn*; *Pfaff*.
5. **Secret process, public product.** Facts: a manufacturer has run a secret process for three years and sold the products; it now wants a process patent. Trap: "the process was never disclosed, so it's novel and not barred." Correct: the inventor's sales of products made by the secret process more than a year before filing bar the process patent, under pre-AIA law and the AIA. Trade secret it stays. *Metallizing*; *Celanese*; *D.L. Auld*.
6. **The competitor's secret process.** Facts: same as 5, but the secret process was run by a **third party**, and the user independently invented and wants to patent it. Trap: "prior use, so unpatentable." Correct: a third party's secret use is not prior art against an independent inventor (pre-AIA *Gillman*; AIA likely the same since it was not available to the public); the third party may have a §273 defense. A third party's confidential sales are an open AIA question; flag it. *Gillman*; *D.L. Auld*; 35 U.S.C. 273.
7. **Contract manufacturer vs. distributor.** Facts: pre-filing, the inventor paid a contract manufacturer to build validation batches (inventor kept title), and separately signed an exclusive distribution agreement. Trap: treating both the same (both barred, or both fine). Correct: paying for manufacturing services where title and marketing rights stay with the inventor is not a sale; the distribution agreement can be a commercial offer for sale. *Medicines Co.* (en banc 2016 and 2018 panel); *Helsinn*.
8. **"It was only a quote."** Facts: the inventor sent a customer a letter headed "quotation" with tiered prices for several sizes, shipping terms, and payment terms, but the customer had not yet picked a size or quantity, and no prototype had been built. Trap: "a quotation isn't an offer, and nothing was built." Correct: if the customer could accept it to form a contract, it is a commercial offer (each size and price tier can be a separate offer); enabling drawings make the invention ready for patenting without a prototype. *Junker*; *Pfaff*; *Group One*.
9. **Hidden inside the product.** Facts: the novel element is an internal spring, firmware routine, or underground step that no customer can see. Trap: "non-informing use isn't public." Correct: pre-AIA, unrestricted use of a sold or given embodiment is public use even if hidden; under the AIA, public use is said to require availability to the public, but sales of the product are still on-sale art regardless of visibility. Treat as barred-risk either way. *Egbert*; *Hall v. Macneale*; *New Railhead*; *Helsinn*.
10. **Beta testers and pilots.** Facts: 40 customers used beta units for six months before filing; the company collected bug reports and charged a discount price. Trap: "it was experimental." Correct: experimental use needs inventor control, testing of claimed features or workability, and records; paid betas, market-appeal testing, and uncontrolled users point to commercial use. Run the experimental-use questions; report the earliest beta date as the conservative trigger. *City of Elizabeth*; *In re Smith*; *Lough*; *Barry*.
11. **The trade-show booth.** Facts: working prototypes were shown and demonstrated at a major industry show, without NDAs, but visitors did not handle them. Trap: "display isn't use." Correct: showing a ready invention to industry members without confidentiality is public use; handling is not required, and FDA approval or later fine-tuning does not delay readiness. *Minerva*; *Klopfenstein* (posters).
12. **The poster or talk.** Facts: a researcher presented a poster at a conference 14 months before filing; no proceedings were printed. Trap: "never published." Correct: a poster displayed without copying restrictions to a skilled audience can be a printed publication; an oral talk with handouts can be too. Ask about slides, handouts, recordings, abstracts in the program. *Klopfenstein*; *MIT v. AB Fortia*.
13. **The undated web page or database record.** Facts: the examiner or an opposing expert relies on a web page or catalog entry with no reliable date. Trap: accepting it as prior art. Correct: accessibility must be shown as of a date before the EFD (or critical date); evidence of general indexing practice can supply it, mere existence cannot. *Lister*; *Hall*.
14. **The variant that slips past the shield.** Facts: inventor publicly discloses A+B+C on March 1; an independent rival publishes A+B+C' on April 1; inventor files Dec. 1. Trap: "inventor disclosed first, so the rival's paper is excluded." Correct: under the USPTO's reading, (b)(1)(B) shields only the same subject matter; the rival's variant is prior art and may support a 103 rejection (an inventor species shields a later genus, not the reverse). Untested in court; flag both readings. MPEP 2153.02; CB p. 257.
15. **A private sale as the "first public disclosure."** Facts: inventor privately sold one unit (no NDA) before a rival filed; the inventor relies on (b)(2)(B) to remove the rival's application. Trap: "a sale is a disclosure under *Helsinn*, so it counts as publicly disclosed." Correct: the private sale starts the inventor's own grace clock but is not a public disclosure; the rival's application stays 102(a)(2) art. *Sanho*.
16. **Common ownership cures (a)(2), not (a)(1).** Facts: a colleague at the same company filed a related application that published 15 months before the user's EFD. Trap: "same company, 102(b)(2)(C) removes it." Correct: (b)(2)(C) removes only 102(a)(2) status; the publication is 102(a)(1) art as of its publication date and more than a year old, and (b)(1)(A) does not apply because a different inventive entity disclosed it. Even if published within the year, it is still art unless the user shows the colleague obtained the subject matter from the user's inventor (1.130(a)) or the user's inventor publicly disclosed it first (1.130(b)). MPEP 2154.02(c); 102(b)(1)(A).
17. **Common ownership that came too late.** Facts: the user's company acquired the earlier-filing startup after the user's EFD. Trap: "we own both now." Correct: (b)(2)(C) requires common ownership or an obligation of assignment to the same person **not later than the claim's EFD**; employment invention-assignment agreements in force at the EFD can satisfy "obligation of assignment." 102(b)(2)(C).
18. **One unsupported claim converts the application to AIA.** Facts: a 2015 continuation in a 2010 family has all claims supported by the 2010 parent, but a preliminary amendment once added a claim with new matter, later canceled. Trap: "pre-AIA, so Rule 131 and pre-AIA 102 apply." Correct: an application that ever contained a post-March 16, 2013 EFD claim is AIA permanently (and pre-AIA 102(g) also applies to all claims as a mixed application). Check the full claim history in the file wrapper, not just the issued claims. AIA 3(n); MPEP 2159.02-.03; *Helsinn* ('219 patent governed by the AIA despite a 2003 provisional).
19. **"We invented first."** Facts: user conceived in 2019, kept it secret, and a competitor filed in 2020 and got a patent; user filed in 2021. Trap: "prove earlier invention and win." Correct: under the AIA, invention date is irrelevant; only a public disclosure before the competitor's filing (102(b)(2)(B)), derivation (135/291 with corroborated conception and communication), or §273 prior commercial use can help. Pre-AIA patents are different: 102(g)(2) and Rule 131 remain. *Woodcock*; *Gillman*; *Apotex*; 35 U.S.C. 273.
20. **Vague dates cut both ways.** Facts: inventor says the prototype was shown "sometime in spring 2025"; the provisional was filed April 20, 2026. Trap: picking the convenient end of the period ("May, so we're fine"). Correct: for the bar clock, use the earliest date the evidence allows (March 1, 2025) until documents narrow it; on that date the conservative screening date was Monday, March 2, 2026 (March 1, 2026 is a Sunday), so report "likely barred unless the showing was on or after April 20, 2025" and ask for the calendar, emails, or event records. For proving the user's own earlier invention date, only the last day of a stated period counts. *Oka*; *Haultain*; 21(b).
21. **Weekend anniversary.** Facts: a public demo on Saturday, March 14, 2026 means the one-year anniversary is Sunday, March 14, 2027. Trap: filing on Friday "to be safe" is fine, but declaring the bar run on Monday is wrong. Correct: filing on Monday, March 15, 2027 is timely under 21(b) as applied to the grace period (MPEP 2153.01). Still advise filing earlier: a disclosure date can later be proven earlier than believed.
22. **Pre-AIA public use abroad vs. AIA.** Facts: a pre-2013 patent; the inventor sold units in Japan two years before the U.S. filing, with no publication. Trap: applying AIA's worldwide rule. Correct: pre-AIA 102(b) public use and on-sale had to be in the U.S.; a foreign sale alone did not bar (check for foreign publications or patents, which did count). Under the AIA, the same foreign sale bars. Pre-AIA 102(b); MPEP 2152.02(d).
23. **The co-authored paper.** Facts: inventor A's paper, published 8 months before the EFD, lists A, B, and C; only A is named on the application. Trap: "it's the inventor's own paper, so excepted." Correct: the exception needs a showing (1.130(a) declaration with a reasonable explanation) that B and C did not contribute to the relied-on subject matter, or that they got it from A; otherwise the paper is a disclosure "by another." *Merck Serono v. Hopewell* (pre-AIA "by another": any mismatch between the reference's contributors and the named inventors defeats exclusion; similar logic); MPEP 2153.01(a), 717.01.
24. **Selling patent rights is not selling the invention.** Facts: before the critical date, the inventor assigned all rights to an investor or employer for a share of proceeds. Trap: "a sale more than a year ago." Correct: assigning or licensing patent rights, without selling or offering an embodiment, is not putting the invention on sale. But a "license" that is really a sale of an embodiment (end-user software license) can be. *Moleculon*; *In re Kollar*; *Group One*.

## Post-casebook developments (verified)

- **Helsinn Healthcare S.A. v. Teva Pharms. USA, Inc.**, 139 S. Ct. 628 (2019) — confidential sale can be AIA on-sale prior art; full entry in §2. **Skill rule:** never clear a bar on confidentiality alone.
- **Medicines Co. v. Hospira**, Nos. 2014-1469, -1504 (Fed. Cir. Feb. 6, 2018) (panel after en banc remand) — the exclusive distribution agreement was a commercial offer for sale; remanded whether it covered the claimed invention. Verified from the opinion (govinfo). **Skill rule:** distribution and supply agreements are on-sale events to date.
- **Barry v. Medtronic, Inc.**, 914 F.3d 1310, No. 2017-2463 (Fed. Cir. Jan. 24, 2019), cert. denied, No. 19-414 (U.S. Jan. 13, 2020) — experimental use negated both bars for a surgeon's use of his instruments in paid surgeries with follow-up evaluation; dissent in part (Prost, C.J.). **Skill rule:** record control, purpose, and follow-up evidence for any pre-filing clinical or field use.
- **Junker v. Medical Components, Inc.**, 25 F.4th 1027, No. 2021-1649 (Fed. Cir. Feb. 10, 2022) (pre-AIA) — a letter labeled "quotation," sent in response to the buyer's request, with tiered prices for several sizes, FOB shipping, and net-30 payment terms was a commercial offer under contract-law principles; that the buyer had not picked a size or quantity did not defeat it (multiple offers, each acceptable); design patent invalid. **Skill rule:** read the document, not its label.
- **Minerva Surgical, Inc. v. Hologic, Inc.**, No. 21-2246 (Fed. Cir. Feb. 15, 2023) (pre-AIA) — showing fully functional prototypes at a major industry conference without confidentiality was public use; physical handling not required; ready for patenting despite later refinement and no FDA approval. Verified from the opinion (govinfo). **Skill rule:** trade-show and conference demos are bar events; ask about them by name.
- **Weber, Inc. v. Provisur Techs., Inc.**, 92 F.4th 1059 (Fed. Cir. 2024) — product operating manuals given to customers were publicly accessible printed publications despite restrictive notices; *Cordis* distinguished. Verified from the opinion (govinfo); reporter cite from a later Federal Circuit opinion. **Skill rule:** in invalidity searches, look for manuals, catalogs, and customer documentation, and test accessibility by actual distribution practice, not by legends on the cover.
- **Sanho Corp. v. Kaijet Tech. Int'l Ltd.**, 108 F.4th 1376 (Fed. Cir. 2024) — "publicly disclosed" in 102(b)(2)(B) requires making the invention available to the public; a private, non-confidential sale does not qualify; full entry in §6. **Skill rule:** the shield needs a truly public first disclosure.
- **Celanese Int'l Corp. v. ITC**, 111 F.4th 1338, No. 2022-1827 (Fed. Cir. Aug. 12, 2024), cert. denied, No. 24-635 (U.S. Apr. 28, 2025) — under the AIA, pre-critical-date sales of products made by a secret process bar a patent on the process; AIA text, 271(g), 273, and legislative history did not change the settled meaning. **Skill rule:** treat product sales as triggers for process claims.
- **Merck Serono S.A. v. Hopewell Pharma Ventures, Inc.**, Nos. 2025-1210, -1211 (Fed. Cir. Oct. 30, 2025) — pre-AIA "by another": the relied-on disclosure must reflect the collective work of the same inventive entity; Board's unpatentability ruling affirmed; limited to the pre-AIA statute. **Skill rule:** demand inventive-entity proof before excluding a co-authored reference.
- Cross-references (digested in `../novelty-and-obviousness.md` §6): *Lynk Labs v. Samsung* (No. 2023-2346, Fed. Cir. Jan. 14, 2025) (published application is pre-AIA 102(e) art as of filing in IPR); *In re Riggs* (No. 2022-1945, Fed. Cir. Mar. 24, 2025) (pre-AIA 102(e): provisional must support the relied-on portions for a reference to take the provisional's date).

Not verified and therefore not relied on: any appellate ruling on secret non-sale public use under the AIA; any appellate ruling on experimental use under the AIA; any appellate test of the USPTO's "same subject matter" reading of 102(b)(1)(B). None was found as of 2026-11-18.

## Checklist for the skill

- Pick the statute per application and per claim (AIA, pre-AIA, mixed) from the full claim and benefit history; record it in `state.json`.
- Compute every claim's EFD from documents that actually support it; never assume a provisional or foreign filing carries all claims.
- Ask the bar questions by channel: gifts and loans, sales, offers, quotes, POs, supply/distribution/license deals, beta and pilot programs, trade shows, conference posters and talks, theses and defenses, preprints, repos and package releases, app-store or crowdfunding pages, investor decks without NDAs, social posts.
- Treat confidential sales and offers as triggers (*Helsinn*); treat product sales as triggers for secret-process claims (*Metallizing*, *Celanese*).
- For outsourced manufacturing, record title, marketing rights, price vs. market value, and confidentiality (*Medicines Co.*).
- For claimed "testing," record control, claimed features tested, records, user notice, payment, and end date (*City of Elizabeth*, *In re Smith*, *Barry*); report the earliest date as the conservative trigger anyway.
- Give every NPL reference a provable accessibility date and a findability basis (*Lister*, *Klopfenstein*); mark undated items `[date unproven]`.
- For each patent reference, compute both its 102(a)(1) date (publication or issue) and its 102(a)(2) date (earliest supporting filing, 102(d)); check that the priority document contains the cited passage (*Riggs*); use the U.S. filing date only for pre-AIA 102(e) (*Hilmer*).
- Apply 102(b) exceptions narrowly: own or derived disclosures only; the (b)(1)(B)/(b)(2)(B) shield needs a public first disclosure of the same subject matter (*Sanho*, MPEP 2153.02); (b)(2)(C) common ownership must exist by the EFD and never cures 102(a)(1) status.
- Apply 21(b) weekend and holiday roll-forward to the one-year anniversary; clamp Feb. 29 to Feb. 28; use the first day of a vague period for bar triggers and the last day for proving the user's own dates.
- Banner foreign-rights risk for any public disclosure before the first filing, regardless of the U.S. grace period.
- For pre-AIA patents in invalidity or FTO work, add pre-AIA 102(a), (e), (g)(2) and Rule 131 analysis; for AIA patents, replace "we invented first" with public-disclosure, derivation (135(a)(2) one-year window), and §273 analysis.
- State whether the facts support a likely bar, with the trigger, relevant exceptions, strongest counterargument and unresolved questions; a computed date alone does not resolve the law.
