# How to use this file

Load it at Phase 1 Check C (deciding whether a single reference is a knockout), Phase 2 element-by-element analysis, invalidity charts (`modules/analyze.md`), and office-action responses to §102 rejections. It covers the identity question: does one reference disclose every limitation, arranged as claimed, in an enabling way, expressly or inherently? What counts as prior art at all (dates, public accessibility, on-sale, grace periods, 102(a)(2)) is in `novelty-and-bars.md`; the charting template and MPEP pointers are in [`../novelty-and-obviousness.md`](../novelty-and-obviousness.md) §4.

**The test the cases build (run in this order).**
1. Construe the claim first (`claims-and-construction.md`). Anticipation compares the reference to the claim, not to the inventor's product or contribution.
2. One reference. Every limitation. Within its four corners (plus material properly incorporated by reference).
3. Arranged or combined as in the claim. Picking features from separate embodiments is a 103 question unless a skilled reader would "at once envisage" the combination.
4. Express or inherent. Inherent means necessarily present, not probably or usually.
5. Enabling. A skilled artisan could make the claimed subject matter (and, for method-of-use claims, practice it) from the reference plus ordinary knowledge, without undue experimentation. No utility need be disclosed.
6. "That which infringes if later, anticipates if earlier." If the claim would let the patentee stop someone practicing the reference, the claim is anticipated.

**State labels for charts.** `express` · `inherent` (cite the evidence of necessity) · `construction_dependent` · `absent` · `unknown`. Never mark `inherent` on a "would likely" or "typically" basis; that is `unknown` or a 103 point.

## 1. The identity requirement and ranges

### Titanium Metals Corp. of America v. Banner, 778 F.2d 775 (Fed. Cir. 1985) · CB p. 172
- **Facts:** Claims to a titanium alloy with 0.6–0.9% nickel, 0.2–0.4% molybdenum, up to 0.2% iron (claim 2: up to 0.1% iron), characterized by good corrosion resistance in hot brine. A short Russian article graphed Ti-Mo-Ni alloys; one plotted data point corresponded to 0.75% Ni and 0.25% Mo. The article said nothing about corrosion resistance. The district court, impressed by the applicants' real contribution, ordered the patent issued.
- **Holding / rule:** A claim covering several compositions (by ranges or otherwise) is anticipated if any one of them is in the prior art: "a claim covers several compositions, the claim is 'anticipated' if one of them is in the prior art." Discovering a new property (corrosion resistance) or the limits of a useful range does not make an old composition new. Reversed.
- **Nuance:** The reference need not say anything in words; a skilled reader's calculation from a graph point counts as disclosure. Enablement was easy because the applicants' own spec assumed skilled artisans knew how to make such alloys and their expert admitted several ways to make it. The functional "characterized by" clause added nothing because the old alloy necessarily had that property. Claim 3 (a specific 0.8 Ni / 0.3 Mo point) was only rejected as obvious; the PTO said it was not pursuing that rejection on appeal, but the court reviewed it anyway and held claim 3 obvious because the reference's 0.25 Mo / 0.75 Ni and 0.31 Mo / 0.94 Ni alloys were so close that the same properties would be expected. A claim that excludes the reference's point can survive 102, but the fight then moves to 103.
- **Status:** Good law as of 2026-11-18 (MPEP 2131.03 relies on it for ranges). Later cases refine overlapping ranges: a prior-art point inside the claimed range anticipates; a prior-art range overlapping or encompassing the claimed range anticipates only if it discloses the claimed range with sufficient specificity (see the additional range cases below and Genentech v. Hospira (2020) and UCB v. Actavis (2023) in Post-casebook).
- **Skill rule:** Phase 1/2 analysis: convert every numeric example, table row, and graph point in a reference into claim units and test it against each claimed range; one point inside the range is a full knockout for that claim. Drafting claims: when the art has points near the range, draft fallback claims with narrower ranges that exclude those points and disclose why the narrower range matters (data showing a change in kind).

**Compact note cases (CB p. 177–181)**
- *Dewey & Almy Chem. Co. v. Mimex Co.*, 124 F.2d 986 (2d Cir. 1942): anticipation is a "four corners" test; no combining references, no equivalents. *Straussler v. United States*, 339 F.2d 670 (Ct. Cl. 1964), allowed "mechanical equivalents"; modern law rejects that, and closeness short of identity is a 103 question. **Skill rule:** Charting: never mark an element `express` because the reference has an equivalent; equivalence is for infringement, and near-misses go to the 103 section.
- *Verdegaal Bros. v. Union Oil Co.*, 814 F.2d 628 (Fed. Cir. 1987), and *Lewmar Marine v. Barient*, 827 F.2d 744 (Fed. Cir. 1987) (cited in Schering): each and every limitation, expressly or inherently, in a single reference.
- *NetMoneyIN, Inc. v. VeriSign, Inc.*, 545 F.3d 1359 (Fed. Cir. 2008) · CB p. 180: the reference must disclose the elements "arranged as in the claim"; elements scattered across separate embodiments of one document do not anticipate unless the document teaches combining them or a skilled reader would at once envisage the combination (Blue Calypso; Kennametal, below). **Skill rule:** Charting: record the embodiment, figure, or example each element comes from; if elements come from different embodiments, mark the chart `combination` and move it to the 103 analysis unless the reference itself links them.
- *In re Kalm*, 378 F.2d 959 (CCPA 1967) · CB p. 178: a prior-art genus did not anticipate claimed compounds whose properties were opposite to those the reference attributed to its genus; the reference never really disclosed them. The working question is how specifically the reference points at the claimed species: does it name it, define a group so small that a skilled reader could write out every member at once (*In re Petering*, 301 F.2d 676 (CCPA 1962)), or single it out in preferred embodiments? Properties opposite to those the reference ascribes to its genus are strong evidence it never contemplated the species. **Skill rule:** Phase 2: a large prior genus is usually a 103 reference against a later species claim, not a 102 knockout; mark the 102 cell `unknown` or `absent` unless the reference names the species or a small, fully defined group containing it.
- Reverse case (Titanium Metals): a prior-art species anticipates a later genus claim that covers it. **Skill rule:** Drafting: before filing a broad genus, run a species search; one old member kills the genus claim, so file Markush or proviso fallbacks.

**Additional range, genus, and arrangement cases (not in the casebook; verified 2026-11-18)**
- *Atofina v. Great Lakes Chem. Corp.*, 441 F.3d 991 (Fed. Cir. 2006): a prior-art range of 100–500°C did not anticipate a claimed 330–450°C; the reference did not describe the narrower range with "sufficient specificity," and a disclosed range is not a disclosure of its endpoints.
- *ClearValue, Inc. v. Pearl River Polymers, Inc.*, 668 F.3d 1340 (Fed. Cir. 2012): "150 ppm or less" in the reference anticipated a claim to "50 ppm or less" where there was "no allegation of criticality or any evidence demonstrating any difference across the range"; teaching away is irrelevant to anticipation.
- *Ineos USA LLC v. Berry Plastics Corp.*, 783 F.3d 865 (Fed. Cir. 2015): a point within the claimed range anticipates; a prior-art range anticipates only if described with enough specificity that there is "no reasonable difference in how the invention operates over the ranges"; cost and appearance evidence did not show criticality.
- **Skill rule (ranges):** Analysis: when a reference range overlaps or encloses the claimed range, look in the patent for evidence that the claimed sub-range works differently (criticality); if none, grade the claim at high anticipation risk, not just obviousness risk. Drafting: put that criticality evidence in the spec.
- *In re Petering*, 301 F.2d 676 (CCPA 1962): a reference whose stated preferences narrowed a generic formula to about 20 compounds anticipated each of them, because a skilled reader would at once envisage each member; size alone is not decisive. *Eli Lilly & Co. v. Zenith Goldline Pharms.*, 471 F.3d 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2006): a genus numbering in the millions did not anticipate olanzapine. *Sanofi-Synthelabo v. Apotex, Inc.*, 550 F.3d 1075 (Fed. Cir. 2008): disclosing a racemate and noting that enantiomers exist did not anticipate a claim to the bisulfate salt of the dextrorotatory enantiomer; anticipation requires specific description plus enablement. **Skill rule:** Phase 2: for a species claim, the 102 cell is `express` only if the reference names the species or a small preferred group a skilled reader could write out; otherwise move the reference to 103.
- *Kennametal, Inc. v. Ingersoll Cutting Tool Co.*, 780 F.3d 1376 (Fed. Cir. 2015): a reference listing 5 binders and 3 coating methods "effectively teaches 15 combinations, of which one anticipates"; the combination need not have been actually made. *Nidec Motor Corp. v. Zhongshan Broad Ocean Motor Co.*, 851 F.3d 1270 (Fed. Cir. 2017): "at once envisage" cannot supply a limitation the reference lacks altogether (claims fell as obvious instead). *Blue Calypso, LLC v. Groupon, Inc.*, 815 F.3d 1331 (Fed. Cir. 2016): a reference anticipates if it teaches that its separately described components may be combined. *Microsoft Corp. v. Biscotti Inc.*, 878 F.3d 1052 (Fed. Cir. 2017): affirming a no-anticipation finding where the challenger stitched together separate embodiments without showing a skilled artisan would at once envisage the claimed arrangement (Newman, J., dissenting). **Skill rule:** Charting: combining embodiments within one document is allowed only with a citation showing the document links them or a short, finite menu a skilled reader would at once envisage; record that basis in the chart, or mark the chart `combination` and analyze under 103.
- *King Pharms., Inc. v. Eon Labs, Inc.*, 616 F.3d 1267 (Fed. Cir. 2010): adding an "informing" step (telling the patient about a known effect) to an otherwise anticipated method does not avoid anticipation; printed matter without a new functional relationship gets no weight. **Skill rule:** Drafting and charting: give no novelty weight to "informing," "instructing," or label-content steps.

## 2. Enablement of the prior art

**Compact note cases (CB p. 179–180, 187)**
- *In re Schoenwald*, 964 F.2d 1122 (Fed. Cir. 1992) · CB p. 179: a journal article that described the compound but disclosed no use anticipated the compound claim; the applicant who found the first use may claim only the method of use. "No utility need be disclosed" for an anticipating reference to an old compound. **Skill rule:** Phase 1/3: when a user's contribution is a new use of a known compound or device, steer claims to the method of use (and new formulations) and say the product claim is likely knocked out.
- *In re Donohue*, 766 F.2d 531 (Fed. Cir. 1985) (cited in Schering, CB p. 187): anticipation requires an enabling disclosure, not actual making; other references may be used to show that the anticipating reference was enabled (not to supply missing elements).
- *EPO T 26/85; House of Lords, Asahi Kasei* (1991) · CB p. 180: Europe and the UK also require enabling prior art. **Skill rule:** SKILL.md US-utility scope: do not assess foreign novelty.
**Additional enablement cases (not in the casebook; verified 2026-11-18)**
- *Rasmusson v. SmithKline Beecham Corp.*, 413 F.3d 1318 (Fed. Cir. 2005): "proof of efficacy is not required in order for a reference to be enabled for purposes of anticipation," while an applicant's own §112 enablement of a treatment claim does require a showing of efficacy. The asymmetry is deliberate.
- *In re Gleave*, 560 F.3d 1331 (Fed. Cir. 2009): a reference listing every 15-base sense sequence of the target gene (more than 1,400) anticipated claims to specific antisense sequences; "a reference need disclose no independent use or utility to anticipate," and enablement of a composition means a skilled artisan would know how to make it.
- *Impax Labs., Inc. v. Aventis Pharms. Inc.*, 545 F.3d 1312 (Fed. Cir. 2008): a prior patent that listed riluzole among many compounds and conditions did not enable treating ALS with it, because each step would require undue experimentation; prior-art patents are presumed enabled, but the presumption was rebutted.
- *In re Montgomery*, 677 F.3d 1375 (Fed. Cir. 2012): a published clinical-trial design (no results) was enabling and inherently anticipated a claim to treating or preventing stroke with ramipril, because administering the drug to that population "inevitably treats or prevents stroke" (Lourie, J., dissenting).
- *In re Morsa*, 713 F.3d 104 (Fed. Cir. 2013): the presumption that a prior-art publication is enabled is procedural; once the applicant makes a non-frivolous argument of non-enablement, the examiner must address it (no declaration required).
- **Skill rule (enablement generally):** Invalidity and office-action response: a reference that names a compound, device, or use but gives no way to make or practice it (and ordinary skill does not fill the gap) may not anticipate; check whether a secondary reference shows how to make it (Donohue) before conceding. The presumption runs in favor of the reference, so the patentee or applicant must argue non-enablement specifically (Morsa; Agilent (2025) in Post-casebook). Never argue that a method-of-treatment reference fails to anticipate merely because it lacks efficacy data (Rasmusson; Montgomery).

## 3. Inherency

### Schering Corp. v. Geneva Pharmaceuticals, Inc., 339 F.3d 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2003) · CB p. 181
- **Facts:** Schering's expired '233 patent claimed and disclosed loratadine (Claritin) and its administration to patients. Its later '716 patent claimed the metabolite descarboethoxyloratadine (DCL) as a compound. The '233 patent never mentioned DCL or metabolites, but loratadine always metabolizes to DCL in the body in detectable amounts.
- **Holding / rule:** A reference anticipates if each limitation is disclosed expressly or inherently; inherency can supply the entire claimed compound, not just one missing feature. Inherent anticipation does not require that a person of ordinary skill at the time would have recognized the inherent subject matter. The prior patent enabled making DCL "in any form encompassed by" the compound claim (in a patient's body) by teaching administration of loratadine. Summary judgment of anticipation affirmed.
- **Nuance:** The court distinguished *In re Seaborg*, 328 F.2d 996 (CCPA 1964) (an isotope possibly made in undetectable traces was not anticipated), and the "accidental, unwitting, and unappreciated" cases, *Tilghman v. Proctor*, 102 U.S. 707 (1880), and *Eibel Process Co. v. Minnesota & Ontario Paper*, 261 U.S. 45 (1923), where the record did not show the claimed result actually occurred. The court told drafters how to protect metabolites: claim the isolated or purified compound (*In re Kratz*, 592 F.2d 1169 (CCPA 1979); *In re Bergstrom*, 427 F.2d 1394 (CCPA 1970)), a pharmaceutical composition, or a method of administering it; the '716 patent's composition and method claims were not anticipated. Judge Newman, dissenting from denial of rehearing en banc, argued a previously unknown product is new and that earlier cases (*Continental Can*) required recognition by skilled artisans.
- **Status:** Good law as of 2026-11-18 (MPEP 2112 cites it for "recognition not required"). Under AIA §102(a)(1) the result is the same: the anticipating document was a patent published years before. Later cases apply it to trace amounts (SmithKline v. Apotex, 2005), to treatment results that follow from a published protocol (Montgomery, 2012), and to inherent physical properties (Arbutus, 2023; Sigray, 2025).
- **Skill rule:** Drafting claims (pharma, chemistry, materials): never file only a bare compound claim for a metabolite, degradation product, polymorph, or reaction by-product of a known substance or process; claim the isolated or purified form, compositions, and methods of use. Invalidity search: for any compound claim, ask what known drugs, processes, or products would necessarily produce that compound, and look for evidence it forms in detectable amounts under the reference's normal conditions.

**Compact note cases on inherency (CB p. 180–186, 189–192)**
- *Continental Can Co. USA v. Monsanto Co.*, 948 F.2d 1264 (Fed. Cir. 1991): extrinsic evidence may fill a gap in a reference only if it shows the missing matter is necessarily present; conflicting expert testimony on whether blow-molded ribs were hollow precluded summary judgment. Schering reads its "recognized by persons of ordinary skill" language as a tool for reading the reference, not a recognition requirement. **Skill rule:** Charting: for each `inherent` cell, cite the evidence (test data, expert explanation, the reference's own conditions) showing necessity; if the evidence is disputed, mark `inherent – disputed [review]`.
- *MEHL/Biophile Int'l Corp. v. Milgraum*, 192 F.3d 1362 (Fed. Cir. 1999): a prior-art laser procedure on skin, carried out as taught, necessarily removed hair, so it anticipated a hair-removal method; the authors' failure to appreciate the result was irrelevant, and nothing in the claim limited it to human skin. **Skill rule:** Drafting: a method claim whose only new feature is the purpose or result of known steps is anticipated; add a step or parameter the prior procedure did not have.
- *In re Oelrich*, 666 F.2d 578 (CCPA 1981); *Gubelmann v. Gang*, 408 F.2d 758 (CCPA 1969); *Bettcher Indus. v. Bunzl USA*, 661 F.3d 629 (Fed. Cir. 2011); *Trintec Indus. v. Top-U.S.A. Corp.*, 295 F.3d 1292 (Fed. Cir. 2002): the feature must be inevitably present as the natural result of practicing the reference as taught; "might," "could under some conditions," and capability by happenstance are not enough. "Inherency does not embrace probabilities or possibilities." **Skill rule:** Phase 1 knockout rule: never call a knockout on inherency unless the feature necessarily results from what the reference teaches; otherwise grade the reference as a 103 risk.
- *In re Cruciferous Sprout Litig.*, 301 F.3d 1343 (Fed. Cir. 2002), and *Atlas Powder Co. v. Ireco, Inc.*, 190 F.3d 1342 (Fed. Cir. 1999): claims to preparing a glucosinolate-rich food by germinating certain seeds and harvesting the sprouts were inherently anticipated by prior sprout growing, since high glucosinolate content is an inherent characteristic of those sprouts; likewise sufficient aeration was inherent in the prior explosive. Discovering why a known product or process works does not make it patentable, and the public may keep practicing the prior art without understanding it.
- *EMI Group N. Am. v. Cypress Semiconductor*, 268 F.3d 1342 (Fed. Cir. 2001) · CB p. 192: recognition by skilled artisans may matter for structural limitations, but natural laws or theoretical mechanisms recited in a claim need not have been recognized for inherency (the oxygen-and-fire example). **Skill rule:** Drafting: reciting the scientific explanation of a known process does not add novelty; claim a new step, structure, or parameter.
- *Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Ben Venue Labs.*, 246 F.3d 1368 (Fed. Cir. 2001) (quoted in Schering): "that which would literally infringe if later in time anticipates if earlier." **Skill rule:** Self-check for every knockout call: would practicing the reference, as taught, literally infringe the claim? If yes, it anticipates; if it would only infringe under equivalents, it does not.
- *Hoechst-Roussel Pharms. v. Lehman*, 109 F.3d 756 (Fed. Cir. 1997), and *Zenith Labs. v. Bristol-Myers Squibb*, 19 F.3d 1418 (Fed. Cir. 1994): a compound claim can cover a compound formed in the body after ingestion, which is why it can also be anticipated that way (the "metabolite defense" Schering ended for bare compound claims).
- *In re Schreiber*, 128 F.3d 1473 (Fed. Cir. 1997) (in Newman dissent): a conical spout claimed for dispensing popcorn was anticipated by a structurally identical oil-can spout; a new intended use does not make an old structure new, and once the examiner showed a sound basis for inherency, the applicant had to prove the old spout lacked the functionally defined features (MPEP 2112(V)). **Skill rule:** Charting apparatus claims: ignore intended-use language in the preamble or "adapted to" clauses unless it requires structure the reference lacks; flag such terms `construction_dependent`.
- *In re Seaborg*, 328 F.2d 996 (CCPA 1964): no anticipation where the prior process would have made at most an undetectable trace of the claimed isotope. **Skill rule:** Invalidity: an inherency theory based on trace formation needs evidence the product actually formed in detectable amounts under the reference's conditions.
- *Merrell Dow Pharms. v. H.N. Norton & Co.* [1996] R.P.C. 76 (H.L.) · CB p. 192: the UK reached the same result as Schering for terfenadine's acid metabolite. Foreign; do not apply (SKILL.md US-utility scope).

**Additional inherency cases (not in the casebook; verified 2026-11-18)**
- *SmithKline Beecham Corp. v. Apotex Corp.*, 403 F.3d 1331 (Fed. Cir. 2005): a prior process that "inevitably results in the production of at least trace amounts" of paroxetine hydrochloride hemihydrate inherently anticipated a claim to that compound; Seaborg was distinguished for lack of positive evidence of formation. **Skill rule:** Drafting: for polymorphs and salts, add claims with purity or amount limits, compositions, and methods; a bare structural claim can fall to trace formation in a prior process.
- *Perricone v. Medicis Pharm. Corp.*, 432 F.3d 1368 (Fed. Cir. 2005): a reference teaching topical application of a lotion to skin anticipated claims to applying the composition to skin generally, but not claims to treating skin sunburn, which the reference did not disclose. **Skill rule:** Drafting: a newly discovered use of a known composition can be claimed as a method directed to a specific, previously undisclosed condition or patient group; a generic "apply to skin" claim cannot.
- *In re Montgomery* (above, §2): inherency of a treatment result when the reference directs the same drug to the same population.

## 4. Incorporation by reference and multi-document "references"

- *Advanced Display Sys. v. Kent State Univ.*, 212 F.3d 1272 (Fed. Cir. 2000) (not in the casebook; prompted by the casebook's hyperlink exercise, CB p. 193): material incorporated by reference becomes part of the host document for anticipation, but only if the host identifies with particularity what material it incorporates and where it is found; a bare citation incorporates nothing, and showing the missing material would have been obvious is not enough. **Skill rule:** Phase 1/2: when one document cites or links to another, treat them as one reference only if the first expressly incorporates specific material; otherwise chart them as a two-reference 103 combination. A hyperlink alone is not incorporation `[review]`.
- Whether a web page is prior art at all (public accessibility, date proof) is a separate question; see `novelty-and-bars.md`.

## Edge-case playbook

1. **Old product, newly discovered property.** Trap: "nobody knew the alloy resists brine corrosion, so my claim to the alloy with that property is new." Correct: the old composition anticipates; the property is inherent. Claim a new method of using it instead. Cases: Titanium Metals; Schoenwald; Atlas Powder.
2. **One example inside a claimed range.** Trap: "the reference is about something else and never mentions my range." Correct: one disclosed point (including a graph point a skilled reader can compute) inside the range anticipates that claim. Cases: Titanium Metals; UCB v. Actavis (2023), via MPEP 2131.03.
3. **Broad prior range around a narrow claimed range.** Trap: calling it an automatic 102 knockout (or automatically safe). Correct: anticipation only if the reference discloses the narrow range with sufficient specificity; showing criticality or a difference in operation across the range matters; even a slight overlap can anticipate when the patentee shows no criticality; otherwise analyze under 103. Do not use the at-once-envisage test for ranges. Cases: Atofina; ClearValue; Ineos; Genentech v. Hospira (2020); UCB v. Actavis (2023).
4. **Large prior genus, later species claim.** Trap: "the species is inside the genus, so it is anticipated." Correct: a genus usually does not anticipate a species unless the reference names it or the group is small enough to envisage at once; but a disclosed species does anticipate a later genus. Cases: Kalm; Petering; Titanium Metals (reverse case).
5. **Features from two embodiments of one document.** Trap: treating a single document as a single reference for every purpose. Correct: elements must be arranged as claimed; combining embodiments is a 103 move unless the reference teaches that they may be combined or a skilled reader would at once envisage the combination from a short menu. Cases: NetMoneyIN; Kennametal; Blue Calypso; Biscotti.
6. **Two documents joined by a citation or hyperlink.** Trap: charting them as one reference. Correct: only material expressly incorporated with particularity becomes part of the host; otherwise it is a 103 combination. Case: Advanced Display.
7. **Equivalent element in the reference.** Trap: marking "functionally the same" as disclosed. Correct: no equivalents in anticipation; closeness is a 103 point. Cases: Dewey & Almy; Straussler (rejected view).
8. **Metabolite, degradation product, or by-product claimed as a bare compound.** Trap: "the prior patent never mentions this compound, so it is new." Correct: if practicing the prior reference necessarily makes it in detectable amounts, the bare compound claim is inherently anticipated, even though no one recognized it. Purified forms, compositions, and methods of use can survive. Cases: Schering v. Geneva; Kratz; Bergstrom.
9. **Trace or theoretical formation.** Trap: extending Schering to "it might form under some conditions." Correct: the product must necessarily form under the reference's normal conditions, with positive evidence that it does (trace amounts suffice if proven; undetectable theoretical amounts do not). Cases: SmithKline v. Apotex; Seaborg; Oelrich; Trintec; Bettcher.
10. **"Usually" or "typically present."** Trap: calling a feature inherent because it is common. Correct: inherency requires necessity; probabilities do not count. Cases: Trintec; Gubelmann; MEHL/Biophile.
11. **New purpose for old steps.** Trap: a method claim whose only novelty is the stated goal or the newly understood mechanism. Correct: if the old steps, practiced as taught, necessarily produce the result, the claim is anticipated; a claim limited to a new, undisclosed condition or patient group can survive. Cases: MEHL/Biophile; Cruciferous Sprout; EMI Group; Bristol-Myers v. Ben Venue; Perricone.
12. **New intended use for an old structure.** Trap: "a popcorn spout is different from an oil spout." Correct: intended use does not distinguish an apparatus claim unless it requires different structure. Case: Schreiber.
13. **Reference names a compound but gives no use.** Trap: "a reference with no utility cannot anticipate." Correct: prior art need only enable making the compound; no utility is required, and a long list of sequences or compounds can anticipate each member it enables. Cases: Schoenwald; Gleave; Donohue.
14. **Reference names a compound but no way to make it.** Trap: conceding anticipation. Correct: the reference must be enabling; check whether the art or another reference shows how to make it (Donohue allows that use), and remember the reference is presumed enabled, so the patentee or applicant must make a specific non-enablement argument. Cases: Donohue; Impax; Morsa; Agilent (2025).
15. **Secret prior use by the patentee.** Trap: "the drug was only tested in secret, so the metabolite was never public." Correct: anticipation needs an enabling disclosure, not actual public making; the published patent teaching administration was enough. Case: Schering (part C).
16. **Patentee's real contribution.** Trap: weighing the inventor's effort or the reference's silence about the benefit. Correct: anticipation asks only what the claim covers and whether that is old. Case: Titanium Metals.
17. **Infringement test reversed.** Trap: applying anticipation without the mirror check. Correct: if practicing the reference as taught would literally infringe the claim, the claim is anticipated. Cases: Bristol-Myers v. Ben Venue; Schering; Atlas Powder.
18. **Clinical-trial protocol with no results.** Trap: "a plan is not a disclosure, and nothing proves the drug works." Correct: prior art needs no efficacy proof; if the protocol gives the drug to the claimed population, the treatment result is inherent and the method claim is anticipated. Cases: Montgomery; Rasmusson.
19. **"Informing" or label step.** Trap: adding "and informing the patient that..." to an old method to make it new. Correct: printed matter with no new functional relationship gets no weight. Case: King v. Eon.
20. **Claim with no threshold.** Trap: arguing the reference has "too little" of the claimed feature. Correct: if the claim sets no minimum, any necessary presence meets it. Cases: Sigray (2025); Bd. of Regents v. Boston Scientific (2026).
21. **Full-scope enablement attack on prior art.** Trap: arguing a reference cannot anticipate a broad claim because it does not enable the whole claim (borrowing Amgen v. Sanofi). Correct: prior art need enable only one embodiment within the claim. Case: Agilent (2025).
22. **Inherent property used to save an obvious claim.** Trap: adding a measured property (stability, PK value, morphology) to an obvious formulation. Correct: if the property necessarily results, it adds nothing under 102 or 103, and later testing may prove it. Cases: Persion (2019); Hospira (2020); Arbutus (2023); Cytiva (2024).
23. **Foreign novelty.** Trap: applying these US rules to EP, UK, or JP novelty. Correct: refer out (SKILL.md US-utility scope); Europe's selection-invention and disclosure rules differ even where results align (Merrell Dow).

## Post-casebook developments (through Nov. 2026)

All verified against the opinions (Federal Circuit site or govinfo) on 2026-11-18.
- *Persion Pharms. LLC v. Alvogen Malta Operations Ltd.*, 945 F.3d 1184 (Fed. Cir. 2019): "inherency may supply a missing claim limitation in an obviousness analysis"; pharmacokinetic limitations were necessarily present in the prior-art formulation. **Skill rule:** Phase 2: when a claim adds a property or PK value to an obvious formulation, test whether it is necessarily present; if so, it does not save the claim under 103 either.
- *Hospira, Inc. v. Fresenius Kabi USA, LLC*, 946 F.3d 1322 (Fed. Cir. 2020): a stability limitation was inherent in an obvious premixed formulation; evidence that is not itself prior art (later testing) may show what is necessarily present. **Skill rule:** Invalidity: commission or cite later testing of the prior-art product to prove inherency; the test data need not predate the patent.
- *Genentech, Inc. v. Hospira, Inc.*, 946 F.3d 1333 (Fed. Cir. Jan. 10, 2020): a prior-art range of 18–25°C anticipated a claimed "about 10°C to about 18°C"; an overlapping range "can be anticipatory, even where the prior art range only partially or slightly overlaps," absent criticality (Newman, J., dissenting). Some tension with Atofina's endpoint statement. **Skill rule:** Analysis: even a one-point overlap at a range endpoint is a 102 risk; check criticality.
- *Raytheon Techs. Corp. v. General Electric Co.*, 993 F.3d 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2021): for obviousness, "the prior art, taken as a whole, must enable a skilled artisan to make and use the claimed invention"; a single 103 reference need not be self-enabling, but here the only evidence was a non-enabling reference, so the obviousness finding was reversed. **Skill rule:** Phase 2/invalidity: for a speculative or futuristic reference (a concept paper, a "could be built" disclosure), check enablement for both 102 and 103 and find supporting art showing how to build it.
- *Arbutus Biopharma Corp. v. ModernaTX, Inc.*, 65 F.4th 656 (Fed. Cir. 2023): lipid-particle claims were inherently anticipated because the reference's formulations necessarily had the claimed non-lamellar morphology; "the prior art need only meet the inherently disclosed limitation to the same extent as the patented invention." Dependent range claims fell because a reference composition sat inside each range, and material incorporated by reference counted as part of the reference. **Skill rule:** Analysis: if the patent's own method of making is the same as the reference's, presume the same properties result and ask the patentee to show otherwise.
- *UCB, Inc. v. Actavis Labs. UT, Inc.*, 65 F.4th 679 (Fed. Cir. 2023): "If the prior art discloses a point within the claimed range, the prior art anticipates the claim" (quoted in MPEP 2131.03); applying Kennametal's "at once envisage" test to overlapping ranges is legal error, since ranges follow the Ineos/Atofina line. **Skill rule:** Analysis: use the range cases for numeric limitations and the at-once-envisage cases for lists of options; never mix the tests.
- *Cytiva BioProcess R&D AB v. JSR Corp.*, 122 F.4th 876 (Fed. Cir. Dec. 4, 2024) (No. 23-2074): a limitation that "merely recites an inherent property of an otherwise obvious combination" needs no separate reasonable-expectation-of-success showing. **Skill rule:** Drafting: claiming a measured property of an otherwise obvious composition adds nothing; claim a structural or process feature that causes it.
- *Sigray, Inc. v. Carl Zeiss X-Ray Microscopy, Inc.*, 137 F.4th 1372 (Fed. Cir. May 23, 2025) (No. 23-2211): reversing a no-anticipation finding; "Any presence of the limitation in the reference inherently discloses it for the purposes of anticipation." The Board's requirement that the reference's beam diverge "enough," which read small magnifications out of the claimed "between 1 and 10" times range, was an improper implicit claim construction; the claim set no minimum degree of divergence, so the slight divergence the reference necessarily had was enough. **Skill rule:** Charting: if the claim sets no threshold, any necessary presence of the feature in the reference satisfies it; if the user wants to avoid that, draft a numeric threshold.
- *Agilent Techs., Inc. v. Synthego Corp.*, 139 F.4th 1319 (Fed. Cir. 2025), cert. denied (Mar. 30, 2026) (No. 25-570): "prior art disclosures are presumed enabling"; an anticipatory reference "need only enable a single embodiment"; the Amgen v. Sanofi full-scope enablement standard for claims does not apply to prior art. **Skill rule:** Office-action response and invalidity defense: do not argue that a reference fails to anticipate because it does not enable the full scope of the claim; argue only that it fails to enable the specific embodiment that falls within the claim.
- *Bd. of Regents, Univ. of Tex. Sys. v. Boston Scientific Corp.*, No. 24-2062 (Fed. Cir. July 27, 2026) (precedential; reporter cite not found): JMOL of anticipation on unrebutted expert testimony; a claim term with no stated minimum ("varying rates") is met by any degree of change. Ordinary express anticipation, no doctrinal change.
- No precedential 2024–2026 decision was found that changes the rules for overlapping ranges or genus/species beyond UCB (2023).

## Checklist for the skill

- Construe each claim term before charting; mark synonym-prone terms `construction_dependent`.
- Chart one reference per anticipation chart. Record the page, figure, example, or embodiment for every element.
- Reject any `express` cell that rests on an equivalent, an "obvious variant," or a different embodiment of the same document.
- For every numeric limitation, extract all numbers, table rows, and graph points from the reference in claim units and test them against the claimed range.
- For a prior-art range that overlaps or encloses the claim, decide "sufficient specificity" explicitly and note any criticality evidence; default to the 103 section when unsure.
- For genus vs. species, ask: does the reference name the species, define a small fully envisageable group, or single it out in preferred embodiments? If not, the 102 cell is not `express`.
- Mark `inherent` only with evidence that the feature necessarily results from practicing the reference as taught, in detectable amounts; otherwise `unknown`.
- Do not require that anyone recognized the inherent feature at the time (Schering).
- Ignore intended-use and purpose language unless it adds structure or a step.
- Treat a second document as part of the first only on express, particular incorporation by reference.
- Confirm the reference is enabling for what the claim covers (making the product; for method-of-use claims, practicing the method). Do not require disclosed utility.
- Run the mirror test before any knockout: would practicing the reference literally infringe the claim?
- In drafting: never file only a bare compound claim for metabolites, polymorphs, or by-products of known substances; add isolated or purified forms, compositions, and methods of use. Add narrower range fallbacks that exclude known points.
- When the user's contribution is a newly discovered property or use of something old, say the product claim is likely anticipated and steer to method-of-use claims.
- Report likely anticipation only when every limitation and its arrangement is express or necessarily inherent in one qualifying enabling reference. Otherwise resolve the strongest objection and response for the claim as a whole; identify the missing proof.
