# How to use this file

Rules, MPEP cites, incorporation-by-reference mechanics, and the spec-drafting checklist live in `../disclosure-112.md`. This file adds the cases: what each holds, where it stops, and the fact that would flip it. Load it at Phase 3 (spec drafting, provisional vs. nonprovisional choice, claim breadth), at any office action with a 112(a) rejection, and in invalidity mode (enablement, written description, lost priority). Definiteness and 112(f) are in `claims-and-construction.md`; obviousness is in `obviousness.md`.

Three separate 112(a) questions, always asked claim by claim and as of the filing date sought:
1. **Enablement:** can a skilled person make and use the full claimed scope without undue (unreasonable) experimentation? (Wands, Amgen)
2. **Written description:** does the application, read by a skilled person, show the inventor possessed what is now claimed? (Vas-Cath, Ariad)
3. **Best mode:** did the inventor disclose the mode he or she considered best at filing? (Chemcast; no longer an invalidity ground after the AIA)

The same document can pass one test and fail another (DiLeone, Ariad). Priority benefit (119(e), 120) turns on the first two, not best mode.

## 1. Enablement

### Gould v. Hellwarth, 472 F.2d 1383 (C.C.P.A. 1973) · CB p. 421
- **Facts:** Interference over the Q-switched laser. Gould filed in April 1959, before anyone had built a working laser. His application named candidate media (including ruby, with no crystal type, size, orientation, or pumping details) and a sodium–mercury gas mix nobody ever made lase. His employer spent nearly $1 million over the next year trying to build a laser from the same material and failed. Maiman's 1960 ruby laser took extensive work to find a specific pink ruby.
- **Holding / rule:** Gould's application did not enable a skilled person to make an operable laser as of its filing date, so it could not serve as a constructive reduction to practice; priority went to Hellwarth, who filed in 1961 after lasers existed. Enablement is judged against the art at the filing date, whether the relevant art is lasers or the "most nearly connected" art.
- **Nuance:** (1) The field's own failures (the funded, failed effort) were decisive proof of undue experimentation. (2) Later successes by others cannot be borrowed to enable an earlier filing. (3) A disclosure that becomes workable only after others' later breakthroughs gets no credit for the earlier date; a fresh filing would get only the later date. (4) Gould later enforced different, optical-amplifier claims in a patent that continued the same April 1959 application (U.S. 4,053,845): a jury found Control Laser's products infringed, though it held four claims invalid, and the case settled; the Federal Circuit then dismissed Gould's appeal on those four claims as moot (Gould v. Control Laser Corp., 866 F.2d 1391 (Fed. Cir. 1989)). Enablement is claim-specific.
- **Status:** Enablement-as-of-filing-date principle is good law. The interference procedure is gone for AIA applications (first-inventor-to-file; derivation proceedings under 35 U.S.C. 135). As of 2026-11-18.
- **Skill rule:** Drafting (provisional and nonprovisional): for a first-of-its-kind device, include at least one complete operable embodiment with concrete parameters (materials, dimensions, operating conditions, how to start it); a list of candidate materials does not buy a filing date.

### Atlas Powder Co. v. E.I. du Pont de Nemours & Co., 750 F.2d 1569 (Fed. Cir. 1984) · CB p. 432
- **Facts:** Claims to an emulsion blasting agent (ammonium-nitrate solution, carbonaceous fuel, at least 4% occluded gas, a water-in-oil emulsifier). The spec listed many salts, fuels, and emulsifiers; its examples were prophetic but based on real experiments adjusted toward what the inventor thought optimal. Atlas's records labeled about 40% of 300 experiments "failures." Du Pont's researchers made working emulsions from the listed ingredients with little difficulty.
- **Holding / rule:** Enabled. Some experimentation is allowed if not undue. Claims need not exclude inoperative combinations; they fail only if inoperative embodiments are so numerous that a skilled person must experiment unduly. Prophetic examples do not make a patent non-enabling by themselves; the challenger must prove non-enablement by clear and convincing evidence.
- **Nuance:** The winning facts: (1) a known selection rule (Bancroft's Rule) told a skilled person which emulsifier fits which system; (2) the "failures" were suboptimal, not inoperative, and optimality is not required; (3) the accused infringer's own easy success. Flip: no selection rule, wide unpredictability, and many real failures (Pharmaceutical Resources, below). Read today with Amgen's rule that the more one claims, the more one must enable.
- **Status:** Good law; still cited for prophetic examples and inoperative embodiments. Prophetic examples must not be written in the past tense (MPEP 2164.02, 608.01(p)). As of 2026-11-18.
- **Skill rule:** Drafting (chem, materials): when listing alternatives, also disclose the selection principle or screening test (with pass criteria) that lets a skilled person pick working combinations; write prophetic examples in present or future tense and never present them as performed.

### In re Wright, 999 F.2d 1557 (Fed. Cir. 1993) · CB p. 437
- **Facts:** Claims to processes for making live non-pathogenic vaccines against any pathogenic RNA virus, the vaccines, and methods of use. One working example: a recombinant vaccine protecting chickens against one avian sarcoma virus. The examiner allowed claims to that example.
- **Holding / rule:** The broader claims were not enabled as of the February 1983 filing date. RNA viruses were diverse, and protective (not merely antigenic) efficacy was unpredictable, so one example only invited experimentation. The PTO's reasonable basis shifted the burden; Wright's conclusory affidavits did not carry it. Even claims limited to avian RNA viruses failed.
- **Nuance:** (1) A 1988 article could show the art was still unpredictable in 1983, but Wright's own post-filing successes could not show enablement as of filing: later evidence may illuminate the state of the art at filing, not supply missing teaching. (2) "Vaccine" means protective, which raised what had to be enabled. (3) Wright's own declaration that in vivo testing was needed undercut him. (4) Arguments not briefed to the Board were waived.
- **Status:** Good law; consistent with Amgen (2023) and the USPTO's Jan. 2024 enablement guidelines. As of 2026-11-18.
- **Skill rule:** Office actions: answer an enablement rejection with dated literature and specific facts on predictability, not conclusory expert statements, and offer fallback claims sized to the examples; drafting: claim efficacy terms ("vaccine," "treats," "protects") only as broadly as the data supports, with intermediate subgenus claims tied to tested species.

### Pharmaceutical Resources, Inc. v. Roxane Laboratories, Inc., 253 F. App'x 26 (Fed. Cir. 2007) (nonprecedential) · CB p. 445
- **Facts:** Par's claims to stable flocculated megestrol acetate suspensions recited "a surfactant" with no type or concentration limit. Par had told the PTO, and argued in earlier litigation, that the art was so unpredictable that any change in surfactant or amount defeated any expectation of success. Three working examples used one new surfactant.
- **Holding / rule:** Not enabled; summary judgment affirmed. Applying the eight Wands factors, extreme breadth (hundreds of surfactants, any concentration) plus admitted unpredictability plus minimal guidance meant the full scope was not enabled. Success with seven surfactants was "merely colorable" evidence; conclusory declarations raised no genuine dispute.
- **Nuance:** The wound was self-inflicted: the unpredictability argument that helped beat the prior art became the enablement problem. If skilled workers knew high concentrations would not work, that only proved much of the claim covered inoperative embodiments (Atlas Powder turned against the patentee).
- **Status:** Nonprecedential; reasoning matches Amgen and Baxalta (2023). As of 2026-11-18.
- **Skill rule:** Prosecution: before arguing "unpredictable art" to overcome a 103 rejection, check each pending claim's breadth against that statement; pair the argument with narrowing amendments or added examples.

### In re Wands, 858 F.2d 731 (Fed. Cir. 1988) · factors quoted at CB p. 446
- Eight factors: quantity of experimentation, direction or guidance, working examples, nature of the invention, state of the prior art, relative skill, predictability, claim breadth. Wands itself found screening hybridomas for high-affinity IgM antibodies to be routine experimentation, so the claims were enabled. **Status:** still applied after Amgen (Baxalta, 2023: "no meaningful difference" between Wands's undue experimentation and Amgen's reasonable experimentation; USPTO guidelines, 89 FR 1563 (Jan. 10, 2024)). **Skill rule:** answer enablement rejections factor by factor, with evidence for each.

### Amgen Inc. v. Sanofi, 598 U.S. 594 (2023) · supplemental opinion
- **Facts:** Claims to every antibody that binds specified residues of PCSK9's 15-amino-acid "sweet spot" and blocks PCSK9 from binding LDL receptors. The spec gave amino acid sequences for 26 antibodies and two methods for finding more: a "roadmap" (generate candidates, then screen) and "conservative substitution" (swap amino acids in working antibodies, then test). The genus potentially held millions of antibodies.
- **Holding / rule:** Unanimous (Gorsuch, J.): not enabled. "If a patent claims an entire class of processes, machines, manufactures, or compositions of matter, the patent's specification must enable a person skilled in the art to make and use the entire class." The roadmap and substitution methods were "little more than two research assignments," leaving trial and error, "a hunting license."
- **Nuance:** (1) Not every embodiment needs a recipe: a few examples can suffice if the spec discloses "some general quality . . . running through" the class that fits it to the purpose (Incandescent Lamp). (2) Reasonable experimentation is allowed, judged by the nature of the invention and the art (Wood v. Underhill; Minerals Separation v. Hyde, 242 U.S. 261 (1916)). (3) The Court agreed enablement is not measured by the cumulative time to make every embodiment, and that there is one enablement standard for all inventions; but "the more one claims, the more one must enable." (4) The 26 sequenced antibodies were conceded enabled, and the Court did not doubt the spec enabled them; the problem was claim breadth beyond them. (5) The Court did not mention Wands; lower courts and the USPTO still use it. (6) Holland Furniture Co. v. Perkins Glue Co., 277 U.S. 245 (1928) (all starch glues that work like animal glue) and Morse and Incandescent Lamp were the Court's anchors. (7) Amgen governs claim enablement, not whether a prior-art reference is enabled (Agilent v. Synthego, 2025). (8) It concerns claims to the class itself; method-of-treatment claims using a known class of tools can come out differently (Teva v. Lilly, 2026, below).
- **Status:** Controlling, as of 2026-11-18. Applied in Baxalta v. Genentech, 81 F.4th 1362 (Fed. Cir. 2023); USPTO says it is not limited to antibodies or biotech (89 FR 1563).
- **Skill rule:** Drafting claims: in unpredictable arts, never rest on a functional genus ("any X that achieves Y") alone; layer the set: structurally defined species, a structural subgenus with the shared feature that makes members work, then a functional genus only if the spec discloses that shared quality and examples across its range. Invalidity: for a functional genus, estimate genus size versus disclosed working species and ask whether the spec gives anything beyond screen-and-test.

### Note cases (enablement), CB pp. 427–451
- Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp., 822 F.2d 1544 (Fed. Cir. 1987): patents are not production documents; mass-production dimensions and tolerances need not be disclosed. **Skill rule:** disclose what a skilled person needs to make and use the claimed features, not a manufacturing package.
- Genentech, Inc. v. Novo Nordisk A/S, 108 F.3d 1361 (Fed. Cir. 1997): a bare mention of cleavable fusion expression of hGH, with no conjugate, no conditions, and one named enzyme, was not enabling. "Tossing out the mere germ of an idea does not constitute enabling disclosure." The spec, not general skill, must supply the novel aspects. **Skill rule:** never write "within the skill of the art" for the part of the invention that is new; describe it concretely.
- The Incandescent Lamp Patent (Consolidated Elec. Light Co. v. McKeesport Light Co.), 159 U.S. 465 (1895): a claim to all carbonized fibrous or textile filaments failed when most such materials did not work and Edison had to test thousands to find bamboo. Reaffirmed by Amgen. A claim could have survived had the patentees found a quality common to the class.
- O'Reilly v. Morse, 56 U.S. (15 How.) 62 (1854): Morse's eighth claim (all uses of electromagnetism to print at a distance) claimed beyond the specification. Amgen treats it as an enablement case. In re Cook, 439 F.2d 730 (C.C.P.A. 1971): string cite for claims broader than the disclosure.
- In re Lundak, 773 F.2d 1216 (Fed. Cir. 1985): a public deposit of a biological material can supply enablement that words cannot; current rules are 37 C.F.R. 1.801–1.809 and the Budapest Treaty. **Skill rule:** Phase 3 bio: if a claimed cell line, strain, or organism cannot be reproduced from the text, flag the deposit requirement before filing.
- Diamond Rubber Co. v. Consolidated Rubber Tire Co., 220 U.S. 428 (1911): an inventor need not understand or correctly explain why the invention works. **Skill rule:** state theory as belief ("without being bound by theory"), and never make a theory a claim limitation.
- In re Dinh-Nguyen, 492 F.2d 856 (C.C.P.A. 1974): claims need not expressly exclude inoperative species.
- Enzo Biochem, Inc. v. Calgene, Inc., 188 F.3d 1362 (Fed. Cir. 1999): antisense claims for all cells, supported by three genes in E. coli, were a "plan" or "invitation," not an enabling disclosure in an unpredictable art.
- Liebel-Flarsheim Co. v. Medrad, Inc., 481 F.3d 1371 (Fed. Cir. 2007): claims construed (at the patentee's urging) to cover injectors without a pressure jacket were not enabled because every embodiment had a jacket and the spec taught that jacketless designs were impractical; "beware of what one asks for." **Skill rule:** FTO and litigation analysis: when a patentee pushes a broad construction to reach a product, test that construction against enablement and written description.
- AK Steel Corp. v. Sollac & Ugine, 344 F.3d 1234 (Fed. Cir. 2003): the full claim scope must be enabled; a spec that says an embodiment within the claim does not work defeats it.
- Automotive Techs. Int'l v. BMW of N. Am., 501 F.3d 1274 (Fed. Cir. 2007): mechanical side-impact sensors were described but the claims also covered electronic sensors, which were not enabled; a declaration lacking detail on why no experimentation was needed raised no fact issue. **Skill rule:** if claims cover two technologies, describe each in operable detail.

## 2. Written description

### Vas-Cath Inc. v. Mahurkar, 935 F.2d 1555 (Fed. Cir. 1991) · CB p. 451
- **Facts:** Mahurkar's double-lumen catheter. A 1982 U.S. design application had drawings only. Utility applications filed in 1984, more than a year after his Canadian design registration published, used the same drawings and claimed benefit of the design application under § 120. The utility claims recited, among other things, a return-lumen diameter "substantially greater than one-half but substantially less than" the combined diameter. Without the design date, the Canadian registration was a bar.
- **Holding / rule:** Drawings alone can satisfy the written description requirement, and a design application's drawings can support utility claims. The test is whether the disclosure conveys "with reasonable clarity" to skilled persons that the inventor had in fact invented what is now claimed, as of the filing date sought. Summary judgment of invalidity reversed. The "invention" is whatever is now claimed; there is no separate "gist" to identify.
- **Nuance:** (1) The district court erred by requiring the drawings to show what was "novel or important" and to exclude every diameter outside the claimed range; no drawing could do that. (2) Mahurkar's later patents claiming other ratios were irrelevant; sufficiency is judged at filing. (3) The expert explained why a skilled designer would read the drawings as fixing the claimed range (pressure tradeoffs). (4) Written description is a fact question, separate from enablement (Ruschig, DiLeone). (5) In related Mahurkar litigation over a different patent (the '968, claiming benefit of a later 1983 design application), Judge Easterbrook found that the design drawings described every claim element and gave the utility claims the design date (831 F. Supp. 1354 (N.D. Ill. 1993), aff'd, 71 F.3d 1573 (Fed. Cir. 1995)).
- **Status:** Good law; its "reasonably conveys . . . possession" standard was reaffirmed en banc in Ariad. As of 2026-11-18.
- **Skill rule:** Drafting: make the drawings carry claimable detail (relative dimensions, ratios, positions) and say in words why the key proportions matter, so later range or ratio claims have support; priority analysis: test each later claim limitation against the earlier drawings and text through a skilled reader's eyes, with an expert-style explanation.

### Ariad Pharmaceuticals, Inc. v. Eli Lilly & Co., 598 F.3d 1336 (Fed. Cir. 2010) (en banc) · CB p. 462
- **Facts:** Ariad's inventors discovered the transcription factor NF-κB and claimed methods of reducing its activity in cells (generic, single-step methods). The 1989 application proposed three classes of molecules that might do it (specific inhibitors, dominantly interfering molecules, decoy molecules) but gave no working example of any. The jury found the claims valid and infringed.
- **Holding / rule:** § 112(a) contains a written description requirement separate from enablement, and it applies to original claims too. The test is "possession as shown in the disclosure," an objective inquiry into the four corners of the spec. A genus needs a representative number of species or structural features common to the members, so that a skilled person can recognize them; functional claim language can work when the art has established a structure–function correlation. "Merely drawing a fence around the outer limits of a purported genus" is not enough. The asserted claims were invalid.
- **Nuance:** (1) Method claims that do not recite a compound still must describe some way to perform them; here that meant describing molecules that reduce NF-κB activity. (2) Each class failed: the only specific inhibitor's sequence appeared only in a 1991 filing; the dominantly interfering molecules were a hypothesis the inventors themselves could not confirm; the decoy sequences were described, but nothing linked them to actually reducing activity. (3) Post-filing evidence (a 1990 paper, later practice by others) cannot show possession at filing. (4) No examples or actual reduction to practice are required, and prophetic examples can suffice, but a "mere mention of a desired outcome" cannot. (5) A description that only renders the claim obvious is not enough (Lockwood). (6) Dissents: Rader (with Linn) would limit written description to policing priority and use enablement for everything else; Linn would treat original claims as their own description. Newman's additional views framed the case as keeping basic science, with no demonstrated application, out of the patent system.
- **Status:** Good law; applied in Juno v. Kite (2021), Regents of Univ. of Minn. v. Gilead (2023), Duke v. Sandoz (2025). No later case overrules or limits it, but its reach is bounded by In re Entresto (2025) (describe what is claimed, not after-arising products) and by Teva v. Lilly (2026), which distinguished Ariad (method of use with a known class of tools). As of 2026-11-18.
- **Skill rule:** Drafting: for every functional genus or result-defined method, disclose concrete species spread across the genus and the structural feature or mechanism tying them to the function, and connect each disclosed species to data or a reasoned showing that it achieves the result; never rely on the original claim's wording as its own support.

### Note cases (written description), CB pp. 451–486
- In re Rasmussen, 650 F.2d 1212 (C.C.P.A. 1981): new matter added to claims is rejected under 112(a) written description; new matter in the spec is objected to under § 132. **Skill rule:** every amendment response cites the page and line of original support for each new or changed limitation.
- Continuation-in-part practice (37 C.F.R. 1.53(b), CB p. 461): new matter can be added only in a CIP; each claim gets the parent's date only if fully supported there, otherwise the CIP date.
- Evans v. Eaton, 20 U.S. (7 Wheat.) 356 (1822); Rengo Co. v. Molins Mach. Co., 657 F.2d 535 (3d Cir. 1981): historical and notice rationales for a separate description requirement.
- In re Ruschig, 379 F.2d 990 (C.C.P.A. 1967): a specific compound within a broad generic disclosure, with nothing pointing to it, is not described, even if it can be made. In re DiLeone, 436 F.2d 1404 (C.C.P.A. 1971): a spec can enable compounds B and C while describing only A.
- In re Gosteli, 872 F.2d 1008 (Fed. Cir. 1989); Ralston Purina Co. v. Far-Mar-Co, 772 F.2d 1570 (Fed. Cir. 1985): the "reasonably conveys . . . possession" test for later claims.
- Aro Mfg. Co. v. Convertible Top Replacement Co., 365 U.S. 336 (1961) (cited in Vas-Cath): no legally protected "heart" of a combination; the claims define the invention.
- In re Smith, 458 F.2d 1389 (C.C.P.A. 1972); Ex parte Westphal, 26 U.S.P.Q.2d 1858 (B.P.A.I. 1992): a genus plus a species does not describe an intermediate subgenus absent specific facts showing it was implicitly described. **Skill rule:** draft intermediate subgenera expressly (broad, intermediate, narrow lists and ranges with endpoints).
- Regents of the Univ. of Cal. v. Eli Lilly & Co., 119 F.3d 1559 (Fed. Cir. 1997): rat insulin cDNA did not describe claims to vertebrate or mammalian insulin cDNA; a functional definition gives a result, not what achieves it. Fiers v. Revel, 984 F.2d 1164 (Fed. Cir. 1993): "DNA coding for interferon activity" was a wish or plan; claiming all DNAs that achieve a result is an attempt "to preempt the future."
- Enzo Biochem, Inc. v. Gen-Probe Inc., 323 F.3d 956 (Fed. Cir. 2002): a deposit can satisfy written description; functional language works with a known structure–function correlation; original claim language repeated in the spec is not automatically sufficient.
- Univ. of Rochester v. G.D. Searle & Co., 358 F.3d 916 (Fed. Cir. 2004): a method of selectively inhibiting COX-2 by administering a compound that does so, with no such compound identified, was not described.
- Capon v. Eshhar, 418 F.3d 1349 (Fed. Cir. 2005): the detail required varies with existing knowledge, the prior art, the maturity of the science, and predictability; known sequences need not be repeated. Falkner v. Inglis, 448 F.3d 1357 (Fed. Cir. 2006); Streck, Inc. v. Research & Diagnostic Sys., 665 F.3d 1269 (Fed. Cir. 2012): no examples or actual reduction to practice required; known structures need not be recited.
- Carnegie Mellon Univ. v. Hoffmann-La Roche Inc., 541 F.3d 1115 (Fed. Cir. 2008): the E. coli polA gene did not describe claims to the gene from any bacterium. Invitrogen Corp. v. Clontech Labs., 429 F.3d 1052 (Fed. Cir. 2005): representative sequences did describe a genus of enzymes.
- Lockwood v. American Airlines, 107 F.3d 1565 (Fed. Cir. 1997): a description that makes the claim obvious is not a description of it; each link in a priority chain must describe the claim.
- LizardTech, Inc. v. Earth Resource Mapping, 424 F.3d 1336 (Fed. Cir. 2005): one specific way of doing a seamless wavelet transform did not describe a claim to any way of doing it; written description is not limited to chemistry and biology. **Skill rule:** software drafting: if claims are generic, describe several implementations and the common principle.
- In re Wallach, 378 F.3d 1330 (Fed. Cir. 2004) (cited in Ariad): converting a full amino acid sequence to all encoding DNAs is now routine, so what counts as describing a nucleic-acid genus changes with the art.
- Boston Scientific Corp. v. Johnson & Johnson, 647 F.3d 1353 (Fed. Cir. 2011): claims to stents with rapamycin "analogs" were not described where the spec gave no common structure for the analog class.
- Gentry Gallery, Inc. v. Berkline Corp., 134 F.3d 1473 (Fed. Cir. 1998): where the spec made the console location of the recliner controls essential, claims without that location lacked support. **Skill rule:** never call a feature essential, critical, or required unless every claim will include it.
- Reiffin v. Microsoft Corp., 214 F.3d 1342 (Fed. Cir. 2000) (cited in Ariad): claim scope cannot overreach the contribution described.

## 3. Genus claims and later subgenus or species claims

Genus problems arise under both prongs and in both directions; the cases above combine into these rules:
- **Functional genus claimed directly** (antibodies, cDNAs, compounds defined by what they do): enablement (Amgen, Baxalta, Wright, Enzo v. Calgene) and written description (Eli Lilly, Ariad, Juno, Amgen 2017) both bite. Fix: structural definitions, representative species across the range, and the shared structure–function feature.
- **Narrower subgenus carved later from a huge genus** (continuations, amendments, interference-style copying): needs blaze marks pointing to that subgenus or a representative set within it (Ruschig, Westphal, Regents v. Gilead 2023, Duke v. Sandoz 2025).
- **Method of using a known class of tools:** the class need not be described as if it were the invention (Teva v. Lilly 2026), unlike Ariad, where no working tool was disclosed or known.
- **After-arising products the claims happen to cover:** the spec must describe and enable what is claimed, not later technology that falls within it (In re Entresto 2025); but a broad construction that reaches products the spec disclaimed or could not enable fails (Liebel-Flarsheim).

## 4. Best mode

### Chemcast Corp. v. Arco Industries Corp., 913 F.2d 923 (Fed. Cir. 1990) · CB p. 487
- **Facts:** Claim to a grommet whose locking portion must be rigid. The spec said materials of 70 Shore A hardness or harder were suitable. The inventor actually used, and contemplated only, a specific commercial compound (Reynosol R-4467) at 75 ± 5 Shore D, three hardness scales away, and disclosed neither the material, the source, nor the hardness.
- **Holding / rule:** Best mode violated; claim invalid. The analysis has two parts: (1) subjective, whether the inventor at filing knew of a mode he considered better than any other, and (2) objective, whether the disclosure was adequate to let a skilled person practice that mode. Where the inventor fails to disclose the only mode he contemplated, the requirement is violated.
- **Nuance:** Skilled readers could not have found the preferred mode or even known where to look. Contrast Randomex (preferred fluid disclosed by trade name alongside one alternative: no violation). The practical lesson: naming the material, its source or trade name, or at least its hardness on the scale the inventor used would have put the mode in the patent; silence on all three did not.
- **Status:** Superseded as an invalidity or unenforceability defense by AIA sec. 15: failure to disclose the best mode "shall not be a basis on which any claim of a patent may be canceled or held invalid or otherwise unenforceable" (35 U.S.C. 282(b)(3)(A)), for proceedings begun on or after Sept. 16, 2011. Best mode remains a requirement of 112(a) at filing (MPEP 2165), but AIA sec. 15(b) removed it from the 112(a) showing needed for benefit under 119(e)(1) and 120. As of 2026-11-18.
- **Skill rule:** Drafting (Phase 3, intake question 14): ask what the inventor would actually build or ship and disclose it in usable terms (material, grade, supplier type, key parameter on the scale the industry uses); best mode is no longer a litigation risk, but it is still a statutory duty and its omission can raise candor and foreign-filing problems.

### Note cases (best mode), CB pp. 488–490
- Randomex, Inc. v. Scopus Corp., 849 F.2d 585 (Fed. Cir. 1988): naming the preferred cleaning fluid by trade name along with one other option was not concealment; Mayer, J., dissented that it buried the best mode. No rule requires labeling a mode "best."
- Shearing v. Iolab Corp., 975 F.2d 1541 (Fed. Cir. 1992): the best mode need only be disclosed in terms a skilled person would understand; spec language about compressing and directing the lens strands sufficed. (The case also involved exhuming a patient to test a prior-use claim.)
- 18 U.S.C. 1001 (CB p. 486): knowing concealment in a filing to the government may carry criminal exposure; whether intentional best-mode concealment can still support inequitable conduct after sec. 15's "otherwise unenforceable" language is status: not verified.
- Foreign priority: commentators argue 35 U.S.C. 119(a) (Paris priority) was not amended like 119(e) and 120, so a best-mode gap in a foreign priority document might still matter. Status: argued position, not verified in case law.

## 5. Priority and provisional support for later claims

Rules: `../disclosure-112.md` §6. Cases that decide edge cases:

- **Vas-Cath (above):** an earlier application (even a design application with drawings only) supports a later claim if a skilled reader would see the claimed invention, limitation by limitation, in it.
- **New Railhead Mfg., L.L.C. v. Vermeer Mfg. Co., 298 F.3d 1290 (Fed. Cir. 2002).** The provisional's drawings did not show the claimed angle between the drill bit and sonde housing (the inventor admitted he knew of the angle from knowing the device, not from the drawings), so the nonprovisional claim got only its own date and the patentee's own commercial sales became a bar. Possession in the lab and buildability from scaled drawings did not help; adequacy is judged from the face of the application. Dyk, J., joined this holding and dissented only on a separate public-use issue. Status: good law; cited in MPEP 2163. **Skill rule:** Phase 3 provisional: every feature the user may later claim, including angles, ranges, and orientations, must appear in words or unmistakably in figures; before filing the nonprovisional, chart each claim limitation to the provisional.
- **Lockwood (above):** each application in a chain must describe the claim; a gap anywhere breaks the chain.
- **Ariad (above):** material first added in a later filing (Figure 43 in 1991) cannot support possession as of the earlier date.
- **Regents of the Univ. of Minn. v. Gilead Sciences, Inc., 61 F.4th 1350 (Fed. Cir. 2023).** Priority applications that described a vast genus in a "maze-like" way, without blaze marks to the later-claimed subgenus, did not support it; with priority lost, a Gilead publication anticipated. Status: good law. **Skill rule:** when a continuation claims a narrower subgenus, find the passage that points to it in every priority document or expect the earliest date to fall.
- **AIA best-mode carve-out:** 35 U.S.C. 119(e)(1) and 120 now require disclosure "in the manner provided by section 112(a) (other than the requirement to disclose the best mode)." **Skill rule:** never tell a user a provisional is worthless because it omits the preferred embodiment; it is weak only to the extent it lacks written description or enablement for the claims.
- **Prior art provisionals** (Dynamic Drinkware, In re Riggs): see `../disclosure-112.md` §6 and `../novelty-and-obviousness.md` §6.

# Edge-case playbook

1. **"The claim is supported; the original claims said exactly that."** Trap: in haec verba support ends the inquiry. Correct: generic or functional language in the original filing does not by itself describe the genus; possession must be shown by species or common structure. Cases: Ariad, Enzo v. Gen-Probe, Fiers, Eli Lilly.
2. **Enabled, therefore described.** Trap: if a skilled person could make it, it is described. Correct: separate tests; a disclosure of compound A may enable B and C without describing them, and a description that only makes the claim obvious is not enough. Cases: DiLeone, Ariad, Lockwood.
3. **The inventor really had it in the lab.** Trap: notebooks prove possession. Correct: possession must be shown in the application itself, as filed. Cases: Ariad, New Railhead.
4. **Drawings only.** Trap: no text, no support. Correct: drawings can describe claimed features, including ranges, if a skilled reader would see them; but a feature that cannot be discerned from the figures is unsupported. Cases: Vas-Cath vs. New Railhead.
5. **Later evidence.** Trap: later papers prove the method worked, so it was enabled and described. Correct: post-filing evidence cannot supply possession or enablement as of filing (Ariad's 1990 paper, Wright's later vaccines), although a later publication may show what the art was like at filing (Wright's 1988 article).
6. **Functional genus with many working examples.** Trap: 26 sequenced antibodies is plenty. Correct: examples must be tied to a general quality shared by the class; screen-and-test roadmaps are research assignments. Cases: Amgen (2023), Baxalta (2023), Juno (2021).
7. **Method claim that does not name a compound.** Trap: no compound in the claim, so no need to describe compounds. Correct: if the method needs agents and none is described or known, the method is not described (Ariad, Rochester). Flip: if the tools are a known class and the invention is the use, the class need not be described as the invention (Teva v. Lilly, 2026).
8. **Product invented later falls within the claim.** Trap: the patent must have described the later product to cover it. Correct: describe and enable what is claimed; after-arising technology inside the claim does not by itself invalidate (In re Entresto, 2025). But if the patentee's own broad construction reaches embodiments the spec disclaimed or could not enable, the claim fails (Liebel-Flarsheim, AK Steel).
9. **Narrow subgenus from a huge genus.** Trap: narrower claims are easier to support. Correct: a subgenus buried in a vast disclosure needs blaze marks or representative species; narrowing can lose support. Cases: Ruschig, Westphal, Regents v. Gilead (2023), Duke v. Sandoz (2025).
10. **Thin provisional.** Trap: any provisional locks the date for everything. Correct: claims get the provisional date only for what it describes and enables; intervening disclosures, including the inventor's own sales or papers, become prior art for the rest. Cases: New Railhead, Regents v. Gilead.
11. **Provisional missing the best mode.** Trap: no best mode, no priority. Correct: after the AIA, 119(e)(1) and 120 exclude best mode from the benefit test, and failure to disclose it cannot invalidate a patent (282(b)(3)(A)). It is still a filing-time requirement; Paris-priority treatment is unsettled.
12. **Best mode hidden in a list or a different unit.** Trap (pre-AIA): any mention suffices. Correct: one alternative alongside the preferred mode was fine (Randomex), but a wrong hardness scale with no material or source was concealment (Chemcast). Post-AIA the litigation consequence is gone; still disclose clearly.
13. **Prophetic examples.** Trap: only real data enable. Correct: prophetic examples can enable and describe (Atlas Powder, Ariad), but must be in present or future tense, never presented as performed (MPEP 2164.02); a bare statement of a desired outcome is not an example (Ariad's decoys).
14. **Some claimed combinations do not work.** Trap: any inoperative embodiment invalidates. Correct: inoperative species are tolerated if a skilled person can readily identify working ones (a selection rule, as in Atlas Powder); not if they are numerous in an unpredictable art (Pharmaceutical Resources).
15. **Arguing unpredictability to beat obviousness.** Trap: a free argument. Correct: it becomes evidence of non-enablement for broad claims; the same skilled person governs 103 and 112. Cases: Pharmaceutical Resources; Kubin is the mirror image (see `obviousness.md`).
16. **"Routine to a skilled person" for the novel part.** Trap: skill fills gaps. Correct: the spec, not the art, must supply the novel aspects; general skill fills in only conventional details. Case: Genentech v. Novo.
17. **Efficacy words in the claim or preamble.** Trap: "vaccine," "treating a patient," or "effective" are labels. Correct: they are limitations that must be enabled (Wright: protective, not merely antigenic) and described; a limiting preamble, including a Jepson preamble's "known" elements, needs written description support (In re Xencor, 2025).
18. **Wrong scientific explanation.** Trap: a mistaken theory voids the patent. Correct: the inventor need not understand why it works (Diamond Rubber); keep theory out of the claims.
19. **Living material.** Trap: describe it in words and move on. Correct: if it cannot be reproduced from the text, a public deposit may be needed (Lundak; 37 C.F.R. 1.801–1.809); a deposit can also support written description (Enzo v. Gen-Probe).
20. **Design application as parent of a utility claim.** Trap: design drawings cannot support utility claims. Correct: they can if they convey every claimed limitation to a skilled reader (Vas-Cath); utility claims needing features not visible fail.
21. **"Essential" language in the spec.** Trap: harmless emphasis. Correct: calling a feature essential can make broader claims without it unsupported (Gentry Gallery) and can drive a narrow construction.
22. **Patent covers two technologies, describes one.** Trap: describing one well is enough. Correct: if the claim reaches both, both must be enabled and described (Automotive Techs. v. BMW, LizardTech).
23. **Production data omitted.** Trap: the spec must give tolerances and dimensions. Correct: patents are not production documents (Christianson); enable the claimed features only.
24. **Later, different claims from the same spec after an enablement loss.** Trap: if one claim set failed, the spec is worthless. Correct: enablement is claim-specific; Gould lost the Q-switch count but later enforced optical-amplifier claims from the same 1959 disclosure, though a jury held four of them invalid (Gould v. Control Laser). Re-draft to what the spec supports.

# Post-casebook developments (after 2018, through Nov. 2026)

Covered elsewhere and not repeated here: In re Riggs (2025) and Dynamic Drinkware (2015) in `../disclosure-112.md` §6.

- **Amgen Inc. v. Sanofi, 872 F.3d 1367 (Fed. Cir. 2017).** Rejected the "newly characterized antigen" test: describing a novel antigen does not by itself describe antibodies that bind it; a jury instruction allowing written description from ability to make and use was error. USPTO later removed the test from its guidance. Status: good law. **Skill rule:** antibody drafting: disclose antibody sequences (CDRs) and binding data, not only the antigen and epitope.
- **Idenix Pharms. LLC v. Gilead Sciences Inc., 941 F.3d 1149 (Fed. Cir. 2019)** (cert. denied Jan. 19, 2021). Claims to methods of treating HCV with a broad class of 2'-methyl nucleosides were not enabled (many thousands of candidates, testing needed) and not described (no blaze marks to Gilead's 2'-methyl-up/2'-fluoro-down species). Status: good law. **Skill rule:** when a competitor's species falls inside a broad claim, check whether the spec singles out that substitution pattern anywhere.
- **Juno Therapeutics, Inc. v. Kite Pharma, Inc., 10 F.4th 1330 (Fed. Cir. 2021).** Reversed a roughly $1.2 billion judgment: claims to CAR constructs whose scFv binds a selected target (the broadest covering any target of clinical interest) lacked written description; two scFvs named only by designation (no sequences) were not representative, and general scFv structure was not a common feature tied to binding. Status: good law. **Skill rule:** name species by structure (sequences), not by lab designation.
- **Regents of the Univ. of Minn. v. Gilead Sciences, Inc., 61 F.4th 1350 (Fed. Cir. 2023).** See section 5: no blaze marks to the claimed subgenus, priority lost, and a Gilead publication anticipated. Status: good law.
- **Amgen Inc. v. Sanofi, 598 U.S. 594 (2023).** Principal entry above.
- **Baxalta Inc. v. Genentech, Inc., 81 F.4th 1362 (Fed. Cir. 2023).** Functional antibody claims (millions of candidates, 11 sequences disclosed) not enabled, "materially indistinguishable" from Amgen; Wands still applies. Status: good law. **Skill rule:** treat any functional-antibody or functional-protein genus as presumptively at risk unless the spec shows a shared structural feature.
- **USPTO, Guidelines for Assessing Enablement in Utility Applications and Patents in View of Amgen v. Sanofi, 89 FR 1563 (Jan. 10, 2024).** Examiners keep using Wands to judge whether experimentation for the full scope is reasonable; Amgen is not limited to antibodies or biotech. **Skill rule:** office actions: expect Wands-based enablement rejections citing Amgen for any functional genus, in any art.
- **In re Entresto, 125 F.4th 1090 (Fed. Cir. 2025).** Reversed a written-description invalidation: claims to a composition of valsartan and sacubitril administered in combination were described and enabled even though the accused product (infringement was stipulated) was a valsartan–sacubitril complex discovered four years after the priority date; written description concerns what is claimed, not after-arising technology. The district court had relied mainly on Chiron v. Genentech (2004); the panel reversed without adopting that reasoning. Status: precedential; still cited by the Federal Circuit in Oct. 2026. **Skill rule:** analysis: when a challenger says the spec did not describe the accused later form, ask first whether the claim recites that form or merely covers it.
- **In re Xencor, Inc., No. 24-1870 (Fed. Cir. Mar. 13, 2025).** A Jepson claim's preamble limits the claim and needs written description support, even for elements said to be well known; a companion claim's "treating a patient" preamble was also held limiting and unsupported. **Skill rule:** drafting: support every preamble element in the spec; do not use a Jepson preamble to import "known" matter you have not described.
- **Duke Univ. v. Sandoz Inc., No. 24-1078 (Fed. Cir. Nov. 18, 2025).** Reversed a $39 million verdict: a claim to a subgenus of roughly 1,600–4,200 prostaglandin compounds, carved from a disclosure of billions, lacked written description; no representative species, no common structural features, no blaze marks. Status: precedential; reporter cite not verified. **Skill rule:** before filing continuation claims to a subgenus, confirm the original spec names that subgenus or enough members of it.
- **Teva Pharms. Int'l GmbH v. Eli Lilly & Co., No. 24-1094 (Fed. Cir. Apr. 16, 2026).** Reversed JMOL of no written description and no enablement for methods of treating headache with humanized anti-CGRP antagonist antibodies: the claims were to a method of use, the antibodies were a known class of tools (Lilly had told the PTAB humanization was routine), and the case would resemble Amgen only if the antibody genus itself were claimed. Status: precedential; commentary frames it as a method-of-use vs. product line. **Skill rule:** Phase 3 claims: where the novelty is a new use of a known class of agents, claim the method of use as a separate independent claim, and describe the known class by citation plus representative members.

# Checklist for the skill (112(a))

- For each claim, identify the effective filing date sought and test enablement and written description against that document only.
- Size every genus: how many members, how many described by structure, and what shared feature ties structure to function.
- Ask for at least one complete operable embodiment with concrete parameters; for first-of-kind inventions, more than one.
- Harvest variants into explicit broad, intermediate, and narrow lists and ranges with endpoints (supports later subgenus claims).
- Describe what is new in detail; use "known in the art" only for conventional parts.
- Label prophetic examples in present or future tense; tie each to a reasoned basis.
- Disclose any selection rule or screening test that tells a skilled person which alternatives work.
- Disclose the inventor's actual preferred mode in industry-standard units and terms.
- Avoid "essential," "critical," and "the invention requires" unless every claim will include the feature.
- Keep scientific theory out of the claims; hedge it in the spec.
- Check every preamble element (and Jepson "known" elements) for support.
- For biologics, plan sequences, data, and any deposit before filing.
- Before arguing unpredictability (103) or predictability (112), check that the position is consistent across both and across all pending claims.
- For a nonprovisional or continuation, chart each claim limitation to the earlier application and flag any limitation found only in the later filing.
- In amendments, cite page and line support for every new or changed limitation.
- In invalidity mode, test functional claims under Amgen and Ariad, and test subgenus claims for blaze marks.
