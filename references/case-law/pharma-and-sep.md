# Hatch-Waxman, BPCIA and SEP/FRAND

Citations tagged `[memory – verify]` were not checked this session. Apply SKILL.md source-verification requirements before any of them appear in output. This file is a doctrine map for screening and drafting. Use it to frame supported, scope-specific risks; it cannot by itself establish launch clearance or a definitive FRAND determination.

## A. Statutory frame (short)

- Safe harbor, 35 U.S.C. 271(e)(1): not infringement to make, use, offer, sell or import a patented invention "solely for uses reasonably related to the development and submission of information" under a federal drug law.
- Artificial infringement, 271(e)(2)(A): submitting an ANDA or 505(b)(2) application for a drug "claimed in a patent" (or whose use is claimed) is itself an infringement. Remedies are in 271(e)(4); no damages until marketing, but injunction against approval or launch before expiry.
- Paragraph certifications on each Orange Book patent: I no patent info, II expired, III will wait for expiry, IV invalid, unenforceable or not infringed. Para IV requires detailed notice to patentee and NDA holder; patentee has 45 days to sue; suit triggers an automatic stay of FDA approval until the earliest of patent expiry, a favorable judgment, or 30 months from notice. A court may shorten or lengthen the stay if a party fails to cooperate in expediting. For a new chemical entity (NCE), a para IV ANDA may be filed at year 4, and the stay extends so that 7.5 years run from NDA approval (slides: 5 years + 30 months).
- Section viii statement: for a method-of-use patent that does not claim a use the generic seeks, the generic carves that use out of its label ("skinny label") instead of certifying.
- Regulatory exclusivities run independently of patents (NCE 5 years; new clinical study 3 years, only for the changed use; orphan 7; pediatric +6 months on top of patents and other exclusivities; first-filer generic 180 days; biologic reference product 12 years, applications from year 4). Patent term extension (35 U.S.C. 156): one patent per approved product, application within 60 days of approval, half of testing phase plus all of FDA review, cap 5 years and 14 years post-approval term, scope limited to the approved product/use.
- Brand-side Orange Book listing: only patents claiming the drug substance, drug product (formulation or composition) or a method of use may be listed. Process, packaging, metabolite and intermediate patents may not (21 C.F.R. 314.53(b)(1), per 2003 rule, 68 Fed. Reg. 36676). Biologics have no Orange Book; the Purple Book lists licensed products and has no patent list in the version the casebook describes. Status of the later Purple Book patent-list requirements: not verified.

## B. Safe harbor scope

### Classen Immunotherapies, Inc. v. Biogen Idec, 659 F.3d 1057 (Fed. Cir. 2011) · CB p. 1078
- **Facts:** Patentee sued vaccine makers over methods of choosing immunization schedules. Defendants said their studies and post-approval reporting to FDA were protected by 271(e)(1).
- **Holding / rule:** Majority held 271(e)(1) is aimed at pre-marketing approval activity and does not cover information routinely reported to FDA long after approval. Preclinical work counts only if there is a reasonable basis to expect it yields information relevant to an IND or NDA (Merck KGaA v. Integra, 545 U.S. 193 (2005)).
- **Nuance:** Judge Moore dissented in part: the text has no pre-/post-approval limit, and Integra read it broadly ("all uses ... reasonably related"). Her dissent still would have held that actually vaccinating patients per the method is outside the safe harbor, because no law required it. Merck won on a separate no-evidence-of-involvement ground, not the safe harbor.
- **Status:** Momenta I (below) treated itself as bound by Classen and distinguished it (the testing there was FDA-required, not "routine"). Momenta II (2015, below) then held routine record-retention testing tied to commercial production unprotected, which is close to Classen's result. Amgen v. Hospira and Edwards v. Meril (post-casebook section) do not cite Classen. The same opinion's 101 holding (eligibility of the claims) was undercut by Mayo (2012); later history of that holding: not verified, check before citing it for eligibility.
- **Skill rule:** FTO and defenses: never tell a user that "post-approval activity is outside the safe harbor"; test each act for a reasonable relation to FDA-required information and note the Federal Circuit's shifting reasoning (Classen, Momenta I, Momenta II). Never treat commercial supply, marketing, or routine use as protected.

### Momenta Pharmaceuticals, Inc. v. Amphastar Pharmaceuticals, Inc., 686 F.3d 1348 (Fed. Cir. 2012) · CB p. 1085 (note)
- **Facts:** Patent on a method of analyzing enoxaparin batches. Generic ran the method on post-approval commercial batches because FDA's identity standards required it.
- **Holding / rule:** Safe harbor applied: data required to keep FDA approval, not "routine," is reasonably related to a submission. No duty to pick a non-infringing test method if one exists.
- **Nuance:** Rader dissented (pre/post-approval line). Matters most for analytical, quality-control and manufacturing-process method claims. Product and composition claims are seldom hit by a QC safe-harbor argument.
- **Status:** cut back in the same dispute. In Momenta Pharmaceuticals, Inc. v. Teva Pharmaceuticals USA Inc., 809 F.3d 610 (Fed. Cir. 2015) ("Momenta II"), the court held that "routine record retention requirements associated with testing and other aspects of the commercial production process" are not safe-harbored, and declined to follow Momenta I as law of the case to avoid manifest injustice (as described in Edwards v. Meril, majority and Lourie dissent, and Amgen v. Hospira n.3). Edwards and Hospira cite Momenta II, not Momenta I.
- **Skill rule:** Claim drafting for biologics and complex generics: do not rely only on analytical-method claims to block a generic, because pre-approval testing is safe-harbored and post-approval testing has gone both ways (Momenta I, Momenta II). Claim the product, composition or the manufacturing step itself.

### Note cases in this part (compact)
- Roche v. Bolar, 733 F.2d 858 (Fed. Cir. 1984): no common-law experimental-use defense for generic pre-expiry testing; Congress answered with 271(e)(1) and PTE. Skill rule: for FTO never raise common-law experimental use for commercial-purpose R&D; see `defenses.md`.
- Eli Lilly & Co. v. Medtronic, 496 U.S. 661 (1990): safe harbor extends to medical devices; 271(e)(2) defines a "somewhat artificial" (elsewhere "highly artificial") act of infringement: submitting the application, before any commercial manufacture, use or sale. Skill rule: apply the safe harbor to device FDA submissions too.
- Merck KGaA v. Integra, 545 U.S. 193 (2005): broad reading; preclinical use in work reasonably expected to yield IND/NDA-type information is covered, even if not submitted. Skill rule: for research-tool patents, ask whether the user's use plausibly feeds an FDA submission; research tools used only as tools are a different question (the Court expressly left the "research tools" question open, Merck KGaA, n.7).

## C. Orange Book listing and listing disputes

### Mylan Pharmaceuticals, Inc. v. Thompson, 268 F.3d 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2001) · CB p. 1087
- **Facts:** Casebook excerpt prints only the statutory background (certifications, 45 days, 30-month stay, bar on DJ actions during the 45 days). The actual dispute: an ANDA applicant sued the NDA holder and FDA, alleging a patent was wrongly listed.
- **Holding / rule (from later descriptions; the no-private-action point is confirmed by Teva v. Amneal (Fed. Cir. 2024), citing 268 F.3d at 1332, and by Caraco, 566 U.S. 399; the DJ point is secondhand):** No private right of action under the FFDCA to force delisting; FDCA enforcement belongs to the United States (21 U.S.C. 337(a)). A DJ route "arising under" patent law failed because the delisting claim was not a patent-law defense. Reaffirmed in Andrx v. Biovail, 276 F.3d 1368 (Fed. Cir. 2002).
- **Nuance:** The excerpt does not show the holding; do not quote the casebook text for it. I have not read the opinion itself.
- **Status:** superseded in part by statute: the 2003 Medicare Modernization Act added a counterclaim to correct or delete listing (21 U.S.C. 355(j)(5)(C)(ii)), usable only as a counterclaim, never as an independent action. Caraco v. Novo Nordisk, 566 U.S. 399 (2012) held a generic may use the counterclaim to force correction of a use code that inaccurately describes the patent as covering a method of use.
- **Skill rule:** FTO for generics: a wrongly listed patent is attacked by counterclaim after being sued, not by a standalone suit; never advise "sue to delist" as a first move.

### aaiPharma Inc. v. Thompson, 296 F.3d 227 (4th Cir. 2002) · CB p. 1094
- **Facts:** Third-party patentee obtained a polymorph patent on Prozac's active ingredient hours before generics launched; NDA holder Lilly would not list it. aaiPharma sued FDA under the APA.
- **Holding / rule:** FDA's role in Orange Book listing is ministerial under Chevron step two; it need not police whether a patent "claims" the drug. A third-party patentee cannot sue the NDA holder to compel listing, and the NDA holder alone submits patents.
- **Nuance:** The court admitted the enforcement gap. It noted that wrongful listing is the more serious harm (30-month stay for a patent that should not carry it) and that an aggrieved party had no remedy then.
- **Status:** Chevron deference was overruled by Loper Bright (2024), so the reasoning route is dead, but the result stands because the statute still has no FDA enforcement of listing correctness; the 2003 counterclaim answers the wrongful-listing half. Teva v. Amneal (Fed. Cir. Dec. 20, 2024, No. 2024-1936; reporter cite not verified) affirmed a delisting order for inhaler device patents: a listable patent must claim at least the active ingredient. Check any post-2024 FDA rule or guidance before relying on listing criteria.
- **Skill rule:** Pharma FTO: do not equate "listed in the Orange Book" with "claims the drug." Check the claims for the active ingredient (device or packaging-only claims are listing-suspect). A listed patent still forces a certification and can carry a stay until it is delisted by counterclaim.

### Teva Pharmaceuticals USA, Inc. v. Pfizer, Inc., 395 F.3d 1324 (Fed. Cir. 2005) · CB p. 1084 (note)
- Held no DJ jurisdiction for a para IV filer against a passive patentee under the reasonable-apprehension test. Overruled in substance by MedImmune and Teva v. Novartis (below). Skill rule: never use "reasonable apprehension of suit" as a jurisdiction test.

## D. Declaratory judgment standing

### MedImmune, Inc. v. Genentech, Inc., 549 U.S. 118 (2007) · CB p. 1108
- **Facts:** Licensee paid royalties under protest on a new patent and sued for invalidity and non-infringement without breaching or terminating the license.
- **Holding / rule:** Article III is satisfied if, under all the circumstances, there is a substantial controversy between adverse parties of sufficient immediacy and reality. A licensee need not repudiate the license first; paying under threat of treble damages and a sales-ending injunction is enough.
- **Nuance:** Footnote 11 said the Federal Circuit's "reasonable apprehension of imminent suit" test conflicts with Supreme Court precedent. District courts still have discretion to decline declaratory relief; the Court left that to remand. Contract clauses (no-challenge, termination on challenge) were not decided. Note Lear v. Adkins limits licensee estoppel.
- **Status:** good law. Follow-on: SanDisk v. STMicroelectronics, 480 F.3d 1372 (Fed. Cir. 2007) held a case or controversy exists where the patentee asserts rights on identified activity and the other side says it needs no license.
- **Skill rule:** Landscape and FTO: any claim-chart-backed licence offer or asserted-rights letter may create DJ standing. Advise that outreach and charting leave a paper trail that can start a suit in the other side's chosen forum, and flag it to the user before sending anything.

### Teva Pharmaceuticals USA, Inc. v. Novartis Pharmaceuticals Corp., 482 F.3d 1330 (Fed. Cir. 2007) · CB p. 1116
- **Facts:** Novartis listed five Famvir patents; Teva certified para IV on all; Novartis sued on the compound patent (expiring 2010) only, leaving four later method patents unasserted. Teva sought a DJ on the four ("civil action to obtain patent certainty", 21 U.S.C. 355(j)(5)(C); 35 U.S.C. 271(e)(5)).
- **Holding / rule:** Jurisdiction exists. The ANDA submission is a single act of infringement; the pending suit on one patent, the listings, the para IV certifications, the stay, the remaining exposure to 271(e)(2) suits (six-year damages limit), and the statutory scheme together made a real controversy. The court said these together are enough as to all certified patents when the patentee has sued on one or more.
- **Nuance:** Judge Friedman concurred on a shorter path: listing plus certification plus a suit on one related patent shows controversy. The majority leaves open the case where the patentee sues on none; its full-circumstances analysis suggests Congress expected jurisdiction in almost every case except a covenant not to sue. The opinion misnames the ANDA act as 271(e)(1) in one passage; the act is 271(e)(2). The statute lets the DJ plaintiff proceed only after the 45 days pass without suit.
- **Status:** good law as applied; later cases require that the DJ patent be one the ANDA certified. The 2003 DJ provisions remain.
- **Skill rule:** FTO for a generic: a patentee's choice to sue on one listed patent does not shield the others from a DJ. Chart each listed patent against the ANDA product, not only the one in suit.

## E. Biologics

### Sandoz Inc. v. Amgen Inc., 582 U.S. 1 (2017), 137 S. Ct. 1664 · CB p. 1132
- **Facts:** Sandoz filed a biosimilar filgrastim application (Zarxio), skipped the information exchange, and gave notice of marketing before FDA licensure.
- **Holding / rule:** (1) The duty to give the sponsor the application and manufacturing information (42 U.S.C. 262(l)(2)(A)) is not enforceable by federal injunction; the only federal consequence is that the sponsor may sue immediately for a declaration (262(l)(9)(C)) and the applicant loses its own DJ right. (2) The 180-day notice of commercial marketing (262(l)(8)(A)) may be given before licensure.
- **Nuance:** State-law remedies (UCL) were remanded; whether the applicant "unlawfully" skipped the dance is a question of state law. The Court did not decide that the dance is optional in every sense: skipping it gives the sponsor control of timing and scope. Applicant benefits from dancing: choice of how many patents go to first-wave litigation, and a bar on DJ actions on unlisted patents until notice.
- **Status:** good law. On remand the Federal Circuit held the BPCIA preempts California UCL and conversion claims based on skipping the dance (Amgen v. Sandoz, 877 F.3d 1315 (Fed. Cir. 2017)). The 12-year reference-product exclusivity and 4-year filing bar are statutory.
- **Skill rule:** Biologics FTO: expect no public patent list; the lists exchanged in the dance are confidential. Search the sponsor's patents by product, process, formulation and use, plus pending applications, because the dance can bring in 100+ patents. Ask whether the user has seen a dance notice; the tool never concludes freedom to launch.

### Note items (compact)
- 271(e)(2)(C): submission of a 351(k) application is artificial infringement for patents on the lists, or for any patent that could have been listed if the applicant skipped the information exchange. Skill rule: for a biosimilar, treat every sponsor patent claiming product, use, or manufacture as in play.
- Sponsor's 60-day list and applicant's 60-day responses, 30-day suit deadline after list agreement, applicant's control of first-wave number (sponsor always gets at least one). Skill rule: when advising a biosimilar user, flag that number control is a strategy lever, not a legal right to avoid litigation.
- Purple Book has no patents. Skill rule: never write "no Purple Book patents" as a clearance signal.

## F. Skinny labels and inducement

### GlaxoSmithKline LLC v. Teva Pharmaceuticals USA, Inc., 7 F.4th 1320 (Fed. Cir. 2021) (per curiam, on panel rehearing; replaced the Oct. 2, 2020 opinion) · not in casebook pages
- **Facts:** GSK's reissue patent (RE40,000) claimed a method of decreasing mortality caused by congestive heart failure (CHF) with carvedilol. Teva's 2007 section viii label carved out the CHF use (use code U-233) and kept the hypertension and post-MI left ventricular dysfunction (LVD) indications; in 2011 FDA had Teva add the CHF indication back. Teva's press releases and catalogs called the product an "AB-rated generic equivalent" of Coreg and "indicated for treatment of heart failure and hypertension." The jury found inducement.
- **Holding / rule:** Vacated JMOL and reinstated the verdict and damages: substantial evidence supported inducement during both the partial-label and full-label periods. The jury could find the partial label itself (post-MI LVD indication plus clinical-study text) instructed the claimed method, so it was not a true skinny label; label plus marketing statements calling the product "AB-rated" or a generic version of the brand, plus circumstantial evidence, supported active inducement. Judge Prost dissented. Rehearing en banc was denied Feb. 11, 2022 (25 F.4th 949 (Fed. Cir. 2022)): Prost, joined by Dyk and Reyna, dissented, and Dyk and Reyna each also dissented. Cert denied May 15, 2023 (No. 22-37) after the Solicitor General was invited to file; Justice Kavanaugh would have granted. The case was seen as chilling skinny labels.
- **Nuance:** The product label alone did not control; the court weighed outside statements and substantial circumstantial proof that physicians would use the product for the patented use.
- **Status:** limited. Hikma v. Amarin, 608 U.S. 580, 590 n.3 (2026), cited GSK (7 F.4th at 1336–1337) as an example of the Federal Circuit's recent focus on whether statements "could be read" as instructions to infringe and said "We reject that trend today." The Court did not expressly overrule GSK, and it relied on GSK (7 F.4th at 1335) for the point that an AB rating means equivalence only under the generic's labeled conditions (608 U.S. at 595).
- **Skill rule:** Treat as history. In pharma FTO, do not rely on GSK for how a court will now treat a carve-out; use Hikma.

### Amarin Pharma, Inc. v. Hikma Pharmaceuticals USA Inc., 104 F.4th 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2024) (No. 2023-1169, June 25, 2024), rev'd, Hikma Pharmaceuticals USA Inc. v. Amarin Pharma, Inc., 608 U.S. 580 (2026) (No. 24-889, argued Apr. 29, decided June 4, 2026)
- **Facts:** Hikma's generic icosapent ethyl used a section viii label that carved out the patented cardiovascular (CV) use and kept only the severe-hypertriglyceridemia indication, whose patents had been held invalid. Amarin pleaded inducement from the label (CV limitation-of-use statement removed, clinical-study text kept), the patient leaflet, Hikma's website ("AB" rating, "hypertriglyceridemia" category), and press releases calling the product "generic Vascepa" and citing Vascepa's total sales.
- **Holding / rule (608 U.S. 580):** Unanimous Court (Jackson, J.; no separate writings) held Amarin failed to state a 271(b) claim and reversed. The question is whether the defendant actively encouraged infringement, not merely whether doctors could plausibly read its statements as instructions (at 584). "Active steps" means affirmative, culpable conduct (Grokster); ordinary acts incident to distribution do not count (at 590). Statements with an obvious lawful explanation do not count: label text the statute requires to match the brand's, and calling a product the "generic equivalent" (normal industry practice) (at 592–593). Omissions (the missing CV limitation, press releases not mentioning the narrower indication) cannot ground inducement (at 593–594). The leaflet, the website category and AB rating, and investor-directed sales figures were too vague; they made inducement possible, not plausible (at 594–595).
- **Nuance:** A pleading-stage holding, not a safe harbor for every skinny label. Encouragement may be implicit but must be "clear" to the audience and "affirmative"; the Court rejected Hikma's argument that it must be express (at 592). Everyone agreed the label alone did not induce. Hikma did not dispute direct infringement or intent before the Court (at 590 n.2). Footnote 3 rejected the Federal Circuit's "could be read" trend (citing GSK) without expressly overruling GSK. On remand the Federal Circuit vacated its 2024 opinion and judgment and sent the case back to the District of Delaware (nonprecedential order, July 15, 2026); later proceedings: not verified.
- **Status:** controlling Supreme Court authority (June 4, 2026). The Federal Circuit's 2024 opinion is vacated; do not cite it.
- **Skill rule:** Pharma FTO: when the product is a generic with a section viii label, screen the label, the website, press releases, sales detail and investor statements for affirmative references to the patented use; a court looks at all outward statements, not only the label. Recommend that the user (not the tool) preserve a review file of all outward statements. Remove the patented indication from every channel, not only the label.

## G. Standard-essential patents and FRAND

### Microsoft Corp. v. Motorola, Inc., No. C10-1823JLR, 2013 WL 2111217 (W.D. Wash. Apr. 25, 2013) · CB p. 1149
- **Facts:** Motorola offered its H.264 and 802.11 SEPs at 2.25 percent of end-product price and sought an injunction in Germany. Microsoft sued for breach of the RAND commitment to ITU and IEEE.
- **Holding / rule:** Court set a RAND rate and range by simulating a hypothetical bilateral negotiation with modified Georgia-Pacific factors; the royalty must reflect the patent's contribution to the standard apart from the value of being standardized, and must account for hold-up and stacking. Rate for the H.264 portfolio: 0.555 cents per unit.
- **Nuance:** Bottom-up method (patent by patent, comparables including the MPEG LA pool). Stacking arithmetic: 52 H.264 SEP owners at Motorola's ask would total 35 to 44 percent of price. Factors 4 and 5 (licensor's no-licence policy; competitor relationship) were held inapplicable. Casebook notes dispute whether the court over-weighted hold-up compared with hold-out. Related: the Ninth Circuit affirmed the anti-suit injunction against Motorola's German injunction (Microsoft v. Motorola, 696 F.3d 872 (9th Cir. 2012)); a later Ninth Circuit opinion, Microsoft Corp. v. Motorola, Inc., 795 F.3d 1024 (9th Cir. 2015), affirmed the judgment for Microsoft, including the bench-trial RAND rate determination and the jury's $14.52 million breach verdict.
- **Status:** district-court method affirmed by the Ninth Circuit (795 F.3d 1024) in a contract case; not binding in the Federal Circuit, where Ericsson v. D-Link's apportionment instruction governs SEP damages.
- **Skill rule:** Landscape for telecom and video: never quote 0.555 cents as "the" rate. Use it as an example of a court's method. A rate the tool computes is never labeled FRAND.

### In re Innovatio IP Ventures, LLC Patent Litigation, 2013 WL 5593609 (N.D. Ill. Oct. 3, 2013) · CB p. 1134 (note)
- Top-down method: fix the aggregate royalty for the standard, then allocate the patentee's share. Contrast bottom-up (Microsoft). UK court in Unwired Planet uses top-down as a cross-check. Skill rule: when estimating exposure, present both approaches and the assumptions; do not choose one silently.

### Ericsson, Inc. v. D-Link Systems, Inc., 773 F.3d 1201 (Fed. Cir. 2014) · CB p. 1160
- **Facts:** Jury awarded about 15 cents per device on Wi-Fi (802.11n) SEPs; Ericsson had a RAND commitment to IEEE.
- **Holding / rule:** A royalty for a SEP must be apportioned to the value of the patented feature, not the whole standard, and must exclude any value due to the patent being standardized. No Georgia-Pacific-style list for every RAND case; instruct only on factors relevant to the record, and on the actual RAND commitment. Hold-up and stacking instructions need actual evidence, not theory. Damages vacated.
- **Nuance:** Exception: if the invention is the whole value of the standard, apportionment is unnecessary. Entire-market-value rule still applies to multi-component products. Casebook notes empirical doubts about hold-up and stacking.
- **Status:** good law on apportionment. Later extension: CSIRO v. Cisco, 809 F.3d 1295 (Fed. Cir. 2015) held that reasonable royalties for SEPs generally, not only RAND-encumbered ones, must exclude any value flowing from the standard's adoption, and vacated an award the district court had raised because the patent was standard-essential (it also accepted a royalty model built on the parties' actual licensing talks). Other FRAND US cases: TCL v. Ericsson, 943 F.3d 1360 (Fed. Cir. 2019) vacated a bench-trial "release payment" for past sales because it was legal relief carrying Ericsson's Seventh Amendment jury right, and also vacated the dependent findings that Ericsson's offers were not FRAND and the prospective FRAND rates; remanded.
- **Skill rule:** Landscape and damages reasoning: when the target patent is a declared SEP, first ask "what part of the standard does this claim read on," then "what is its incremental value apart from standardization."

### Core Wireless Licensing S.A.R.L. v. Apple Inc., No. 2017-2102 (Fed. Cir. Aug. 16, 2018) (precedential) [reporter cite 899 F.3d 1356 not verified; casebook: 2018 WL 3893122] · CB p. 1166 (note)
- Failure to disclose a patent application to ETSI while proposing the technology can create an implied waiver or licence if the patentee had a duty of disclosure and breached it; the district court's reasons (proposal rejected, claims not final) did not support rejecting waiver; vacated and remanded. See also Hynix v. Rambus, 645 F.3d 1336 (Fed. Cir. 2011).
- **Skill rule:** Drafting and portfolio: if the user takes part in a standards body, run the IPR disclosure duty and timing question before filing or amending claims that read on the draft standard.

### Huawei Technologies Co. v. ZTE Corp., Case C-170/13, EU:C:2015:477 (CJEU 2015) · CB p. 1167
- **Facts:** German referral on when an SEP owner abuses a dominant position by seeking an injunction.
- **Holding / rule:** SEP owner who made a FRAND commitment does not abuse dominance by seeking an injunction or recall if, before suing, it (1) alerts the infringer, naming the patent and how it is infringed, and (2) after the infringer shows willingness, presents a specific written FRAND offer stating the royalty and how it is calculated; and the infringer has not diligently responded in good faith. An infringer who rejects must promptly counter-offer on FRAND terms and give security for past use. Parties may agree to third-party determination. Damages and accounting claims are not barred.
- **Nuance:** Replaced the German Orange-Book-Standard (BGH 2009) allocation, which put the first move and security on the implementer. National courts differ on whether the steps are strictly sequential; the Commission is reported to have urged strict sequence in an amicus filing in VoiceAge v. HMD in Munich (filing and outcome: not verified). Unwired Planet (UKSC) held the Huawei steps are not mandatory in England.
- **Status:** good law in the EU; remains the reference framework. EU SEP Regulation proposal: withdrawal announced by the Commission in February 2025 (secondary source); EUR-Lex records the formal withdrawal on Oct. 6, 2025 (procedure 2023/0133/COD). Later BGH decisions: not verified.
- **Skill rule:** SEP FTO and licensing: advise a user that an injunction risk in Germany or the UPC depends on documented steps (notice, offer, counter-offer, security); preserve timestamps and send nothing without the human's approval.

### Japan Patent Office, Guide to Licensing Negotiations Involving Standard Essential Patents (2018) · CB p. 1176
- **Content:** Not law. A five-step good-faith framework based on Huawei v. ZTE: rights-holder notice with claim charts; implementer willingness; written FRAND offer with comparables or method; counter-offer; ADR or court. Flags the dispute over whether to express willingness before arguing essentiality and validity (reserving rights).
- **Status:** Guidance; later revisions: not verified.
- **Skill rule:** When the user receives an SEP notice, include a response timeline that records willingness and specific counter-terms; do not advise "ignore until sued."

### Unwired Planet International Ltd v. Huawei Technologies Co. Ltd, [2017] EWHC 711 (Pat) (Birss J, Apr. 5, 2017; reissued with revised redactions as [2017] EWHC 2988 (Pat)), aff'd [2018] EWCA Civ 2344, aff'd [2020] UKSC 37 · CB p. 1181
- **Facts:** NPE with SEPs bought from Ericsson sued Huawei in the UK; patentee wanted a global portfolio licence, implementer only a UK licence.
- **Holding / rule (Birss J):** One set of FRAND terms for given circumstances (no "range"); FRAND is enforceable in England as a contract-based commitment under French law; global portfolio licence is FRAND for such a portfolio and the patentee may insist on it; benchmark rate comes from the value of the patentee's portfolio and is the same for large and small licensees; patent counting (with attention to over-declaration) is inevitable; an implementer that refuses to take the court-determined licence faces an injunction. Rates for the 4G handset benchmark: 0.062 percent.
- **Nuance:** Implementers also owe fair negotiation. None of either side's offers was FRAND. The Court of Appeal disagreed with the "only one set of FRAND terms" point (more than one set may be FRAND; the SEP owner may choose), without changing the result. The Supreme Court (Aug. 26, 2020) held English courts can fix terms of a global FRAND licence, treated the FRAND obligation as one composite obligation, kept non-discrimination general rather than hard-edged, and held that a failure to follow Huawei's steps is not itself an Article 102 breach.
- **Status:** UK Supreme Court authority. Later: Optis v. Apple [2025] EWCA Civ 552 (May 1, 2025) replaced the trial judge's $56.43m with a lump sum of $502m for 2013–2027, before interest (total with interest: not verified), and found that Apple's insistence on patent-by-patent licensing "manifestly would involve a degree of hold out"; Panasonic v. Xiaomi [2024] EWCA Civ 1143 (Oct. 3, 2024) and Lenovo v. Ericsson [2025] EWCA Civ 182 (Feb. 28, 2025) declared that a willing licensor would grant an interim licence pending the final determination; Lenovo set the interim payment at the mid-point between the two sides' interim-licence offers. InterDigital v. Lenovo [2024] EWCA Civ 743 (July 12, 2024) raised the trial rate from $0.175 to $0.225 per unit (about $178m before interest), holding the comparable-derived figure too low because it reflected a degree of hold-out, and upheld royalties for all past sales with interest. US: Ericsson v. Lenovo, 120 F.4th 864 (Fed. Cir. 2024) vacated the denial of an anti-suit injunction under the Ninth Circuit's Microsoft framework, holding the "dispositive" threshold met because an ETSI FRAND commitment requires the SEP holder to comply with its good-faith negotiation obligation before pursuing SEP-based injunctions; remanded for the rest of the analysis.
- **Skill rule:** Multi-country exposure: do not assume the home forum sets the rate. Note the UK global-rate route, US anti-suit practice, and German injunction speed. Never state a rate or "licence is FRAND."

## Edge-case playbook

1. **"The patent is in the Orange Book, so it covers the drug."** Trap: listing is by the NDA holder, with no FDA check (aaiPharma). Analysis: a listed patent triggers a certification and, if suit follows, a stay, even if the claims are device-only; delist only by counterclaim. Teva v. Amneal (2024) limits listability to patents claiming at least the active ingredient. Cases: aaiPharma, Mylan, Teva v. Amneal.
2. **Suing to delist.** Trap: a generic brings an independent suit. Correct: no private FFDCA cause of action; use the counterclaim after being sued (21 U.S.C. 355(j)(5)(C)(ii)). Cases: Mylan, Andrx.
3. **Comparing the claim to the market product.** Trap: infringement under 271(e)(2) is judged against the product described in the ANDA, not samples. A later change in formulation changes the answer. Source: 271(e)(2) (Eli Lilly v. Medtronic describes the infringing act as the submission itself, before any commercial manufacture or sale; it does not itself hold that the ANDA product controls, and the Federal Circuit cases on that point are not digested here).
4. **Only suing on one listed patent.** Trap: believing unasserted listed patents are safe. Correct: the generic may seek a DJ on the others and the brand may still sue later; the six-year damages limit applies. Cases: Teva v. Novartis, MedImmune.
5. **Second 30-month stays.** Trap: assuming each late-listed patent adds a stay. Correct: the 2003 amendments allow one stay per ANDA for patents listed before filing; late listing does not add one. Source: 2003 amendments (casebook note).
6. **Post-approval testing as "outside" the safe harbor.** Trap: relying on Classen. Correct: Momenta I held some FDA-required post-approval testing protected, but Momenta II (2015) held routine record-retention testing tied to commercial production is not; Edwards v. Meril (2024) says the safe harbor covers acts reasonably related to FDA submissions even if there is also commercial purpose. Cases: Classen, Momenta I and II, Edwards.
7. **Stockpiling and commercial-scale batches.** Trap: assuming manufacture is protected or not. Correct: fact-specific; in Amgen v. Hospira, 944 F.3d 1327 (Fed. Cir. 2019) the jury found only 7 of 21 batches protected, and batches made for commercial inventory were not; Edwards v. Meril (2024) covered imported samples. Do not generalize beyond the facts. Cases: Hospira, Edwards.
8. **Skinny label equals safe.** Trap: carve-out label alone clears inducement. Correct: Hikma (2026) lowers risk: calling a product a generic equivalent, an AB rating, statute-required label text and omissions are not enough by themselves. But clear, affirmative encouragement of the patented use (express or implicit) in labels, ads or sales messages can still induce; check every outward channel. Cases: GSK, Hikma.
9. **Skinny label equals unsafe.** Trap: relying on GSK to advise against any carve-out. Correct: after Hikma, the "could be read as instructions" approach that GSK exemplified is rejected (Hikma n.3), though GSK was not expressly overruled. Cases: GSK, Hikma.
10. **Biosimilar patent search from the Purple Book.** Trap: no patents listed means no patents. Correct: no public list; patents surface only through the confidential dance or litigation. Case: Sandoz v. Amgen.
11. **Biosimilar skipping the dance.** Trap: assuming a penalty. Correct: federal law gives only an immediate DJ for the sponsor; state-law penalties preempted (Amgen v. Sandoz, 2017). But skipping hands the sponsor timing and the whole patent set. Cases: Sandoz, Amgen remand.
12. **Notice before approval.** Trap: waiting for licensure to send the 180-day notice. Correct: notice may go before licensure. Case: Sandoz.
13. **Licensee DJ.** Trap: believing a licensee must breach to sue. Correct: no. Clause-based limits (no-challenge, termination) are contract risks, not Article III bars. Case: MedImmune.
14. **Sending a claim chart to a competitor.** Trap: a polite licence offer. Correct: it can create DJ jurisdiction in the recipient's forum. Cases: MedImmune, SanDisk.
15. **A patent is "in the standard", so it is essential.** Trap: treating a declaration as proof. Correct: declarations over-count; essentiality needs a claim chart against the exact standard version. Unwired Planet noted over-declaration. Cases: Unwired Planet, Microsoft.
16. **FRAND means a low rate.** Trap: 0.555 cents or similar. Correct: rates are fact and forum specific; the UK and US methods differ (top-down versus bottom-up, apportionment instructions, benchmark portfolios); no tool output is "the FRAND rate." Cases: Microsoft, Ericsson, Unwired Planet.
17. **Apportioning to the standard instead of the feature.** Trap: pricing the whole standard. Correct: royalty must reflect the claimed feature's incremental value, apart from standardization. Case: Ericsson v. D-Link.
18. **Injunction against an SEP implementer.** Trap: assuming SEPs never get injunctions. Correct: Germany and the UPC grant them if Huawei steps are followed; UK may grant injunction if the implementer refuses the court-set licence; US courts weigh eBay and good-faith negotiation (Ericsson v. Lenovo). Cases: Huawei, Unwired Planet.
19. **Hold-up only.** Trap: ignoring hold-out. Correct: courts credit both; Optis (2025) treated patent-by-patent refusal as hold-out. Cases: Unwired Planet, Optis.
20. **A standards body disclosure duty.** Trap: filing later claims that read on a draft standard without disclosure. Correct: may support implied waiver; check the SDO policy first. Cases: Core Wireless, Hynix.
21. **Global rate by a national court.** Trap: parallel suits and anti-suit orders. Correct: UK global licence jurisdiction exists; Microsoft anti-suit injunction was upheld where the U.S. action was dispositive of the foreign injunction claim; timing and good faith matter. Cases: Unwired Planet, Microsoft (9th Cir. 2012), Ericsson v. Lenovo.
22. **Patent term extension.** Trap: stacking term across patents. Correct: one patent per product, 5-year cap and 14-year post-approval cap; scope limited to the approved product. Source: 35 U.S.C. 156.

## Post-casebook developments (verified unless tagged)

- **Hikma v. Amarin, 608 U.S. 580 (2026) (No. 24-889, June 4, 2026)** (cert granted Jan. 16, 2026): unanimous reversal; a skinny-label inducement claim must plead affirmative, culpable encouragement, not statements doctors could read as instructions. Skill rule: see Part F. Pinpoints: 584, 590 & n.3, 592–595.
- **Teva Branded Pharmaceutical Products R&D v. Amneal, No. 2024-1936 (Fed. Cir. Dec. 20, 2024):** device patents not claiming the active ingredient are not listable; delisting order affirmed. Skill rule: FTO screens list only claims reciting the drug.
- **Edwards Lifesciences v. Meril Life Sciences, 96 F.4th 1347 (Fed. Cir. Mar. 25, 2024) (2-1; Stoll, J., for the majority; Lourie, J., dissenting):** "solely" modifies "for uses"; acts reasonably related to FDA submissions are covered, even if there is also another purpose; en banc denied Aug. 21, 2024; cert denied Jan. 13, 2025 (No. 24-428). Skill rule: broaden safe-harbor analysis; still test each act.
- **Amgen v. Hospira, 944 F.3d 1327 (Fed. Cir. Dec. 16, 2019):** affirmed jury instructions that a defendant's additional purposes do not matter if each act of manufacture was reasonably related to FDA submission; the question is whether each batch was made for such uses. The jury found 7 of 21 drug-substance batches protected; batches made for commercial inventory were not, and submitting information about a lot to FDA does not by itself bring its manufacture in (n.3, citing Momenta II).
- **Panasonic v. Xiaomi [2024] EWCA Civ 1143; Lenovo v. Ericsson [2025] EWCA Civ 182:** interim-licence declarations; **Optis v. Apple [2025] EWCA Civ 552:** $502m lump sum; **Ericsson v. Lenovo, 120 F.4th 864 (Fed. Cir. 2024):** anti-suit injunction framework and good-faith negotiation duty. Skill rule: in SEP FTO, flag that interim-licence declarations (UK) and anti-suit injunctions (US) are live tools and the risk shifts by forum.
- **EU SEP Regulation proposal withdrawn (announced Feb. 2025; formal withdrawal recorded Oct. 6, 2025 on EUR-Lex):** no EU regulatory overlay now; Huawei v. ZTE remains. UPC issued first FRAND judgment in Nov. 2024 (details not verified).

## Checklist for the skill

- Pharma or biotech FTO: identify the pathway first (NDA, ANDA, 505(b)(2), BLA, 351(k)); list Orange Book patents and their use codes; separate claims on the active ingredient, formulation, method of use, process.
- Compare each claim to the product as described in the application, not a commercial sample.
- Record exclusivities (NCE, clinical, orphan, pediatric, 180-day, biologic 12-year) and PTE; compute the date range but state it as a screen.
- For a section viii plan, require the user to review label, website, ads, sales aids, and press releases for the patented use (Hikma). Flag unresolved items.
- For biologics, state that the Purple Book has no patent list; search sponsor patents by product, process, formulation and use; mention the dance.
- Treat safe-harbor claims fact by fact; never extend to commercial sales; apply Classen, Momenta I and II, Hospira and Edwards act by act.
- Avoid the unsolicited licence-offer or charting step before the user decides on DJ risk (MedImmune).
- Pharma drafting: file separate method-of-use claims per indication so a carve-out maps to a claim; claim the active ingredient in anything intended for listing; do not rely only on analytical-method claims against generics.
- SEP drafting: for claims aimed at a standard, chart against the specific standard version and test whether each limitation is mandatory or optional in the standard; note disclosure duties to the SDO.
- SEP searches: query ETSI, IEEE, ITU declaration databases; treat declarations as leads only; require a claim chart before calling a patent essential; note FRAND commitments travel with the patent.
- SEP exposure: describe forum differences (Germany/UPC injunction, UK global rate and interim licence, US apportionment and anti-suit); never output "FRAND rate" as a conclusion.
