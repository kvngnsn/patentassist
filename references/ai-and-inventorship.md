# AI and inventorship

## 1. Only natural persons can be inventors

- *Thaler v. Vidal*, 43 F.4th 1207 (Fed. Cir. 2022): the Patent Act requires inventors to be natural persons; an AI system (DABUS) cannot be named. Cert. denied Apr. 24, 2023 (No. 22-919).
- The Nov. 2025 USPTO guidance: "Artificial intelligence systems, regardless of their sophistication, cannot be named as inventors or joint inventors on a patent application as they are not natural persons."
- Examiners will reject under 35 U.S.C. 101 and 115 all claims in an application that names an AI system or other non-natural person as inventor or joint inventor.

## 2. Current USPTO guidance: Nov. 2025 revised inventorship guidance

"Revised Inventorship Guidance for AI-Assisted Inventions," 90 FR 54636 (published Nov. 28, 2025; filed Nov. 26, 2025). Signed by Director John A. Squires.

What it did:
- Rescinded "in its entirety" the Feb. 13, 2024 "Inventorship Guidance for AI-Assisted Inventions" (89 FR 10043) and its examples.
- Withdrew the 2024 approach of applying the *Pannu* factors to a single human's use of AI.

Rules it states:
- "The same legal standard for determining inventorship applies to all inventions, regardless of whether AI systems were used in the inventive process. There is no separate or modified standard for AI-assisted inventions."
- Conception is "the touchstone of inventorship": "the formation in the mind of the inventor, of a definite and permanent idea of the complete and operative invention, as it is hereafter to be applied in practice" (*Burroughs Wellcome Co. v. Barr Labs.*, 40 F.3d 1223, 1228 (Fed. Cir. 1994)). Conception is complete when the inventor has "a specific, settled idea, a particular solution to the problem at hand, not just a general goal or research plan."
- "AI systems, including generative AI and other computational models, are instruments used by human inventors. They are analogous to laboratory equipment, computer software, research databases, or any other tool."
- Inventors may "use the services, ideas, and aid of others" without those sources becoming co-inventors; the same applies to AI.
- One human using AI: ask only whether that person conceived the invention. *Pannu* does not apply (no joint-inventorship question, because AI is not a person).
- Several humans using AI: apply ordinary joint-inventorship law, including *Pannu*. "The fact that AI tools were used ... does not change the joint inventorship analysis among the human contributors."
- The USPTO generally presumes the inventors named in the ADS or oath/declaration are correct (MPEP 2157, 602.01).
- Applies to utility, design, and plant applications and patents.
- Priority/benefit claims: the U.S. application must share at least one natural-person inventor with the prior application. A priority claim to a foreign application naming an AI as sole inventor "will not be accepted." If the foreign application names humans and an AI, list only the humans in the U.S. ADS. Same for PCT national stage entry (37 CFR 1.76; MPEP 1893.01(e)).

What it does not say:
- It does not set a duty to disclose AI use. (The April 2024 practice guidance below still governs disclosure of material information.)
- It binds examiners, not courts. Courts apply case law on conception and joint inventorship.

## 3. Inventorship basics

- Inventor = the individual or individuals who invented or discovered the claimed subject matter (35 U.S.C. 100(f)-(g)). Inventorship is decided claim by claim.
- Conception, not reduction to practice, decides inventorship. A person who only builds or tests what someone else conceived is not an inventor.
- Joint inventors (35 U.S.C. 116(a)): may apply jointly even if they did not work together or at the same time, did not make the same type or amount of contribution, or did not contribute to every claim.
- *Pannu v. Iolab Corp.*, 155 F.3d 1344, 1351 (Fed. Cir. 1998). Each joint inventor must: "(1) contribute in some significant manner to the conception or reduction to practice of the invention, (2) make a contribution to the claimed invention that is not insignificant in quality, when that contribution is measured against the dimension of the full invention, and (3) do more than merely explain to the real inventors well-known concepts and/or the current state of the art." (quoted in 90 FR 54636)
- Correcting inventorship: 35 U.S.C. 256 (patents); 37 CFR 1.48 (applications). Wrong inventorship can make a patent invalid if not corrected.
- Inventors sign an oath or declaration (35 U.S.C. 115; 37 CFR 1.63) or a substitute statement (1.64).

### Intake questions the skill should ask

- Who formed the specific, settled idea for each claim element? When? Is there a record (notes, emails, commits)?
- What did each AI tool produce (prompts and outputs)? Did a human select, modify, or recognize the solution as complete and operative?
- Did AI propose embodiments that no human conceived? If so, do not claim them unless a human conceived them, and consider whether to describe them at all (see section 5).
- Were several humans involved? Run *Pannu* for each.

## 4. Using AI tools in practice before the USPTO (April 2024 guidance)

"Guidance on Use of Artificial Intelligence-Based Tools in Practice Before the United States Patent and Trademark Office," 89 FR 25609 (Apr. 11, 2024). Says existing rules cover AI use. No later Federal Register notice replaced it (searched USPTO FR documents 2024-Nov. 2026). Its references to the Feb. 2024 inventorship guidance ("significant contribution," *Pannu* for each claim) are now outdated by the Nov. 2025 guidance; the rest stands.

Duties it restates:
- Signature (37 CFR 1.4(d), 11.18(a)): documents must be signed by a natural person. An AI tool cannot sign. The signer must personally insert the signature; having an AI tool apply it is non-compliant (1.4(d)(2)(i)).
- Certification on presenting a paper (11.18(b)): the presenter must have read and verified it after a reasonable inquiry. Check AI output for errors, made-up citations, and legally unwarranted arguments. Violations can bring sanctions (11.18(c)).
- No general duty to tell the USPTO that AI helped draft a paper, unless the USPTO asks (e.g., 37 CFR 1.105).
- Duty of candor (37 CFR 1.56): disclose AI use if it is material to patentability, e.g., evidence that a named inventor did not contribute to a claim because an AI did. (Apply this now under the Nov. 2025 conception standard.)
- Do not file or prosecute claims known to be unpatentable; fix AI-drafted claims as needed.
- IDS: the signer must review every cited document; do not file AI-generated IDS lists unreviewed. Check that cited documents exist.
- Competence and client consultation (11.101, 11.104(a)(2)).
- USPTO.gov accounts are for natural persons only. "AI systems may not obtain a USPTO.gov account." Practitioners may not sponsor an AI tool as support staff. An AI tool is not a "user" of Patent Center.
- Do not let tools exceed authorized access. Heavy automated scraping of USPTO sites violates the Terms of Use; use bulk data products.
- Confidentiality (11.106): entering invention details into an AI system may disclose client information; the tool may retain or train on it. Read the tool's terms, privacy, and security practices. Supervisors must ensure staff comply (11.501-11.503).
- Export control and foreign filing license: AI tools may use servers outside the U.S.; entering technical data may be an export. Use by non-U.S. persons of U.S.-hosted systems can also be an export (37 CFR 5.11; 15 CFR 734.13).

Enforcement:
- *In re Brian E. Mitchell*, OED Proceeding No. D2026-16 (order posted Aug. 27, 2026). Settlement; public reprimand. A registered patent attorney used generative AI to help draft a claim construction document in a federal district court case (*Magpul Industries v. Mission First Tactical*, E.D. Pa.) and did not verify citations. Violations: 37 CFR 11.101 (competence), 11.103 (diligence), 11.804(c) (misrepresentation), 11.804(d) (prejudicial to the administration of justice). First USPTO discipline order expressly predicated on generative-AI use (per secondary source).
- An earlier 2025 reprimand (Matos, D2025-13) is described by commentators as AI-related, but the order reportedly does not discuss AI (unconfirmed).

## 5. AI-generated text and drawings in applications

From the April 2024 guidance, with the current inventorship law applied:
- Review AI-drafted specifications and drawings for technical accuracy before filing. Fixing them after filing can be new matter (35 U.S.C. 132(a); MPEP 2163.05).
- Prophetic examples must not be written in the past tense (MPEP 2164.02). Mark them clearly.
- Check that foreign priority documents drafted with AI are accurate; errors can lose the priority date.
- If AI adds embodiments or claim scope the inventors did not conceive, re-check inventorship and claim support before filing. Claims must be conceived by a named natural person.
- The USPTO has not issued separate rules on AI-generated drawings. Drawings must still meet 37 CFR 1.84 and show every claimed feature (1.83(a)) (general drawing rules; not AI-specific).
- AI-generated public material can be prior art if publicly accessible. The USPTO asked for comments on AI's effect on prior art and the person of ordinary skill (89 FR 34217, Apr. 30, 2024) but has issued no final guidance on that topic as of this check.

## 6. Rules for this skill

- Never list an AI system (including this skill) as an inventor.
- Record what the inventor conceived versus what the tool suggested. Keep prompts and outputs if inventorship may be questioned.
- Flag every AI-drafted citation, claim, and figure for human verification. Never fabricate a reference.
- Warn users before they paste confidential or export-controlled technical data into any third-party AI service.
- A human must sign and file. The tool cannot hold a USPTO.gov account or file follow-on papers.
